                                                   Abstract
           English interjections bring together grammatical, semantic, and interactional properties
       whose distributions don’t coincide. I propose three overlapping, inquiry-relative classifica-
       tions: a grammar-facing lexical category, a semantics-facing category of expressives, and an
       interaction-facing category of conventional minimal resources. Their common bearers are
       conventionalized sign-types, identified independently of the particular target a prediction is
       meant to test. The classifications preserve different contrasts; their further inferential value
       requires evaluation rather than following from their distinct extensions.
           The historical argument concerns path dependence: recruitment can leave source-specific
       residues after forms converge on similar uses. The documented history of gee and the partly
       reconstructive comparison with complement-taking damn motivate this account. Candidate
       relations among prosody, syntax, meaning, and interaction organize further explanatory hy-
       potheses, without establishing a complete causal network or excluding constructional alterna-
       tives. GloWbE and COHA probes describe country-level frequency variation and inventory
       turnover. Audits of two preregistered analyses reveal implementation failures that prevent their
       outputs from testing semantic persistence in derivatives or ordered erosion in fie. Those results
       are withdrawn as substantive evidence and documented in the appendices. The resulting frame-
       work distinguishes descriptive classification, predictive success, representational advantage, and
       causal explanation. Explicit forward declarations specify what remains to be tested, including
       the possible usefulness of joint category membership.

Keywords: interjections; field-relative categories; projectibility; causal networks; supplements; path

1 Introduction
  English interjections are small forms with large consequences. A speaker’s oh, wow, hey, damn, or
  mm-hm can package a response, display stance, open or sustain a sequence, initiate repair, or invite
  recipient uptake. Those jobs don’t line up neatly with a single grammatical distribution. Standalone
  damn! is prosodically separate, expressive, and available as a complete stance display. Prenominal
  damn in the damn car preserves expressive meaning while participating in the syntax and prosody
  of the noun phrase. Complement-taking damn these mosquitoes! supplies a third combination. A
  single verdict on the word family would conceal the differences that need explaining.
       This paper proposes three overlapping classifications: a grammar-facing interjectionsyn , a
  semantics-facing interjectionsem , and an interaction-facing interjectionprag . They draw on es-
  tablished grammatical, semantic, and interactional analyses, but aren’t three settled technical senses
  that the literature already distinguishes consistently. The subscripts make an analytical proposal ex-
  plicit. Each classification preserves particular contrasts and supports a different family of inferential
  hypotheses; none acquires general priority merely because its principal consumer is grammar, seman-
  tics, or interactional pragmatics. Their relative usefulness has to be assessed for specified questions.
       The proposal has a historical setting. In 1586, William Bullokar wrote the earliest grammar of
  English. It included interjections, which he defined as words that betoken “a sudden passion of the
  mind” (Bullokar, 1586/1906, p. 373). Four centuries later the standing is less secure. Jespersen (1924,
  p. 90) treated interjection as a manner in which words of other categories may be used, which is to
  deny it categoryhood. Quirk et al. (1985, p. 67) kept the category but called it “marginal and anoma-
  lous”.
       Huddleston and Pullum (2002, p. 22) listed interjection as one of nine lexical categories in The
  Cambridge Grammar of the English Language (CGEL): ah, damn, gosh, hey, oh, ooh, ouch, whoa,
  and wow. The companion textbook set interjections aside from its eight-category inventory (Huddle-
  ston & Pullum, 2005, p. 16). Its second edition returns interjection to the inventory, but concedes
  that “grammarians typically say very little about interjections, and we don’t plan to be an exception”
  (Huddleston et al., 2022, p. 24). Readmitted to the list, and still largely undescribed.
       The Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English redraws the class instead. It makes in-
  serts a class of “stand-alone words” marked by their “inability to enter into syntactic relations with
  other structures”, whose boundary with syntactic non-clausal units is “a gradual one” (Biber et al.,
  1999, p. 1082). Interjection is one of several functional types of insert, and damn, gosh, and gee be-
  long to another, the expletives (pp. 1083, 1094–1095). Ljung (2011, pp. 81, 83), like Jespersen, denies
  the category: interjections “should not be regarded as a word class”, and the term is better kept for
  exclamative utterances.
       Libert (2019) objects that calling interjections neglected “is an overstatement”: work on their pho-
  netics, phonology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics goes back a long way. What’s stayed unsettled
  is standing rather than volume of attention: whether grammar has a lexical category of interjections,
  whether the same classification serves semantics and interaction, and what any of those memberships
  would license. Cram (2008, p. 63) makes the historiographic point more sharply. Surveying the tra-
  dition from Greek antiquity onward, he finds “no firmer consensus about this category than at any
  earlier period”, and notes that the interjection can “function as a touchstone or test case for vari-
  ous boundary controversies”, naming the syntax–pragmatics and semantics–pragmatics boundaries.
  Cram asks what interjections show about our theories. I ask which distinctions the classifications

preserve, what holds their profiles together, and what makes them diverge.
     The empirical picture hasn’t simplified. Evidence bearing on the traditional domain is spread
across phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics. No familiar diagnostic by itself
captures its full extension, and the boundaries with nouns, verbs, adverbs, fillers, and routine for-
mulae are disputed. Cross-linguistically, “interjection” is at best a comparative concept (Haspelmath,
2010). But it doesn’t follow that only a morphosyntactic classification can be a category. A seman-
tic or interactional classification can earn standing through the reliable distinctions and inferences it
supports. Conversely, giving a classification a field-specific name doesn’t establish that it earns that
standing.
     The common bearer proposed here is a conventionalized linguistic sign-type: an identifiable
expression in a conventional use, to which a profile of grammatical realization, meaning, and inter-
actional affordance can be attributed. Verbal damn in I damn you, standalone damn!, complement-
taking damn these mosquitoes!, and prenominal damn in the damn car are distinguishable uses within
one historical family. Their individuation needs evidence of conventional contrasts; it can’t simply
reproduce every diagnostic difference the analysis is meant to explain. Section 3 specifies the identity
discipline needed when a prediction is tested in a new environment.
     A sign-type’s profile is the particular combination of properties attributed to it: whether it’s
prosodically set off, whether it inflects, whether it refers, and what stance or response it carries. The
word is doing bookkeeping and has nothing to do with the figure-and-base sense familiar from cogni-
tive grammar. I use property cluster descriptively for recurrent co-occurrence. Neither a cluster’s
recurrence nor its usefulness as a classification establishes a causal order among its properties.
     The inferential standard is projectibility: observing some properties of a new case warrants
expectations about others (Goodman, 1955). To say that a property or category licenses an inference
is to propose a defeasible default, not a necessary consequence. Three questions then arise separately.
Does the proposed inference succeed? Does a category- or network-structured representation im-
prove generalization over an appropriate alternative? And what processes explain the association?
An answer to one needn’t answer the others. This paper specifies forward projective hypotheses; it
doesn’t estimate their held-out performance.
     The strongest present evidence concerns path dependence: recruitment histories remain visi-
ble in some present-day combinations of properties. Gee is documented as developing through a re-
ligious invocation and takes no complement. Complement-taking damn is compatible with a verbal
optative source and retains NP complementation. The second history remains partly reconstructive.
These cases motivate an account in which convergence doesn’t erase every source-specific difference.
They don’t uniquely select a causal-network theory over developed accounts of constructional inher-
itance and change.
     Two preregistered corpus analyses were intended to test semantic persistence in verbal deriva-
tives and ordered erosion during the obsolescence of fie. An audit of the archived coding and anal-
ysis found that neither implements an independent test of its target: the derivative coding assigns
the confirmatory outcomes, while the obsolescence analysis retains ineligible records and prediction-
labelled assignments whose independent validation isn’t documented. Their archived numerical out-
puts therefore can’t establish semantic persistence or an ordering of property loss. Appendices B
and C document those failures; no corrected estimate is substituted. The remaining corpus probes
concern frequency variation and inventory turnover, which bear on narrower descriptive questions.

      The network proposal organizes those explanations and the further links still needing evidence.
  It draws on Khalidi (2013, 2018), for whom kinds occupy positions in causal structures rather than
  being defined by essences. Here the proposal concerns local relations among prosodic isolation, syn-
  tactic independence, meaning, morphology, and interactional use. Some are historically supported
  pathways; others are candidate dependencies or stabilizers. The classifications and the causal claims
  must remain separable: the field-relative distinctions could prove useful even if a particular coupling
  hypothesis fails, and historical persistence could obtain without vindicating all three classifications.
      Bare “interjection” remains a cover term for this traditional domain and its canonical cases. Joint
  membership in the three proposed classifications records their overlap. I don’t introduce the inter-
  section as a fourth category here, but neither its mathematical derivability nor the present evidence
  rules out its usefulness as a further grouping. Section 5 gives it the same test as other representations:
  whether it supports useful generalization from restricted evidence for a specified task.
      Section 2 documents the profiles and contrasts. Section 3 develops the classifications, the common
  bearer, and the distinction between predictive and causal claims. Section 4 grades the recruitment and
  coupling evidence, with source-specific residue as its strongest present payoff. Section 5 states forward
  declarations and the comparisons needed to evaluate them. The discussion distinguishes what the
  historical and corpus probes establish from what remains a research hypothesis.

2 The property cluster
  The traditional interjection domain brings together properties from phonology, morphology, syntax,
  semantics, and pragmatics. This section draws primarily on Ameka (1992), Quirk et al. (1985), and
  Huddleston and Pullum (2002) to identify recurrent combinations and the contrasts that the three
  proposed classifications preserve. The descriptive claim concerns co-occurrence in selected conven-
  tional uses, rather than an invariant profile shared by everything called an interjection.
       Six properties recur: prosodic isolation, non-inflection, external syntactic non-integration, ab-
  sence of ordinary entity or event denotation, expressive or stance contribution, and conventional
  sequential use. The last includes minimal contributions to a sequence, such as a continuer, without
  requiring the speaker to take over the floor. These properties aren’t definitions of the three categories
  or six interchangeable measurements of one underlying variable. Each classification preserves partic-
  ular contrasts among them and proposes targets of its own.
       The subscripts introduced above are used throughout. Section 2.6 gives operational assignment
  criteria and a diagnostic matrix; Section 3.4 explains the relation to existing classifications. The matrix
  records the present analysis rather than a new annotation study. Section 5 asks what partial observa-
  tions could license, with targets withheld. The separate question of how the combinations arose, and
  which relations might help sustain them, is taken up in Section 4.
  2.1 Phonology
  Canonical grammatical interjections are prosodically isolated, and Ameka (1992, p. 108) states it with-
  out hedging: they’re “always separated by a pause from the other utterances with which they may
  co-occur” and “always constitute an intonation unit by themselves”. I treat the property as strongly
  characteristic rather than exceptionless, because the diagnostic matrix below records partial cases (um).
  That gap between a categorical statement and its boundary cases is the pattern Section 2.6 takes up.
  In writing, this isolation surfaces as the punctuation that sets supplements off from the rest of the
  sentence (commas, dashes, parentheses; Huddleston and Pullum, 2002, p. 1350).
2 The property cluster                                                                                  5


     The corpus evidence below is written, so prosodic isolation is approached through punctuation,
standalone position, and other indications of detachment. These are indirect indicators rather than
acoustic measurements. On Jackendoff (2025, p. 41)’s account a parenthetical is typically realized as
an intonation phrase, ending in a boundary marked by “some combination of pause, lengthening,
pitch discontinuity”. Punctuation can represent such boundaries, which motivates its use as a proxy.
     The correspondence is imperfect: punctuation varies with genre, editorial practice, and period.
Nor can a punctuation mark serve as independent evidence for both a prosodic boundary and a syn-
tactic dependency and then establish a causal link between them. Written records can support a pack-
aging hypothesis, but the proposed prosodic–syntactic relation requires separately assessed evidence
for its two sides.
     Interjections may also use sounds outside a variety’s ordinary phoneme inventory. The interjec-
tion uh-oh ([əʔo]) includes a glottal stop as part of its conventional form, including in varieties where
that sound has no independent phonemic status. Other atypical sounds include the dental-alveolar
clicks in tut-tut ([ǀǀ]; Wilkins, 1992, p. 147), the voiceless bilabial fricative in whew ([ɸɪu]), and the
voiceless velar fricative in ugh ([ʌx] or [əx]), though /x/ isn’t a phoneme of standard American or
British English (Quirk et al., 1985, pp. 74, 853). Spelling pronunciations arise for these forms (tut-tut
said [tʌt tʌt], ugh said [ʌɡ]; Quirk et al., 1985, p. 853), suggesting that the atypical sounds are marked
even for native speakers.
     Prosodic isolation is a strong cue: if a novel form occupies its own intonation unit and resists
integration into the surrounding prosodic contour, the network account proposes syntactic non-
integration and non-referentiality as targets. Their held-out relation to that cue remains to be tested.
Prosodic isolation isn’t unique to interjections, since supplements in general are “detached prosod-
ically” from what they attach to (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 66). For canonical grammatical
interjections, the diagnostic is the combination of prosodic isolation with the properties below.
2.2 Morphology
Canonical grammatical interjections are invariant in their interjectional uses. The familiar contrasts
are with plurals (*ouches), tense forms (*ouched), comparatives (*oucher), and nominalizations (*ouch-
ness) (Ameka, 1992, p. 106). These judgements concern the intended interjectional reading; they aren’t
claims that the corresponding strings can never occur. A claim about resistance to derivation is also
distinct from a claim about the absence of an inflectional paradigm.
     Noun and verb uses of ouch are attested in British child-directed speech (It’s a big ouch; I think
this one’s just ouched slightly more than you; Stange, 2016, p. 36). Wilkins (1992, p. 147) reports that
tut-tut and tsk-tsk became verbs after acquiring ordinary word pronunciations. Such uses can mo-
tivate distinct conventional sign-types. Their status must be supported by evidence for a different
construction or interpretation; an unexpected affix alone can’t be used to exclude a case from a test
whose target is affixation.
     Since Libert (2019) notes that “few, if any, papers focus on the morphology of interjections”, the
broad morphological generalization needs care. Non-occurrence is informative only on occasions
where a rival analysis predicts an inflected form, and this paper doesn’t count those occasions. Deriva-
tional productivity needs its own comparison. Appendix B documents a preregistered attempt to as-
sess semantic persistence in selected derivatives, together with a coding problem that prevents treating
its reported success as independent confirmation. The existence of derivative uses remains relevant;
the archived numerical result doesn’t establish their frequency or their semantic profile.

    Some interjectional expressions preserve material associated with an earlier category. Heavens and
bollocks contain the shape of the nominal plural suffix. In their conventional exclamatory uses this
doesn’t supply an ordinary productive number contrast: heavens! doesn’t normally alternate with
singular heaven! in the way clouds alternates with cloud. The retained shape and the availability of a
productive morphological operation are separate facts. Source material can persist without the source
paradigm remaining active.
    Non-inflection is associated with restrictions on ordinary argumenthood in the recurrent profile.
It doesn’t entail them: adverbs also don’t inflect (Ameka, 1992, p. 106), and invariance alone doesn’t
prevent an expression from filling an argument slot. The stronger diagnostic is syntactic: adverbs
characteristically form constituents with other words, while canonical interjections are syntactically
isolated and resist integration with neighbouring material (Meinard, 2015, pp. 152–153, 164).
2.3 Syntax
Interjections are used on their own or as supplements (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 1361), “ele-
ments which occupy a position in linear sequence without being integrated into the syntactic struc-
ture of the sentence” (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 1350). In Damn, we’re going to be late!, the
interjection isn’t a complement or modifier of anything in the clause. It’s appended to the beginning
as a parenthetical.
     Supplement function isn’t unique to interjections. Relative clauses, noun phrases, adjective
phrases, and adverb phrases can all function as supplements (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, pp. 1356–
1360):

 (1)   a. Ah, so you were there after all!                                       (interjection, p. 1360)
       b. We called in to see Sue’s parents, which made us rather late.        (relative clause, p. 1356)
       c. A university lecturer, Dr Brown, was arrested for the crime.          (noun phrase, p. 1357)

What characteristically distinguishes interjection supplements is their combination with the other
cluster properties: non-referentiality, non-inflection, prosodic isolation, and emotive force. The func-
tion is shared; the category differs.
     External non-integration must be distinguished from internal syntactic structure. A constituent
can stand outside its host’s clause structure while containing a head and dependents of its own. For
damn in Damn these mosquitoes!, Huddleston and Pullum (2002, 1361 n. 70) suggest that “it may be
best to regard such words as exceptional interjections that combine with an NP complement to form
an interjection phrase” (IntjP). The tentative classification matters: an NP complement doesn’t by
itself turn the whole expression into an integrated part of a host clause.
     No subject is overt and damn appears in its plain form, but those facts don’t distinguish the IntjP
analysis from all verbal alternatives. An optative or imperative source already permits plain-form
morphology, and the present construction needs independent comparison with those alternatives.
The complement is compatible with retained verbal syntax; it doesn’t on its own establish either a
historical sequence or present-day verbal category membership.
     A second kind of residue is internal to a lexicalized expression. Goddammit preserves the shape
of a verb–object-pronoun combination, so source structure survives as fossilized material. Cuenca
(2002, p. 40) gives Goddammit as her example of change in function and meaning “accompanied by
2 The property cluster                                                                                  7


phonetic weakness and word integration”. That history differs from productive complement selec-
tion. The internal structure of lexicalized pairs such as oh boy and holy cow is less clear and won’t be
resolved here.
     Argument-taking needn’t reflect inherited verbal syntax, though. In languages of the Caucasus,
clauses with “here, take it!” interjections often include direct objects and recipients (Maisak, 2026,
pp. 386, 397). The recipient is “not an argument expected in the syntactic frame of a ‘take’-predicate”
(p. 399), and Maisak relates it to the joint giving-and-taking event the interjection signals rather than
to a verb it came from (pp. 399–400).
     Supplement status combines an external syntactic analysis with expectations about prosodic pack-
aging. The following constructed examples illustrate the intended contrasts:

 (2)   a. * I think that damn we’re going to be late.
       b. I think that, damn, we’re going to be late.
       c. * Damn and quickly, we left.

The first two examples in (2) are intended to differ in prosodic detachment, represented by punctu-
ation; the third illustrates resistance to ordinary integrated coordination. The punctuation contrast
motivates a prosodic hypothesis rather than independently measuring it. The general version, for par-
entheticals of every kind, is Jackendoff (2025, pp. 33, 41–43)’s IP-Break Hypothesis: placement tracks
intonational-phrase breaks rather than syntactic constituency, so a host admits a parenthetical where
it independently permits an IP break, provided the parenthetical is itself licensed in that position. He
gives a pair of hosts that are “syntactically identical” but differ in where a parenthetical may go, since
“the difference in the parenthetical’s position is a consequence of the difference in position of the IP
breaks in the host” (Jackendoff, 2025, p. 42).
     Supplement function provides a useful starting point for semantic analysis, but it doesn’t set-
tle meaning type. Removing a supplement may leave the host clause’s descriptive proposition un-
changed while removing a truth-evaluable proposition contributed by the supplement itself: which
made us rather late in (1) illustrates the distinction. Independence from the host therefore doesn’t by
itself establish expressive meaning. For damn, the additional claim is that the contribution belongs
to an expressive dimension of the kind discussed by Potts (2007, p. 166). That claim needs semantic
evidence, including behaviour under embedding, perspective shifts, and repetition.
2.4 Semantics
Canonical interjectional uses characteristically lack ordinary entity or event denotation. I retain the
traditional label non-referentiality for this semantic hypothesis, while keeping it separate from
the syntactic questions whether an expression can fill an argument position, take a complement, or
modify another expression. No negative syntactic test is built into the semantic characterization. Oth-
erwise a proposed inference from meaning to distribution would risk restating its source.
     Interjections “do not refer to anything, but simply express the speaker’s emotion or wish” (Leech,
2006, p. 57): anger (damn), disgust (eww, yuck), surprise (wow), regret (alas), pain (ouch), and real-
ization (eureka) are familiar labels. These labels don’t exhaust their contributions. Yes and no affirm
or deny propositions, and oh can signal recognition of new information rather than an emotional
reaction. Absence of an ordinary referent, expressive meaning, and an interactional action are distin-
guishable claims.
2 The property cluster                                                                                    8


     Wierzbicka (1992, p. 159) argues that interjections have genuine semantic content, decomposable
via Natural Semantic Metalanguage. The forms may have content, and yes and no may operate on
propositions, while still lacking ordinary entity/event denotation.
     Wilkins (1992, p. 119) presses harder, and his title states the objection: interjections are deictics.
On his account they “convey complete propositions”, the “referential arguments in the propositions
conveyed by interjections are provided by context”, and so interjections are “shifters (indexicals) by
virtue of being built semantically out of basic deictic elements” such as I, you, now, and here. If that’s
right, calling interjections non-referential looks like a straightforward mistake.
     Wilkins’s account needn’t conflict with the narrower semantic hypothesis. His referential argu-
ments are supplied by context, and the conventional contribution of ouch! can organize that contex-
tual interpretation without functioning as an ordinary entity-denoting nominal or descriptive event
predicate. But the fact that context supplies values doesn’t by itself prove non-referentiality: familiar
indexicals also depend on context. The point is therefore compatibility between analyses at different
grains, not a deduction of one from the other.
     The grammatical observations remain to be assessed separately. On its interjectional reading,
ouch! resists the ordinary determiner and argument constructions available to a noun. Whether a
new use with such syntax represents conversion, quotation, or counterevidence requires an indepen-
dently supported interpretation of that use. Wilkins’s deictic analysis and those distributional ques-
tions can be investigated together without making either a definition of the other. His objection also
shows why “non-referential” is an imperfect label for a narrower and contested semantic property.
     Not everyone reads Wilkins that way. On the conceptualist analyses of Wierzbicka (1992), Ameka
(1992), and Wilkins (the grouping is Wharton, 2000, p. 174’s), interjections encode substantial con-
ceptual structure. Wharton (2000, p. 211) argues from relevance theory that they encode considerably
less, being “partly natural and partly coded” and falling “at various points along a continuum between
showing and saying”. That dispute doesn’t need settling here. Both approaches permit a distinction
between an interjection’s contribution and an ordinary nominal or verbal denotation, but they ex-
plain that contribution differently.
     Wharton’s continuum also identifies a dimension of variation: how much of a sign’s import is
coded rather than shown. Variation on that dimension doesn’t settle whether membership in a gram-
matical, semantic, or interactional classification is itself graded. Nor does separating the classifications
settle the continuum’s status. The questions have to be asked for the relevant use and criterion, rather
than resolved by the notation (Section 2.6).
     Cruz (2009, p. 241) carries the dispute further. He argues that the heterogeneity of interjec-
tions, “with items incorporated from other lexical categories”, together with the openness of the class,
“could indicate the existence of a continuum of more and less conceptual items”, and that the con-
ceptual ones encode “not full concepts, but some schematic material requiring subsequent pragmatic
adjustments”. What matters here isn’t the continuum, which Wharton already supplies, but its cause.
Cruz derives gradedness from recruitment out of other categories and from open-class coinage, which
are the mechanisms Section 4 treats as the cluster’s sources.
     Wierzbicka’s explications and Potts’s expressive diagnostics provide different resources for inves-
tigating conventional meaning. They shouldn’t be treated as interchangeable analyses. The pro-
posed interjectionsem classification tracks expressive, use-conditional contribution; the empirical
question is which sign-types support that assignment and which further diagnostics follow. Non-
2 The property cluster                                                                                                        9


referentiality is one potentially associated property, rather than a definition or a substitute for those
tests.
     The contrast with nouns is sharpest where both share a lexical source. Jesus as a noun refers to
a person; Jesus! as an interjection doesn’t. In the interjection use, as Gehweiler (2008, p. 88) puts it,
Jesus “has lost its propositional content, i.e. it is no longer used to refer and hence no longer influences
truth conditions”. This semantic shift is part of the recruitment pathway from noun to interjection
(Section 4).
     The proposed cross-domain inference is mostly negative: an interjectional use independently
characterized by its meaning may resist ordinary definiteness, quantification, or argument position.
The intended contrasts include *the damn!, *every ouch, *Damn surprised me, and *There’s damn, on
readings that retain the conventional interjectional contribution. Quotational and converted uses
require separate analysis, as do the independently motivated noun and verb uses of ouch. These ex-
amples motivate a construction-specific hypothesis; they don’t measure its predictive success.
     The relevant dependencies must also be separated. An expression’s capacity to denote an en-
tity bears differently on occupying a nominal argument position than on selecting a complement
or functioning as a modifier. Prenominal damn is itself an integrated expressive modifier. Section 4.5
therefore considers changes in particular source constructions rather than a universal inference from
non-referentiality to syntactic isolation.
2.5 Pragmatics
Familiar interjectional uses carry exclamatory or imperative force: ouch! expresses pain, shh! requests
silence (Quirk et al., 1985, pp. 88, 853). But the pragmatic range extends beyond exclamation. How
far beyond is worth a number. Elffers (2008, p. 18) reports that in Hofstede’s analysis of a corpus of
spoken Dutch, only 7 % of interjections “turned out to fulfil an expressive illocutionary function”,
against 52 % classed as expositive, which Hofstede’s table glosses as directive in the conversation. The
corpus is Dutch rather than English, so the figures provide a comparison rather than an estimate for
English.1 It points the same way as Dingemanse’s interactional focus (Dingemanse, 2024, p. 259). The
comparison cautions against organizing the entire domain around emotional exclamation; it doesn’t
establish an English proportion. Interjections summon (hey; the greeting hello sits at the boundary
with routine formulae, see Section 6.2), mark agreement (yes, yeah, amen), indicate understanding
(oh, uh-huh), and signal backchanneling, the listener’s ongoing attention to a speaker’s turn (Norrick,
2009, pp. 868, 873–876; Dingemanse, 2024, p. 265).
     The most common interjections tagged in the Corpus of Global Web-Based English (GloWbE;
Davies & Fuchs, 2015) illustrate this pragmatic breadth: yes, no, oh, yeah, hi, hey, wow, hello, ah, ha.
Many of these forms organize responses, greetings, or stance displays. The tagged frequencies don’t
establish their primary function, and an answer such as yes can both manage a sequence and commit
the speaker to a proposition. Poggi (2009, p. 171) presses the other way. An interjection, on her anal-
ysis, is a holophrastic signal whose equivalent isn’t a word but “a communicative act including the
meaning of both a performative and a propositional content”, so that ouch! paraphrases as “I am feel-

uses. Prenominal damn in the damn car differs: it contributes expressive meaning while occurring
inside an NP. Holophrasis and expressive meaning therefore cross-classify the domain, which is one
reason to keep the proposed grammatical and semantic classifications separate. Neither holophrasis
nor external detachment, by itself, establishes the full expressive profile.
     Dingemanse (2024, pp. 257, 263) estimates that, across 18 languages, roughly one in seven conver-
sational turns is an interjection. Interactional tools dominate the most frequent cases: continuers like
mm-hm that scaffold extended turns, repair initiators like huh? that “help people calibrate mutual
understanding on the fly”, and change-of-state tokens like oh that “display knowledge as it evolves in
interaction” (Dingemanse, 2024, p. 257; cf. Norrick, 2009, pp. 868, 870).2
     The boundary with routine formulae is instructive. Coulmas (1981, pp. 2–3) characterizes
routine formulae as “highly conventionalized prepatterned expressions whose occurrence is tied to
more or less standardized communication situations”. Ameka (1992, pp. 108–110) argues that formu-
lae such as bye, sorry, and thank you differ from interjections in three ways: they include addressees,
they’re predictable responses to social conventions, and they’re always speech acts rather than mere
reflections of the speaker’s mental state. Wilkins (1992, p. 142) counters that such formulae meet his
definition of interjection, and that being tied to specific, very common situations and serving mainly
to maintain social relations makes them “a distinct pragmatic and semantic subtype of interjections”,
not a separate category.
     The disagreement identifies contrasts that a field-relative analysis must preserve, but it needn’t
divide neatly between fields. Ameka and Wilkins can disagree about the appropriate breadth of a
pragmatic classification while recognizing the same social routines. Standalone status is relevant to
grammatical distribution; addressee orientation, conventional response slots, and felicity conditions
matter to interactional subtyping. Neither set automatically determines whether bye belongs to a
superordinate category.
     Under the interaction-facing criterion adopted here, closing bye is a positive case: it’s a conven-
tional minimal resource in an identifiable sequential environment. That assignment doesn’t erase the
distinction between closing formulae, continuers, and stance displays. It makes the proposed super-
ordinate classification broad enough to include them, leaving each subtype’s further predictions to be
evaluated separately. Field relativity permits this reconstruction; its usefulness still requires evidence
beyond the freedom to draw a boundary.
     Which projective declarations are available depends on what was observed. A semantics-facing
classification of a novel stance form may motivate targets in unobserved grammatical or interactional
environments. A routine-formula classification may motivate addressee orientation or placement in a
conventional social sequence, provided those properties weren’t used to assign the case. The overlap
explains why routine formulae sit at the boundary; the divergence explains why the classifications
needn’t agree. Section 5 fixes the source–target discipline.

2.6 Co-occurrence, not necessity
These properties recur together in the descriptive literature without being uniformly present. Some
widely adopted definitions consequently qualify each criterion they state. Wilkins (1992, p. 124) de-
fines an interjection as “a conventional lexical form which (commonly and) conventionally consti-
tutes an utterance on its own, (typically) does not enter into construction with other word classes, is
(usually) monomorphemic, and (generally) does not host inflectional or derivational morphemes”.
Four criteria, a parenthetical hedge on each, and Gehweiler (2008, p. 73) adopts the definition while
calling those criteria the class’s “obligatory properties”. Those qualifications leave this definition with
typicality judgements rather than an exceptionless membership rule.
     A sharper proposal belongs here. Libert (2012, pp. 287, 292) proposes exactly one criterion: in-
terjections have a conversational valence of 1, meaning they can be used felicitously with no
addressee present, where goodbye requires one and so has a valence of 2. If you stub your toe you can
say ouch! alone. On this account interjections are “a homogeneous word class, clearly delimited by a
sole criterion” (Libert, 2012, p. 294).
     The criterion supplies a narrower grouping. What it excludes is explicit: interjections “as I have
redefined them, have only one function (as ‘expressions of feeling’); the function of ‘marks of address’
is undertaken by another class” (Libert, 2012, p. 294). Addressing uses are moved out, including hey,
shh, and psst, and the same restriction bears on continuers, repair initiators, and other responses that
require an interlocutor. A category defined this way needn’t coincide with a grammar’s interjections
or with the interaction-facing classification proposed here.
     Libert credits Ameka (1992, p. 109) with a related distinction while objecting that the contrast be-
tween “directed at” and “addressed to” people is obscure. The disagreement shows why the purpose
and consequences of a criterion matter. Conversational valence and expressive content pose different
questions: whether a use requires an addressee and how it contributes meaning. An expressive sign
may occur in address or in solitary use, so the mere presence of an addressee doesn’t settle its valence.
Libert’s criterion can identify an informative subtype without deciding the standing of the broader
classifications.
     Nothing in the present approach forbids a category with a single reliable diagnostic. The question
is what that diagnostic distinguishes and what further expectations it warrants. In the grammatical
tradition, lexemes are classified “on the basis of their inflection, the kinds of dependent they take and
the function in larger constructions of the phrases they head” (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 30).
Those independent dimensions motivate retaining a broader distributional comparison here. Their
multiplicity doesn’t prove a causal network, and no convention about the required number of criteria
settles categoryhood.
     Co-occurrence without necessity doesn’t settle whether membership itself is graded. The analysis
needs a more local discipline. Each diagnostic has to be assessed for a specified sign-type: complement-
taking damn either takes an NP complement in the construction at issue or it doesn’t, while the

3 The causal-network framework
  The profiles in Section 2 give the framework two tasks. One is classificatory: to preserve differences
  that disappear when every use receives a single interjection verdict. The other is explanatory: to iden-
  tify processes responsible for particular combinations and divergences. The first doesn’t require a
  complete account of the second. A useful classification can precede an explanation of its success, and
  a successful explanation of one historical pathway needn’t establish every classification applied to its
  products.
  3.1 Profiles, projections, and their grounds
  The relations that connect a profile may be local mutual reinforcement, one-directional de-
  pendence, or converging historical routes (Khalidi, 2013, 2018). Each has a proposed role here:
3     The causal-network framework                                                                       14


prosodic–syntactic coupling is a candidate for mutual reinforcement, semantic change may alter
the motivation for a source construction’s argument structure, and distinct recruitment histories
may produce partly convergent outcomes. What matters is which relations the evidence supports.
Calling their representation a network doesn’t establish direction, necessity, or explanatory surplus.
     I take this organizational proposal from Khalidi without requiring the metaphysical conclusion
that interjection is a natural kind. The linguistic question is which classifications support worthwhile
distinctions and projections, and which processes explain them. Nor are field-relative categories nec-
essarily nodes of the same sort as the properties and processes in a causal model. A classification sum-
marizes selected relations in that model. An arrow from bleaching to a change in reference makes
a causal proposal; assigning a sign-type to a category from its observed profile makes an analytical
judgment. Those relations need different evidential treatment.
     Four objects help keep the commitments apart (adapted from Reynolds, 2026c, pp. 11–12). They
fail separately, so evidence for one shouldn’t silently stand in for evidence for another.
– A projective declaration specifies what has been observed, what is predicted, and over which
   cases. Here the bearer is a conventionalized sign-type, and the initial population is present-day En-
   glish. A source classification uses a restricted set of observed diagnostics; its target is an unobserved
   property or environment. A target may belong to the category’s wider profile, but can’t also be
   case-level admission evidence for that declaration.
– Warrant is the case for the inference: the observations, comparisons, and background knowl-
   edge that support it, their strength, and what would defeat it. A declaration can be precise while
   having little warrant. Table 2 grades the proposed explanatory links; Section 5 distinguishes evi-
   dence for predictive success from evidence for a particular representation.
– A profile is the combination attributed to the sign-type. Stability is the further claim that it
   recurs across forms or over time. Network order is a different further claim: that specified pro-
   cesses connect its properties. Recurrence alone doesn’t identify direction, while an independently
   supported historical dependence needn’t establish inventory-wide recurrence.
– Grounds explain why the profile obtains or persists (Reynolds, 2026c, p. 12). Stabilizers are
   grounds that maintain the combination as forms enter and leave. Corrective control is
   stronger: a process restores a specified variable after a disturbance (Reynolds, 2026b, p. 5). I reserve
   homeostatic, Boyd’s term, for this last case, a narrower use than his own (Boyd, 1999, p. 143).
   The evidence here doesn’t establish it.

3.2 Causal networks and prototype accounts
Prototype theory (Rosch, 1975; Taylor, 2003, pp. 43–53) was developed for patterns of unequal typi-
cality, overlapping similarities, and disputed boundaries. It handles much of Section 2’s descriptive
material well. A causal-network account needn’t replace those achievements. Typicality concerns
the organization of a classification; causal explanation concerns how the relevant properties come to
co-occur. The two questions can be asked together.
     Field-relative decomposition adds a more specific possibility. An apparent intermediate case un-
der one undifferentiated label may be an ordinary member of one classification and outside another.
Prenominal damn illustrates this: its expressive contribution doesn’t become less expressive because
its grammar differs from standalone damn!. Separating those judgments removes a forced compro-
mise between them. It doesn’t follow that every disputed boundary will disappear or that member-
ship within each classification must be sharp.
     Aarts (2007, p. 206) distinguishes membership from typicality for adjectives: “utter is as much an
adjective as thin is; it is merely less prototypically a member of that class”. His compromise between
a discrete Aristotelian view and “an unconstricted Sorites-style smooth cline with no clear transition-
point between the categories” (p. 225) offers one possible analysis of a field-relative class. Whether it
fits a particular interjection classification remains a local question. The present proposal doesn’t need
all three to have the same membership structure.
     Zimmer and Ackermann (2025, p. 5) instead model core and periphery as “end points of a con-
tinuum”, with deviations accumulating: “A lexeme that exhibits several peripheral features is further
away from the centre than another that only deviates in terms of a single aspect”. Their example
of names, syntactically prototypical nouns but morphologically peripheral ones, also reveals depen-
dence on the chosen perspective. The issue here is which differences should be combined into one
typicality measure and which should remain separate classifications. That decision requires an infer-
ential purpose, not a general prohibition on continua.
     The historical proposal supplies a further question: why do deviations take the particular forms
they do? If independent histories explain retained complementation, euphemistic deformation, or
a source-specific meaning, those differences aren’t merely unstructured distance from a centre. A
synchronic similarity description doesn’t by itself supply that explanation. Diachronic prototype
and centre–periphery accounts can, however, incorporate it. Zimmer and Ackermann (2025, p. 8)
relate German nouns lacking genitive -s to loanword integration, and Heine (2023, p. 48) explains
overlap between interactive types through differing degrees of grammaticalization.
     Pathway residue therefore tests a historical explanation rather than uniquely discriminating net-
works from prototypes. A stronger comparison would require both accounts to specify different
expectations for the same held-out cases. Conditioning could likewise show that some apparent gra-
3   The causal-network framework                                                                    17


dience comes from combining distinct uses or populations; continued variation after credible condi-
tioning would leave a gradient analysis available. The GloWbE frequency probe below doesn’t make
that comparison and can’t settle the shape of a category boundary.
3.3 What causal structure would add
A property list records recurrent association. A feature analysis can encode restrictions and derive
consequences from them. A developed constructional analysis can do considerably more: relate
item-specific knowledge to productive patterns, represent links among constructions, and explain
restricted generalization through the organization and learning of that knowledge (Goldberg, 2006,
Ch. 6). Construction grammar isn’t merely an inventory against which any account mentioning a
process automatically gains explanatory depth (cf. Croft, 2001; Goldberg, 1995).
     The present proposal is compatible with a constructional implementation. Its distinctive com-
mitment is to make the relations used in cross-constructional and cross-field inference explicit and
to grade their warrant. Whether that organization produces explanatory or predictive gains is a fur-
ther question. Ordinary constructional inheritance can explain historical residue, and a model with
shared causes can explain co-movement without direct links among every pair of properties. Those
are alternatives to compare, not consequences to exclude by definition.
     For example, an association between independent prosodic packaging and external non-
integration admits several explanations. Prosody might constrain opportunities for integration,
syntactic organization might favour a particular prosody, or a conventional expressive construction
might supply both. These explanations can yield similar observations in the familiar examples. Their
differences concern changes under independently identified conditions, not whether one diagram
has an arrow and another has a constructional link. A useful comparison must say which observation
would favour one explanation over the others and why that observation isn’t also used to define the
construction or the properties being related.
     Five questions organize that comparison. First, which observed properties predict which unob-
served ones, at what grain? Second, does the proposed representation generalize more accurately
or economically than an alternative with comparable information and flexibility? Third, which his-
torical or synchronic processes explain the dependencies? Fourth, does a profile recur as the inven-
tory changes? Fifth, what observations constrain or defeat each claim? These questions concern pre-
diction, representation, explanation, stability, and failure respectively. Success on one shouldn’t be
counted as success on all five.
     The strongest existing material concerns the third question: documented recruitment and some
source-compatible residues. It supports particular histories more strongly than it establishes direct
synchronic coupling. Turnover addresses change in inventory, leaving profile stability to be measured
separately. The archived derivative and fie analyses fail at the level of measurement, so their outputs
supply neither confirmation nor disconfirmation of the intended substantive hypotheses. Even a
valid test of ordered erosion would constrain a particular model of change; it wouldn’t test every
network compatible with the synchronic profiles.
     An explanatory advance would identify a dependency that a developed alternative fails to explain,
or show that an explicit process account organizes otherwise separate facts with fewer unsupported
commitments. A predictive advance would require a suitable comparison on unobserved cases. Nei-
ther needs the category label to cause speakers’ behaviour. Conversely, reproducing familiar distribu-
tions in a diagram establishes neither advance. The framework earns its keep only to the extent that
3   The causal-network framework                                                                       18


its distinctions make the commitments clearer and its retained claims survive those comparisons.
3.4 Three field-relative categories
The explanatory interests categories serve “diverge somewhat within different scientific disciplines
and subdisciplines” (Khalidi, 2013, p. 71). Different inquiries can therefore preserve different distinc-
tions in the same objects. This doesn’t make successful classification arbitrary: an inquiry fixes the
question, while evidence determines whether the classification answers it. Nor does it require every
field to receive exactly one category or every property to belong exclusively to one field.
     Ponsonnet (2025, p. 3) takes a nominalist position: interjections “are not a natural class that would
exist prior to our defining them”, and definitions are “more or less suited to a scientific goal or an-
other”. I agree that goals matter to the choice. Whether the selected grouping also supports projec-
tions to unexamined cases is a further question about the forms. This paper proposes three classifica-
tions for that evaluation, drawing on existing traditions without treating their extensions as already
settled by those traditions.
     The proper-noun/proper-name distinction supplies a close parallel. Proper noun principally
serves grammar, while proper name principally serves semantics, pragmatics, and onomastics. Nei-
ther is domain-pure. Names have grammatical structure, and proper nouns have conventional ref-
erential tendencies. Their extensions still come apart: the University of Toronto is a name whose
head is a common noun, while a proper noun can be used predicatively or metalinguistically without
straightforwardly naming its usual bearer. Neither classification is a defective version of the other.
     The interrogative/question distinction makes a similar point. Interrogative identifies a gram-
matical clause type; question identifies an action that can make an answer relevant. Can you pass
the salt? is interrogative in form and ordinarily performs a request. Such mismatches motivate keep-
ing the classifications separate. They don’t establish that grammatical form is irrelevant to action or
that the corresponding categories have equal predictive value for every task.
     Noncoextension is likewise a reason to keep judgments distinct, not sufficient evidence that every
resulting column is an explanatory category. One could represent all the same contrasts in a construc-
tional inventory without assigning the three proposed labels. The question is what each grouping
then contributes: a dependable descriptive generalization, a useful way to share evidence among signs,
or an explanation of otherwise disconnected behaviour. The examples establish that the proposed
judgments aren’t interchangeable. The projectibility comparisons ask whether organizing them into
these particular classes does further work.
     The three proposed interjection classifications follow that logic. Their common bearers are sign-
types identified under the discipline above; their profiles and principal consumers differ:
– interjectionsyn is the grammar-facing classification, developed from the lexical category in
   CGEL and related grammatical accounts. Its principal questions concern distribution and
    packaging. Source evidence includes characteristic standalone or supplement use, morphological
    invariance, external non-integration, and restrictions on phrase-internal organization. These
    properties need separate coding: taking a complement is an internal relation, whereas supplement
    status concerns the expression’s relation to its host. CGEL’s tentative treatment of complement-
    taking damn accordingly remains a substantive classification question, not a violation of a
    definition that has already decided the answer.
– interjectionsem is the semantics-facing classification, developed in relation to Potts (2007)’s ex-
    pressives. Its principal questions concern conventional meaning contribution. Independence
3   The causal-network framework                                                                       19


    from descriptive content, perspective dependence, nondisplaceability, and descriptive ineffability
    are candidate diagnostics (Potts, 2007, p. 166). Prenominal damn provides a clear case beyond the
    grammar-facing class. The term doesn’t assume that every form traditionally called an interjection
    has the complete expressive profile; extension to further sign-types requires the relevant semantic
    evidence.
– interjectionprag is the interaction-facing classification, developed from analyses of conven-
    tional minimal resources in sequence organization (Dingemanse, 2024, p. 265). It includes
    candidate response, stance, repair, continuer, change-of-state, and routine uses. Their action
    subtypes must remain visible, because a continuer and a repair initiator don’t project the same
    next turn. At the non-lexical edge, conventionalized sighs, sniffs, and clicks connect it with
    Dingemanse (2020)’s liminal signs.
      The subscripts name principal projective families, not exclusive sources of evidence. Each classifi-
cation can depend on relations among form, grammar, meaning, interaction, convention, and history.
They needn’t be equally rich, equally well supported, or equally sharp. A disagreement over an indi-
vidual assignment may reflect missing evidence, rival criteria, or a genuine boundary problem; these
shouldn’t all be treated as degrees of membership.
      Field relativity therefore concerns the inferential task as much as the institutional field. A gram-
marian studying turns may need the interaction-facing distinctions, and a semanticist investigating
expressive modifiers may need detailed phrase structure. The same investigator can use more than one
classification in one analysis. What changes is the contrast being preserved and the target being pre-
dicted. This also limits the claim of equal standing: there is no general precedence among inquiries,
but evidence can favour one representation decisively for a particular question. Refusing automatic
priority doesn’t require treating unsuccessful projections as equally useful.
      Recent accounts illuminate the alternatives. Heine (2023, pp. 1, 7–8) proposes a cross-linguistic
“grammatical category of its own”, interactives, prototypically “an invariable deictic form that
is in some way set off from the surrounding text semantically, syntactically and prosodically and can
neither be negated nor questioned”. Mushin et al. (2023, pp. 159, 164) questions criteria built on word
classes and phrase-and-clause syntax and argues for integrating a grammar of conversational turns into
descriptive practice. Dingemanse (2023, p. 4) puts the interactional orientation succinctly: “The true
home of interjections is in conversation”.
      Heine, like the present proposal, individuates uses: of five uses of fuck, only the standalone one
is “within the scope of the present study” (Heine, 2023, p. 12). Evaluative damn in That damn Ka-
plan was promoted is excluded as “a modifier of sentence grammar” (pp. 334–335), and German modal
particles are excluded because, “While their functions are clearly interactional, they are syntactically
integrated” (p. 51). These exclusions are intelligible for his classification. They also show why expres-
sive and interactional inquiries may need a different extension.
      Mushin et al.’s turn grammar supplies a further source of grammatical and interactional evidence
rather than a reason to discard clause-level observations. Turn positions can establish a source envi-
ronment for a pragmatic declaration; the question is what predicts treatment in another environment.
The CGEL classification instead preserves distinctions in phrase structure and external integration.
An analysis can investigate both without assuming that one vocabulary exhausts the grammar of con-
versation.

4.1 Recruitment pathways
The cases in Section 2 motivate three recruitment pathways: semantic bleaching from nouns, syntac-
tic deactivation from verbs, and coinage via onomatopoeia. The first has a developed historical case;
the proposed verbal route and individual coinage histories have less extensive documentation here.
The first two are analysed in detail below; the third returns in Section 4.7. These routes identify pos-
sible sources of present-day profiles rather than necessary stages through which every member must
pass.
     The pragmatic issue is how changes in communicative use become categorially consequential.
Grammaticalization and pragmaticalization matter because they can alter a form’s communicative job
together with its grammatical distribution (cf. Traugott and Dasher, 2002, p. 26; Heine and Kuteva,
2002, pp. 2–3). The communicative uses themselves have histories, which historical pragmatics seeks
to explain (cf. Jucker & Taavitsainen, 2013, pp. 3–5). A historical explanation should specify which
properties change, which are inherited, and which haven’t been independently established.
4.2 Path 1: religious noun to interjection
Gehweiler (2008) traces the development of gee from Jesus through three positions:
 (5)     a. Proper name used as invocation (Jesus! addressed to the deity)
         b. Secondary interjection: exclamatory use, no longer addressing the deity (Jesus! as an
            exclamation)
         c. Primary interjection: clipping yields gee!, which detaches from the source noun
    The two transitions don’t work the same way. Gehweiler explains the first as gradual and speaker-
based, through Traugott and Dasher’s invited inferencing, though “advanced by the syntactic isola-
tion of Jesus”, a factor she counts as hearer-based (Gehweiler, 2008, pp. 84–85). The second she treats
4   Causal structure                                                                                  22


as abrupt and hearer-based: tabooistic clipping produced gee!, and she insists that this step “was not
gradual” and “can therefore not be explained with reference to Invited Inferencing or syntactic posi-
tion” (Gehweiler, 2008, p. 88).
     The positions layer properties unevenly. Prosodic isolation is already available in the invocation,
because a vocative like Jesus! can occupy its own intonation unit (contrast the integrated argument
in Put your faith in Jesus). What changes in the secondary interjection is the referential relation: the
speaker is no longer addressing the deity. Phonological divergence then obscures the connection to
the source. The invocation supplies a syntactically peripheral slot, which Gehweiler, together with
invited inferencing, credits with enabling the first change (Gehweiler, 2008, p. 85). She denies that
the same explanation extends to the clipping.
     Both gradual and abrupt changes can leave historical residues. Here the documented clipping
explains a particular residue: the phonological shape of gee! records taboo avoidance even where
speakers no longer recognize its source. That claim depends on the independent history; opacity
alone wouldn’t identify the route. Gehweiler’s record fits an abrupt innovation: gee! is first attested
in 1895, and “there are no transitional, ambiguous uses attested” (Gehweiler, 2008, pp. 85–86). Eu-
phemistic forms of religious names are much older; gog for God is attested from the fourteenth cen-
tury (Gehweiler, 2008, p. 81). Abruptness concerns this transition, not a general condition on path
dependence.
     Two cautions matter. The boundary between invocation and exclamation is hard to operational-
ize in historical texts, since prosodic and interactional evidence is indirect. And gee! arose under
Victorian taboo pressure (Gehweiler, 2008, pp. 85–86), so social pressure did work that shouldn’t be
credited to an autonomous tendency toward an interjectional endpoint. The account requires only
the more restricted claim that social pressure, invited inference, and reanalysis contributed differently
to this form’s development.
     Gehweiler (2008, pp. 71–72, 88) accordingly distinguishes gradual Invited Inferencing (speaker-
based: the expressive use is conversationally implicated, then conventionalized) from abrupt reanaly-
sis (hearer-based: clipping obscures the source, and hearers no longer recognize Jesus! as the semantic
base of gee! ). These are successive processes within the documented route, operating on different
properties and at different timescales. Their combination explains how a familiar cluster can emerge
without every property changing together. It doesn’t establish synchronic order among those prop-
erties.
     This sequence motivates a restricted prediction for independently identified cases on comparable
invocative routes. A conventional exclamatory use may have lost address to the deity while retain-
ing source phonology and the peripheral packaging already available to the invocation. Such a case
needn’t await clipping to become prosodically independent, and it needn’t subsequently undergo
clipping at all. The proposed relation is between semantic change and retained source form, not be-
tween an allegedly incomplete category membership and a necessary next stage. Testing it requires
historical evidence for the source and independent coding of reference, form, and packaging.
4.3 Path 2: verb to interjection
A second proposed trajectory involves syntactic deactivation. Damn has a verbal source, and the
optative construction supplies a plausible gateway. Optatives include God save the Queen and God
help you, where a main-clause subjunctive survives in a narrow range of formulaic frames (Huddleston
& Pullum, 2002, p. 944). God damn him has that shape, and the wish interpretation of damn him is
4   Causal structure                                                                                    23


compatible with reanalysis after omission of the subject.
     The proposed path is reconstructive rather than a demonstrated token-by-token derivation. The
optative already contains plain-form damn, so no tense morphology need be lost in the proposed tran-
sition. What survives in damn these mosquitoes! is an NP complement compatible with verbal source
syntax. In CGEL’s tentative synchronic analysis, damn is an exceptional interjection heading an In-
tjP with an NP complement rather than a VP (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, 1361 n. 70). Its disputed
category assignment must remain separate from the uncontroversial presence of the complement.
     If the reconstruction is right, the development involves reduced verbal obligations and increas-
ingly conventional expressive force. As a verb, damn contributes content involving condemnation;
standalone damn! conventionally displays frustration. The present-day contrast and complement
structure motivate the reconstruction, but they don’t establish the sequence independently. Nor
does a form’s taking a complement entail its integration into a surrounding clause: internal phrase
structure and external distribution are different properties.
     This isn’t degrammaticalization in Norde’s sense, since the changes she treats that way start from
grams and bound morphemes (cf. Norde, 2009, p. 186), whereas the proposed source here is a lexi-
cal verb. Nor does the form join “a major (inflected) word class”, a parameter she scores separately
(Norde, 2009, p. 192). Pragmatic conventionalization with reduced verbal obligations describes the
proposal more closely.
     For other verb-derived forms the imperative is a candidate gateway. Imperatives characteristically
lack an overt subject and can stand alone prosodically. Discourse-marker uses of come on and look
show reduced argument-structural obligations in the relevant sign-types. Those patterns are compat-
ible with deactivation, but again don’t supply a historical series by themselves.
     The residue hypothesis is that independently established verbal sources should help predict sur-
viving restrictions in recruited uses. Complementation may supply one observation; withheld targets
must concern other source-compatible behaviour, such as the interpretation or distribution of de-
pendents. Predicting complement retention from complement retention would add nothing. The
present cases motivate this comparison without establishing a category-wide relation.
4.4 Convergent pathways
The two paths exploit gateway constructions that already permit standalone or peripheral packaging:
invocation for Path 1 and, on the reconstruction above, an optative or imperative for Path 2. They can
yield similar profiles through changes in different properties, while preserving different source rela-
tions. Their documented strength is asymmetric: the invocative history is established in substantially
more detail than the proposed verbal path.
    Both can be situated within Traugott and Dasher (2002, p. 40)’s subjectification trajectory (con-
tent > procedural, nonsubjective > intersubjective), but they instantiate it differently. On the gram-
maticalization analysis of Path 1, the invocation carries content meaning (addressing an entity) to-
gether with subjective stance (reverence); the secondary interjection loses the addressive relation as ex-
pressive force becomes conventional. Clipping then obscures the source. Gehweiler finds “no further
subjectification” in that last step: gee! is “neither more nor less subjective” than Jesus! ; only a phrase
such as gee I wonder! is more intersubjective (Gehweiler, 2008, pp. 88–89). Path 2 instead proposes
decategorialization involving reduced verbal morphosyntax and conventional expressive force.
    Onomatopoeic coinage (Section 4.7) needn’t involve either transition. A newly coined form can
acquire a conventional use without an earlier noun or verb stage, and thus without bleaching or deacti-
4   Causal structure                                                                                 24


vation of such a stage. Grammaticalization, pragmaticalization, and coinage can yield forms assigned
to one or more of the same synchronic classifications. A causal-network representation keeps these
routes distinct while allowing their outcomes to be compared. This is an explanatory synthesis com-
patible with developed historical and constructional accounts, not a claim that those accounts can’t
represent the histories. Shared outcomes alone don’t establish shared synchronic causes.
4.5 Synchronic coupling
Recruitment concerns how profiles arise. The synchronic question is whether particular depen-
dencies help sustain them. Four proposals need to be separated: prosodic-syntactic coupling,
construction-specific relations between semantic change and distribution, interactional routine
entrenchment, and regional/indexical conditioning. The first two concern structural dependencies;
the third is a stabilizer candidate for interactional profiles; the fourth asks whether community
conditions maintain an item, a profile, or both. None follows merely from recurrent co-occurrence.
4.5.1 Prosodic isolation and syntactic non-integration
Prosodic separation and external syntactic non-integration may reinforce each other. Material pack-
aged separately may be less available for integration into the host, while material outside the host’s
constituent structure may receive separate packaging. The corpora used below are written, however,
and their punctuation and position proxies can’t establish either direction (Section 2.1).
     The diachronic record supplies a related but different dependence. Gehweiler (2008, p. 85) finds
that invocational Jesus “typically stands utterance-initially and – like other interjections – set apart
from the rest of the utterance, or even on its own”, and argues that “only because of this syntactic
isolation of the proper name – and Invited Inferencing, of course – could the secondary interjection
Jesus! develop from the proper name”. That supports a role for peripheral syntax in this semantic
development, not a causal effect of prosody on syntax. She also excludes the later clipping from the
same explanation (Gehweiler, 2008, p. 88).
     The synchronic contrast is clear. Standalone Damn! has separate prosodic packaging and no role
inside a host clause. In I think that, damn, we’re going to be late, damn occurs as a parenthetical
supplement. In the damn car, the form is inside the NP and receives the phrase’s prosody. The
contrast supplies co-variation across constructions, rather than an observed intervention that changes
one property while holding the others fixed.
     Dingemanse and Akita (2017) explain similar co-variation in ideophones through a common
cause. Because both properties are often cited as defining features, explaining their relation through
their co-occurrence “would border on tautology”; they derive both from depiction rather than de-
scription (p. 526), and propose a generalized version for interjections (p. 529). In Bambara, an ideo-
phone in non-final position loses its high intonation and expressive-adverb status together (p. 506),
which they relate to how depictive meaning is realized. Standalone and prenominal damn both index
attitude, but differ in how indexical and descriptive material combine. They therefore don’t decide
between direct coupling and a common cause.
     A useful prediction must separate external integration from internal structure. Independently
observed prosodic isolation may predict reduced integration into a host across withheld environ-
ments. It doesn’t by itself predict an absence of complements or modifiers within the isolated ex-
pression: phrases with internal structure can form their own intonation units. Complementation,
phrase-internal modification, and coordination consequently require distinct observations and tar-
4   Causal structure                                                                                   25


gets. Joint changes in packaging and external integration would support the proposed association;
identifying its causal direction requires more than that association.
4.5.2 Semantic change and constructional dependencies
Semantic bleaching can remove motivations for a source construction without removing every kind
of syntactic structure. Loss of entity reference in an invocative use may reduce the relevance of nom-
inal environments that introduce, identify, or quantify the source referent. Reduced use of a verb’s
source event meaning may similarly accompany changes in its complement frame. These are separate,
construction-specific hypotheses. Being an argument, licensing an argument, modifying another ex-
pression, and contributing propositional content can’t be treated as one property.
     For a standalone stance use independently identified by its contribution, proposed targets include
resistance to ordinary nominal frames: *the damn!, *every ouch, and *Damn surprised me, with the
stance use held constant. Whether the same use embeds as propositional content, as in *She said that
damn, is a further target, distinct from reference. Quotation, metalinguistic mention, and conven-
tional noun or verb uses must be identified independently, rather than removed after a prediction
fails.
     There’s no general ratchet from non-referentiality to syntactic isolation. Prenominal damn re-
tains expressive meaning while modifying inside an NP, and complement-taking damn retains inter-
nal structure. Nor do the derivative uses examined below suggest that return to integrated grammar
is blocked. The narrower proposal is that independently supported changes in meaning help explain
particular distributional changes, given a source construction and a recruited use. Persistence and
reversibility have to be investigated rather than inferred from that proposal.
4.5.3 Interactional routine entrenchment
The interaction-facing classification motivates a candidate stabilizer of its own. Conversation repeat-
edly places forms in recognizable sequential slots: continuers, repair initiators, change-of-state tokens,
confirming answers to polar questions, where 81 % of confirmations are interjections (Stivers, 2019,
p. 195), and stance displays (Dingemanse, 2020, pp. 189–190). Dingemanse (2024, pp. 257–258) calls
the first three the “real workhorses” of the class, central rather than peripheral among high-frequency
interjections.
     Repeated deployment may favour particular packages, but two explanatory levels remain distinct.
On an occasion, mm-hm can serve as a continuer without taking over the turn or adding propositional
content. Oh can mark an information update before further at-issue content, and bruh can display
evaluation without altering the surrounding proposition. Across occasions, those uses may entrench
expectations about the form. The stronger inventory-level hypothesis is that such expectations help
maintain corresponding grammatical and interactional profiles as individual forms gain or lose cur-
rency. That accumulation is proposed here, not established by the frequency probes.
     The targets must also be specific to the interactional subtype. A continuer invites the current
speaker to continue; a repair initiator makes a response relevant; a stance display has different possible
consequences. Recognition as a complete move doesn’t mean completion of the current speaker’s
turn. If entrenchment operates as proposed, classification from source environments should help
predict independently coded recipient treatment in withheld environments. Prosodic packaging or
reportability could supply further targets, but they don’t follow uniformly from membership in the
broad interactional classification.
4   Causal structure                                                                                 26


    The same distinction explains how an interactional function can outlast a particular reduced
form. A full clause such as I agree can do agreement work without the packaging of a minimal re-
sponse form. Such alternatives don’t defeat the existence of an interactional category. They make it
necessary to establish which expectations belong to a specific construction, to an interactional sub-
type, or to the broader classification.
4.5.4 Regional/indexical conditioning
Some candidate forms are sharply concentrated by region, register, or community, and some may
also do identity work. Lah in Singapore and Malaysia, yaar in India and Pakistan, haba in Nigeria,
and bruh, which Louf et al. (2023, p. 8) finds among vocabulary associated with African American
English, motivate that possibility.
    They aren’t the same kind of case. Haba is free-standing: Unuabonah and Daniel (2020) avoid
the terms utterance-initial and utterance-final for it and its Nigerian congeners “because they are free
standing and are far more detached from the utterances than PMs” (Unuabonah & Daniel, 2020,
p. 69). Lah, by contrast, is clause-final and prosodically dependent on its host on either account
discussed by Lim (2007). Gupta treats the particles as “the carrier of the intonation contour of the
utterance” (p. 447); the rival account gives them lexically specified substrate tones. That account
is weakest for lah, one of the few particles not matched to Cantonese (p. 463): candidate Hokkien
sources have “neutral tone and weak stress”, and Bazaar Malay sources “carry no tone” (pp. 462–463).
    The clause-final, host-dependent distribution motivates excluding the relevant lah use from
interjectionsyn . That evidence doesn’t by itself establish either semantic or interactional mem-
bership. A more specific basis for interjectionprag comes from Lee (2022, §1, example 3), who
documents low-falling lah in a confirmation-seeking utterance followed by an affirmative response.
The particle contributes through its host utterance, which is compatible with the pragmatic criterion
here. This use motivates a pragmatic assignment; it doesn’t classify every occurrence of the string.
Lee’s not-at-issue semantic analysis also doesn’t by itself establish the conventional expressive
contribution required for interjectionsem , which remains unresolved for this case. Regional
association could accompany either profile. The GloWbE analysis in Section 6 and Appendix A
measures country-level variation in overall frequencies. It doesn’t measure social indexing through
uptake, stance, enregisterment, style shifting, metapragmatic awareness, or comparable speaker
groups.
    The maintenance hypothesis therefore distinguishes persistence of an item from persistence of a
profile. Out-group adoption could provide a comparison, but its baseline must be the form’s actual
source-community use. For lah, that includes prosodic dependence, not isolation. If an adopted use
retained a recognizable social association while changing its semantic contribution, attachment, or
sequential affordances, that would show that item recognition can survive a profile change. Retain-
ing those properties would show profile continuity. Neither outcome alone would identify regional
or indexical pressure as the cause; a maintenance claim needs evidence connecting that pressure to
persistence.
    The four proposals concern different relations and have unequal support. Together they outline
how historical recruitment and present-day conditions might sustain recurring profiles. They don’t
yet establish a single ordering that governs all forms or all three classifications.

     The archived trajectories and posterior ordering calculation are retained there as superseded out-
puts, not as evidence for or against ordered erosion. Even after retrieval errors were removed, changing
proportions among valid occurrences could reflect a changing mixture of uses rather than one conven-
tional sign-type losing its profile. A valid test therefore needs both independently audited occurrence
coding and a stated relation between occurrence-level diagnostics and sign-type change.
     These audits withdraw the two intended empirical contributions without deciding their hypothe-
ses. Recruitment and attrition needn’t reverse one another, and an ordering hypothesis would remain
auxiliary to the broader claim of historically conditioned profiles. The positive historical case here
rests on the documented invocative route; the conversion and erosion studies specify further ques-
tions whose answers require valid measurements.
4.7 Inventory turnover
Pathway residues concern the histories of forms and profiles. Turnover asks how a recorded inventory
changes. Replacements are compatible with persistent grammatical or interactional opportunities,
but changing ranks don’t establish that the same profiles or dependencies persist.
    COHA illustrates substantial turnover in its interjection-tagged inventory. In the 1820s the six
most frequent lemmas are no, oh, yes, nay, o, and ah. By the 2010s, yeah, hey, and uh have replaced
nay, o, and ah in the top six, while no, oh, and yes retain places throughout (Figure 4). This is replace-
ment in a frequency ranking, not disappearance of the displaced forms. Nay falls from 175 tokens per
million to 2.2; yeah, unattested in the tagged 1820s material, reaches 723.5
    Regional variation supplies a synchronic comparison among recorded inventories. GloWbE
(Davies & Fuchs, 2015, 1.9 billion words across 20 English-speaking countries) contains strong
raw-string concentrations: lah in Singapore and Malaysia, yaar in India and Pakistan, and haba
in Nigeria. Subject to the measurement limits in Section 6, those counts support a geographic
description. They don’t identify sociolinguistic conditioning, category boundaries, or maintenance

     Onomatopoeic coinage supplies another possible source of conventional forms. Here ono-
matopoeia names a relation between a form and a sound, not an exclusive lexical category alongside
noun and verb. Forms standing in that relation span grammatical categories: bang is a noun in a loud
bang, a verb in he banged the door, and, in the grammatical analysis adopted here, an interjection
in Bang! You’re dead. Imitation can therefore supply forms with an interjectional profile without a
preceding nominal or verbal use.
     The stock of imitable sounds isn’t fixed by the stock of animals: devices supply beep, ping, and
whirr, while sounds can be re-rendered, as comics illustrate with bam and pow. On one analysis, inter-
jections are relatively open because they “can be rather freely created by onomatopoeia”, as in comic-
strip yucck and gr-r-r-r (Quirk et al., 1985, p. 74). The proposal concerns a productive recruitment
route, not the assumption that every imitation acquires the same meaning or interactional use.
     Research on ideophones draws a different boundary. Dingemanse (2018, pp. 3, 19) treats ono-
matopoeia as the sound-imitating subset of ideophones, words that “depict, using the iconic affor-
dances of speech”; Dingemanse and Akita (2017, p. 529) distinguishes interjections as “primarily in-
dexical”. Meinard (2015, pp. 150, 167) instead excludes onomatopoeias from interjections: “Isolated
onomatopoeias focus on an object of conceptualization and fulfill the referential function of lan-
guage”, whereas interjections serve conative, phatic, or expressive functions.
     These contrasts bear differently on the three classifications. Standalone depictive Bang! meets
the adopted distributional criterion for interjectionsyn ; CGEL has no ideophone category, and
Diffloth describes ideophones as “conspicuously undeveloped and poorly structured in the languages
of Europe” (Diffloth 1972, as cited in Dingemanse, 2018, p. 12). Its depiction of a sound doesn’t estab-
lish the expressive contribution required for interjectionsem . A state-display use such as oof is a
candidate for that classification, as ouch is, subject to the use-conditional diagnostics. Whether either

  position.
       Interactional recruitment also motivates comparison among source histories. Oxford English Dic-
  tionary (2026a) records bruh as a noun from 1894, as a title before a man’s name, and from 1967 as

  sequence. The stance use has the prosodically separate, non-inflecting, non-referential, externally
  non-integrated profile at issue here, but the aggregate string counts don’t distinguish it from the
  nominal and address uses. They consequently don’t establish its rise separately or the persistence of
  its profile.
       Dingemanse (2020, p. 191) describes signs that equivocate between sound and speech, such as
  sighs, sniffs, and clicks, as liminal, treating that status as “an essential part of their form and function”.
  Such signs “may develop into full-blown interjections as they become increasingly conventionalised
  and on-record”, which Dingemanse (2023, p. 12) identifies as “one source path for interjections”. Cul-
  tural evolutionary processes “sample the landscape of possible resources” (Dingemanse, 2020, p. 191).
  The present proposal relates that landscape to the distinct histories and possible dependencies devel-
  oped above, without assuming that every conventionalized form acquires every cluster property.
       The historical cases establish some pathways and motivate others. The frequency probes describe
  changes and differences in recorded inventories. Those findings make a further question precise with-
  out answering it: what can a restricted observation license about an unexamined property or environ-
  ment, and which proposed classification helps that inference?
5 Projectibility as a testable hypothesis
  The historical cases in Section 4.6 concern relations between source constructions and later profiles.
  Forward projectibility asks a different question: what can restricted observations of an identified sign-
  type tell us about a property or environment not yet observed? This section states declarations that
  could answer it. Their specification is a contribution to test design, not a report that their held-out
  success has been established.
       Three outcomes must be distinguished. A declaration has predictive success when its target
  is sufficiently well anticipated in the specified population. A representation has predictive advan-
  tage when it improves on an appropriate alternative under comparable information and evaluation
  conditions. Causal support concerns the processes responsible for the dependence. A cue can
  predict successfully without a category representation improving on it; a representation can improve
  prediction without identifying a causal direction; a historically established process can explain a case
  without yielding a practically useful predictor for new ones.
  5.1 Partial-observation inference
  Projectibility becomes circular if it says only that an interjection has the properties used to classify it.
  A legitimate declaration fixes the source evidence and withholds the target. The target can belong to

5.3 The intersection and representational value
Joint membership in all three classifications is a conjunction, but mathematical derivability doesn’t
settle whether that conjunction is a useful grouping. The same applies to any category defined from
lower-level features. An unrestricted predictor given the complete membership vector can already
represent the intersection, so adding its label cannot supply information absent from those inputs.
Treating that redundancy as decisive would also disqualify the component categories under the argu-
ment rejected above.
     The empirical question concerns a specified representation and task. An explicit intersection
might help pool sparse observations, constrain a model, or represent an interaction that separate ef-
fects miss. Alternatively it might add complexity without useful improvement. A fair comparison
holds available evidence and evaluation cases constant, specifies whether interactions are already repre-
sented, and assesses both predictive quality and the costs of the representation. Neither an advantage
over an artificially additive baseline nor equality with an unrestricted model settles every question of
category standing.
     I therefore use the intersection descriptively here and leave its further usefulness open. There is
no present evidence of additional projective value, but also no independent test establishing its ab-
sence. Krajinović et al. (2025, p. 557) make a related demand for explanatory surplus in discussing the
iamitive: a proposed aspectual category may be warranted where language-internal mechanisms don’t
explain a marker’s new meanings. Here too, surplus has to be assessed against a specified alternative,
not inferred from the presence or absence of a familiar name.
5.4 What would establish the forward claims
A direct evaluation should hold out entire lexical or constructional families, rather than random to-
kens from already familiar item–variety combinations. The source observations, identity rules, and
target coding must be independent in the relevant respects. The archived derivative analysis illus-
trates why pre-registration alone doesn’t ensure this: an outcome assigned by the coding rule isn’t an
independently measured success. The fie audit likewise shows that a temporal model can’t repair an
ineligible denominator or undocumented prediction-derived labels.
    Predictive success should first be assessed against the target base rate on genuinely relevant occa-
sions. Targets expressed as absences, such as no determiner or no complement, require an opportu-
nity denominator: cases where a competing analysis or controlled elicitation makes the form a live
possibility. Otherwise never encountering the relevant environment can masquerade as a successful
restriction. A further comparison evaluates a category- or network-structured model against compo-
nent cues and developed constructional alternatives given comparable information and flexibility.
    For interactional targets, independent coding can distinguish recognition of a conventional ac-
tion from its particular sequential consequences. For semantic repeatability, a separate judgment or
  6   Discussion                                                                                       35


  production design may be needed. Evaluation should be paired on the same held-out families, assess
  calibration as well as discrimination, and represent uncertainty over items, uses, and environments. A
  high base rate can yield accurate but uninformative predictions; a useful improvement in one subtype
  needn’t generalize to the whole classification.
      Systematic target failure weakens the declaration. Accurate targets without comparative improve-
  ment leave predictive success intact while limiting the claim for that representation. Neither outcome
  alone identifies a causal mechanism. To test a proposed coupling, the design needs evidence that dis-
  criminates direct dependence from a shared cause or a conventional constructional association. The
  three questions can be investigated together, but their results should be reported separately.
  5.5 Category, function, and the domain of projection
  The category/function distinction sharpens the comparison. Supplements of several categories share
  external non-integration and characteristic prosodic packaging. Those properties therefore don’t by
  themselves establish the value of interjectionsyn . Its proposed contribution concerns a wider,
  independently measured distributional profile, including morphological behaviour and restrictions
  across frames. Non-referentiality or expressive meaning may supply cross-category targets, but their
  frequent association with canonical examples doesn’t make them defining consequences of the
  grammar-facing classification.
       Productivity requires a different grain. Onomatopoeia can supply new standalone signs, as dis-
  cussed in Section 4.7, but a productive recruitment route is a property of an inventory and process,
  not a withheld property of one sign-type. It shouldn’t be counted as another case-level success beside
  determiner resistance or supplement behaviour. The same distinction separates a category’s persis-
  tence across turnover from the continued use of an individual item.
       The interactional literature motivates concentrating on sequential targets: frequent central forms
  include continuers, repair initiators, and change-of-state resources (Dingemanse, 2024, pp. 257–259,
  263). It doesn’t establish that the broad pragmatic classification is richer or more predictive than its
  alternatives. Similarly, Potts (2007, pp. 166–167)’s expressive properties motivate semantic targets
  without making all traditional interjections expressives. The relations being investigated should de-
  termine the domain of projection, rather than a prior ranking of grammar, semantics, and interaction.
       The classifications can consequently succeed to different degrees for different purposes.
  interjectionsyn may preserve a useful grammatical contrast without improving a semantic
  prediction; an interactional subtype may be strongly predictive while its superordinate classification
  adds little. A category’s standing needn’t depend on winning every comparison. The stronger claims
  are local: that this declaration is informative, this representation helps, or this process explains the
  dependence. Those are the claims for which the framework requires separate warrant.

6 Discussion
  6.1 What the evidence supports
  The claims developed above don’t stand or fall together. Differences among grammatical, expressive,
  and interactional profiles motivate separate classifications. Independently documented histories can
  explain particular residues. Neither achievement establishes every proposed causal link, and neither
  substitutes for testing predictions made from incomplete profiles. The existing probes have to be
  assessed against the particular claims they can bear on.
6   Discussion                                                                                         36


     The strongest historical case is gee. Gehweiler’s account documents invocative recruitment
and taboo-managed phonological change. Complement-taking damn supplies a plausible verbal
comparison, but its optative sequence remains reconstructive. These cases support the weaker
path-dependence claim: convergence on a similar profile needn’t erase source-specific structure. A
broader claim would require independently established histories to predict residues across cases,
including cases without the expected trace. Residues can’t establish a pathway and then count as
independent confirmation of that same reconstruction.
     This historical account isn’t distinctive merely because it connects a present property to a source.
Grammaticalization, diachronic prototype, and constructional accounts can preserve such relations
too. The proposed network’s contribution is to make particular dependencies and their consequences
explicit, so that alternative explanations can be compared on the same cases. Its adequacy depends on
those links, not on the label network.
     The verbal-derivative study doesn’t add the independent confirmation originally intended. Its
primary-coding script assigns semantic residue to every retained derivative candidate; the uniformly
positive outputs therefore follow from implementation rather than a separately observed semantic
outcome (Appendix B). Those outputs are retained as an audit record, not evidence. The illustrative
distinction remains useful: a derived verb may preserve content associated with a source interjection
while changing how that content contributes. Describing a disapproving response doesn’t itself ex-
press the current speaker’s disapproval. Content continuity and continued interjectionsem mem-
bership are different claims.
     The fie attempt has a different problem. Its model input retains OCR substitutions as zero-
valued linguistic observations despite their preregistered exclusion, and numerous assignments have
unresolved verification provenance (Appendix C). The resulting curves and ordering probability
aren’t evidence for or against ordered erosion. Even a corrected token analysis would need to
distinguish a changing mixture of uses from changing properties conditional on a conventionalized
sign-type. Moreover, the predicted pragmatic–semantic–syntactic order was an auxiliary historical
hypothesis, not a consequence of recognizing three categories. Neither an invalid test nor a valid
failure of that ordering would decide the full framework.
     Inventory turnover establishes a different fact: the forms occupying a conventional tagged inven-
tory change over time. It doesn’t show that a category profile persists through replacement. That
would require comparable diagnostics across periods, rather than continuity of the tag. The recruit-
ment examples and turnover records consequently motivate a stability question; they don’t answer
it.
     GloWbE supplies a narrower distributional probe (Davies & Fuchs, 2015). Candidate forms con-
centrate in particular country-labelled web sections, and a crossed-intercept model estimates addi-
tional overall country variation: country SD 0.44, 95% CrI 0.28–0.65. Its estimated predictive im-
provement is positive but imprecise, approximately 18 ELPD units with SE 13. These results don’t
establish item-type-specific conditioning, category-boundary decomposition, or identity work. Per-
million rates also conflate availability, preference, and frequency of relevant occasions. The counts,
models, and country-specific measurement audit are reported together in Appendix A.7

    cases; matching a descriptive list is insufficient, but failure of one added link needn’t eliminate the
    classifications that remain independently useful.
    6.2 Boundary disputes and explanatory scope
    Separating the three inquiries clarifies boundary disputes without automatically resolving them. Rou-
    tine formulae such as bye, hello, and sorry share standalone availability with canonical interjections,
    while addressee orientation, conventional response slots, and felicity conditions motivate further dis-
    tinctions (Ameka, 1992, pp. 108–110). Social convention and politeness may explain some of that
    organization, but identifying those candidate grounds is separate from demonstrating their effects.
         Fillers such as um and uh share syntactic non-integration but differ in emphasis, pause distri-
    bution, and citation-introduction behaviour (O’Connell & Kowal, 2005, pp. 560, 567–572). Their
    overlap with interjections raises questions about processing and sequential organization rather than
    requiring a single position on an undifferentiated scale.
         Ameka’s separation of routine formulae preserves distinctions in speech-act status and social rou-
    tines (Ameka, 1992, pp. 109–110); Wilkins’s inclusion preserves pragmatic and semantic commonal-
    ities (Wilkins, 1992, p. 142). Both arguments concern meaning and interaction, so assigning each
    author to a different field wouldn’t settle their disagreement. The proposed decomposition instead
    makes the competing contrasts explicit. Which classifications earn a place still depends on the distri-
    butions, explanations, and non-circular projections they support.

7   Conclusion
    The traditional label interjection covers distinctions that a single classification needn’t preserve. I
    have proposed three overlapping classifications: grammar-facing interjectionsyn , semantics-facing
    interjectionsem , and interaction-facing interjectionprag . They organize different questions
    about common sign-types without assigning general priority to one inquiry. They aren’t claimed
    to exhaust every useful grouping, to have uniformly sharp boundaries, or to make their intersection
    projectively redundant.
        The strongest historical evidence concerns particular recruitment pathways and source-
    compatible residues. The history of gee documents an invocative route and euphemistic deformation.
7   Conclusion                                                                                       38


Complement-taking damn is compatible with a verbal or optative source, though the sequence
remains reconstructive. These cases support historical persistence without uniquely establishing the
proposed network. Auditing the archived derivative and fie analyses reveals that neither supplies a
valid test of its intended target. Their numerical outputs can’t establish semantic residue or ordered
erosion. GloWbE supplies evidence about country-level frequency variation, and COHA illustrates
inventory turnover; neither establishes category-boundary decomposition or profile stability.
    The proposed coupling and entrenchment links organize further explanatory questions. Their
evidence must be assessed separately from the usefulness of the classifications they connect. No com-
plete synchronic network, category-level stability, or corrective control has been established. A devel-
oped constructional account remains both a possible implementation and a substantive alternative
explanation.
    Forward projectibility likewise remains to be evaluated directly. The declarations distinguish pre-
dictive success, the advantage of a particular representation, and causal explanation. Their tests re-
quire independently identified bearers, withheld targets, appropriate baselines, and a clear distinc-
tion between broad category predictions and subtype-specific consequences. A failure constrains the
corresponding declaration; it needn’t abolish the other classifications or every historical explanation.
    The methodological claim is that inquiry-relative categories can overlap without competing for
one universally privileged extension. Their standing depends on what they let an inquiry distinguish
and infer. Keeping the bearer, the source evidence, the target, and the explanatory commitment sep-
arate makes that standing open to assessment rather than securing it by definition.

A Supplementary materials: GloWbE conditioning analysis
   A.1 Data and measurement
   Frequency counts for 18 items were extracted from GloWbE (Davies & Fuchs, 2015): 1.9 billion words
   across 20 countries. The items are the 10 most frequent interjection-tagged forms (yes, no, oh, yeah,
   hi, hey, wow, hello, ah, ha), three regional forms (lah, yaar, haba), semi-regional eh, boundary items
   cheers and bye, and fillers um and uh. Core counts are tag-restricted; regional counts are raw strings be-
   cause the tagger doesn’t recognize the relevant uses. Neither measure identifies the three field-relative
   categories independently.
        Country is assigned to a page rather than a speaker. Assignment uses Google’s region function:
   country domains provide a direct cue, but for .com and .org sites Google “relies on several heuris-
   tics, including 1) the IP address for the web server, 2) who links to that website, and 3) who visits
   the website” (Davies & Fuchs, 2015, p. 4). Davies and Fuchs themselves question “how well Google
   has correctly identified web sites by country”. The page list was seeded through Google searches for
   “hundreds of very high frequency 3-grams (three word strings)” from the Corpus of Contemporary
   American English (p. 4), anchoring the sampling frame to American frequency data. Country differ-
   ences may therefore reflect genre mix, internet ecology, and sampling alongside linguistic variation.
        Automatic tagging adds another measurement layer. An overall error rate doesn’t warrant com-
   parisons if validity varies by country (Reynolds, 2026a). Concordance coding estimates precision,
   the proportion of tagged hits that are genuine, not recall, the proportion of genuine tokens retrieved.
   Neither haba nor yaar receives an interjection tag. All 373 Nigerian haba hits are tagged as common
   or proper nouns, with 65% on a reading including the proper noun. The raw-string regional counts
   can therefore overstate frequency while a tag-restricted query misses these forms entirely.
   A.2 Geographic distributions
   Regional concentration is clear in the raw strings: lah occurs at 9.35 per million words in Malaysia
   and 6.72 in Singapore, and yaar at 2.88 in Pakistan and 2.33 in India, with near-zero rates elsewhere.
   Nigeria’s 373 haba hits give an upper bound of about 9 per million. Inspecting hits, counting five
   spellings, and excluding Nigerian Pidgin, indigenous-language material, and duplicates, Unuabonah
   and Daniel (2020, p. 69) report 248 tokens, or 5.8 per million. The gap illustrates why raw strings and
   tagged counts aren’t directly comparable.
        Among the core items, ah has the largest cross-country coefficient of variation (CV; standard de-
   viation divided by mean), 0.71: Singapore’s 60.42 and Jamaica’s 53.16 per million are about eight times
   Kenya’s 7.62. For yeah, CV is 0.55 and the United States–Tanzania comparison is approximately 6:1
   (103.78 versus 16.85). Yes and no have lower CVs, 0.21–0.22. The fillers um and uh have CVs of 0.61
   and 0.76, and bye has 0.61.
        Bootstrap intervals resample the twenty observed country sections. They describe sensitivity to
   that resampling, not sampling uncertainty over all English-speaking communities: the sections aren’t
   a random sample of such communities. The yes/no intervals lie below those of eleven of the thirteen
   other nonregional items, overlapping only hello and ha. This supports their comparatively low ob-
   served dispersion. The remaining marginal intervals overlap; that doesn’t establish equal dispersion
   or settle pairwise differences. A ranking would require paired contrasts within resamples. Regional
   concentration is better represented by the counts: when a form is absent from eighteen sections, re-
   sampling can sharply alter the mean and CV. For lah, the interval runs from 0.89 to 4.27.

A.3 Concordance audit and sensitivity
The tagger’s training on inner-circle English limits its treatment of regional forms, but recognized
items aren’t necessarily clean. An initial twenty-token check of tagged ha in Kenya and Tanzania
retained six and eight interjections respectively; most other hits abbreviated hectare in agricultural
and development prose. Applying the Kenyan estimate reduces 24.9 tokens per million to about 7.5
and moves Kenya from fifth to nineteenth among the twenty sections. This preliminary estimate
differs from the fuller audit below.
     The ha audit also requires an explicit decision about laughter. Iterated HA! HA! may represent
laughter, closer to eye dialect than a lexical item, whereas sarcastic or sceptical ha is an interjectional
use. Written data make the distinction harder, and digital haha may itself be conventionalized. This
decision doesn’t exclude imitative forms in principle; imitation is one proposed recruitment route.
The reported counts include iterated laughter.
     Coding tagged concordances across all twenty sections shows the sensitivity. Laughter constitutes
51.5% of tagged ha in the United States and 63.0% in Great Britain. Across six defensible treatments
of laughter and residual tokens, its CV ranges from 0.40 to 0.83 (cf. Gelman & Loken, 2014); the
reported 0.41 includes laughter. The dispersion measure therefore depends on the criterion. A sepa-
rate ranking result survives: country-specific validity correction moves Kenya from fifth to fifteenth
under every coding.
     The proportion of tagged ha judged interjectional ranges from 34.5% in Bangladesh to 84.0% in
Great Britain. Hectare dominates contamination in East Africa and South Asia; personal names
matter in Singapore, where only 6.1% of hits are hectare. Pooling country rates to reflect unequal
precision – about 8,100 tagged tokens for the United States versus 420 for Ghana – moves no section
by more than 0.6 percentage points. Between-section variation exceeds the sampling variation within
sections. These checks correct false positives; they don’t estimate missed interjections.
     Spelling variants introduce another country-dependent error. Raw ya and yeh look like variants
of yeah, but they concentrate where substrate words are homographs: ya peaks in Tanzania (19.5 per
million) and Malaysia (13.9), and yeh in Singapore (5.6), Pakistan (4.9), and India (4.9). No yeh token
carries the interjection tag. Pooling both strings with yeah reduces its CV from 0.55 to 0.46 while
importing non-English tokens unevenly, so the analysis keeps them separate.
     Adding umm, ummm, and ummmm to um changes its CV from 0.61 to 0.60 with Pakistan ex-
cluded, but to 0.72 with Pakistan included. Pakistan returns nearly eight times as many umm as um
tokens, against a median ratio of about a third elsewhere. In a twenty-token Pakistani sample, nine-
teen are the Arabic teknonym Umm, as in Umm Salama, from hadith or biographical material; the
twentieth mentions the filler. Raw-string lengthening thus measures substrate homography as well
as orthographic variation.
     Regional forms face the same problem. Hand-coding sixty-nine yaar hits puts the English-matrix
rate at roughly 60% in Pakistan and 65% in India; other hits include Tamil yaar “who”, names, and
Roman-script Hindi or Urdu. The relevant twenty-token samples are too coarse for correction, but
establish that raw rates are upper bounds. Raw yes, no, ha, ah, oh, well, right, and like likewise include
clausal, quoted, discourse-marker, literary, or noisy uses. The audit establishes a measurement result:
validity varies across countries, so pooled frequencies can conceal measurement differences alongside
linguistic differences.

A.4 Model specification
Four Bayesian negative binomial models were fitted in brms 2.23 in R, with four chains of 2,000 it-
erations, including 1,000 warmup iterations. Models m0–m2 use exact reloo for leave-one-out cross-
validation; exploratory m3 is assessed through Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS) diagnos-
tics only.

      m0 (unconditional):
      count ~ 1 + offset(log(words_m)) + (1 | item)
      m1 (conditioned):
      count ~ 1 + offset(log(words_m)) +
      (1 | item) + (1 | country)
      m2 (item type):
      count ~ 1 + item_type + offset(log(words_m)) +
      (1 | item) + (1 | country)
      m3 (exploratory):
      count ~ 1 + item_type + offset(log(words_m)) +
      (1 | item) + (1 + item_type || country)

    The country intercept in m1 estimates an overall frequency shift across items, not an item-specific
country effect. Model m3 adds country-varying slopes by item type, but the sparse type cells – semi-
regional: 1 item; boundary: 2; filler: 2; regional: 3 – provide little support for those slopes. Neither
model estimates grammatical availability separately from preference or frequency of relevant occa-
sions.
A.5 Results
Exact leave-one-out comparisons for the confirmatory models are:

                             Model              ELPD diff SE diff
                             m1 (conditioned)        0.0     0.0
                             m2 (item type)         −9.3     7.9

     The country-conditioned model has the highest expected log predictive density (ELPD) point
estimate, with an imprecise advantage over m0: ∆ELPD = 17.9, SE 13.3 (rounded in the main text to
18 and 13). Adding item type doesn’t improve the point estimate. Model m3 isn’t formally ranked
because nine observations retain Pareto k > 0.7 after moment matching, leaving its PSIS estimates
unstable.
     In m1, the country standard deviation is 0.44 (95% credible interval [CrI]: 0.28–0.65), against an
item standard deviation of 2.07 (1.49–2.96), a ratio of 4.7:1. The country interval describes estimated
variation under the model; its positive bounds aren’t independent evidence against an absent effect,
since standard deviations are constrained to be nonnegative. The predictive comparison and descrip-
tive frequencies supply the relevant evidence. With 18 items × 20 countries = 360 observations, the
ELPD uncertainty remains substantial.

    To propagate the ha precision estimates, each country’s count was thinned by draws from its beta
posterior, leaving the other seventeen items unchanged, and m0 and m1 were refitted. Within this

above. Precision correction leaves recall unidentified.

try variation in these measured frequencies. It doesn’t establish social indexing, item-type-specific

   B.2 Intended measures and implementation
   The intended confirmatory measures were VP-head syntax and semantic residue: impressed or eval-
   uative response for wow, disapproval or contempt for boo, appreciative or surprised response for ooh,
   and directive away-sending for shoo. Secondary codes concerned interactional residue – recognizable
   response, uptake, display, or direction – and a recoverable target, object, addressee, or stimulus.
       An implementation audit changes the evidential status of the results. The archived
   primary-coding script first applies exclusion rules, then assigns both verbal_syntax = 1 and
   semantic_residue = 1 to every retained row. The exclusion rules were intended to select verbal
   uses; their accuracy can’t be inferred from the syntax code automatically assigned to every retained
   row. The resulting file contains 1,462 rows: 969 included and 493 excluded. Semantic persistence
   wasn’t independently measured among the included derivatives; it was assigned by the pipeline.
   Consequently the outputs below don’t constitute a confirmatory result.
       Even independently established source content would need separating from expressive meaning
   type. A verb can describe or cause the response expressed by its source interjection without
   contributing use-conditionally itself. The performance wowed us illustrates that distinction. Con-
   tinuity of source-associated content would support a limited historical relation, not continued
   interjectionsem membership.

C Supplementary materials: audit of the fie analysis in COHA
   C.1 Design, coding, and denominator
   The preregistration (e18675b, 15 March 2026) predicted interactional, then semantic, then syntactic
   loss among attested COHA uses of fie, 1820–2019. It excluded metalinguistic mentions, non-English
   passages, metadata, and optical character recognition (OCR) noise. Represented speech, including
   fictional dialogue, was eligible. This ordered-erosion hypothesis was an additional historical commit-
   ment; it doesn’t follow merely from distinguishing three field-relative categories.
        The archived analysis contains 616 coded rows. Its exclusion file separately records 141 entries: 134
   OCR errors, three metalinguistic mentions, two non-English cases, and two name fragments. How-
   ever, the model input still includes obvious OCR substitutions coded zero on all three diagnostics,
   including “FIe stroked his closed eyelids” (F014), “FIe drew her to him” (F028), and “FIe turned his
   attention” (F043). The model filters only for a non-missing syntactic code. Thus the stated exclusion
   rule wasn’t successfully implemented in the analysed denominator.
        The joint syntactic–semantic–interactional codes are 111 for 448 rows, 000 for 139, 011 for 19, 100
   for eight, and 010 for two. In addition, 350 rows carry a note beginning “predicted”; the archive
   doesn’t establish independent manual verification of all these assignments. These are implementa-
   tion findings, not new linguistic classifications. An OCR substitution isn’t an interjection that has
   lost three properties. The archived declines therefore can’t establish either ordered or simultaneous
   linguistic erosion.
   C.2 Archived agreement and proportions
   The decade column was hidden in the randomized coding sheet. The reported 10% blind-recode
   comparison (n = 62) yielded:

                               Diagnostic             Agreement Cohen’s κ
                               interjectionsyn             87.1%    0.68
                               interjectionsem            96.8%     0.90
                               interjectionprag           96.8%     0.90

       These archived agreement values don’t validate inclusion decisions or resolve the provenance of
   predicted codes. Figure 7 likewise records the unvalidated input: proportions of 85–100% through
   the 1890s, a decline between 1900 and 1940, and later fluctuations. Historical fiction and Shakespeare
   adaptations contribute positive represented uses in the 1980s and 2000s. Publication dates index the
   sampled texts, not necessarily the contemporary availability of the represented sign-type.
   C.3 Archived models and interpretation
   The script fits three separate decade-level binomial logistic regressions, with time centred and scaled in
   ten-year units. Its normal priors have mean zero and standard deviations 1.5 for the intercept and 0.5
   for the slope, with the latter restricted to negative values. Each fit uses four chains, 2,000 warmup and
   2,000 subsequent iterations per chain, and seed 2026. The negative-slope restriction imposes mono-
   tonic decline and can’t model revival. The script centres decade labels rather than the midpoints
   specified in the preregistration. Separate likelihoods also leave the three outcomes’ within-token de-
   pendence unmodelled.
