\begin{abstract}
English linguists use \term{interjection} for three overlapping but
non-identical classifications: a grammar-facing lexical category, a
semantics-facing category of expressive or use-conditional signs, and an
interaction-facing category of conventionalized sequential resources. I
argue that none has priority over the others. Each cuts through a mixed
network of prosodic, morphological, syntactic, semantic, interactional, and
historical relations. Each preserves different distinctions and generates a
different family of projective hypotheses. Their common bearers are
conventionalized sign-types: recurrent pairings of form with grammatical
realization, meaning, and interactional affordance.
Canonical cases belong to all three categories, but their overlap isn't a
fourth category and supplies no independent projective force.

The empirical argument concerns path dependence: recruitment histories remain
visible in source-specific residues. Invocative, verbal, and onomatopoeic
routes, documented to different degrees, converge on recurrent combinations
of properties. The history of \mention{gee}, residual
complement structure, euphemistic deformation, and semantic persistence in
selected verbal derivatives support parts of this account. A preregistered
study of \mention{fie} fails to support ordered erosion, while GloWbE and COHA
probes establish more limited patterns of country-level frequency variation
and inventory turnover. These results support path dependence and motivate
parts of a causal ordering. They also yield explicit projectibility
hypotheses: predictions from observed properties to unobserved meaning and
interactional treatment. Their
held-out predictive performance remains to be tested directly.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} interjections; field-relative categories; projectibility;
causal networks; supplements; path dependence

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

English interjections are small forms with large consequences. A
speaker's \mention{oh}, \mention{wow}, \mention{hey}, \mention{damn}, or
\mention{mm-hm} can package a response, display stance, open or sustain a
sequence, initiate repair, or invite recipient uptake through a response or
other displayed treatment. Grammars have never been
settled about what to do with them. This paper argues that part of the
difficulty is hidden by the singular question \enquote{Is interjection a
category?} Grammar, semantics, and interactional pragmatics use the same term
for three overlapping classifications with different extensions and
different inferential purposes.

In 1586, William Bullokar's grammar of English, one of the first,
included interjections. He defined them as words that betoken
% Normalized from Bullokar's 1586 spelling. OTA text
% (literature/bullokar1586-pamphlet-ota.txt, ll. 1599-1605): "a su}dden
% passion of the my^nd: the signific.at}ion or^ maeni.ng of which spe^ch
% mu}st be^ v}nderstande.d by the g^est<u>r, cou}ntenanc^, or^ passion of
% the spaeko.r^"; Plessow p. 373 (OCR l. 29001).
\enquote{a sudden passion of the mind}, whose meaning \enquote{must be
understanded by the gesture, countenance, or passion of the speaker}
\citep[373]{bullokar1586}. His examples run from sorrow and wonder to
calling, silence, and stopping, and he counted
% OTA ll. 745-749, "eu^ery wor^d or^ clau}z of sentenc^"; Plessow p. 350
% (OCR l. 26957, between the page marks for 350 and 351).
\enquote{every word or clause of sentence} so spoken
\citep[350]{bullokar1586}. The class had no settled place even then. Of
the six English grammars printed in England between 1569 and 1621, only
three use the label: Greaves put interjections among adverbs, Gil among
words that yield no meaningful utterance on their own, and Hart, Tonkis,
and Hume left the category out \citep[483--485]{bagli2026}.

It hasn't settled since.
\textcite[90]{jespersen1924} treated interjection as a manner in which
words of other categories may be used, which is to deny it categoryhood.
\textcite[67]{quirk1985} kept the category but called it
\enquote{marginal and anomalous}.
\textcite[22]{HuddlestonPullum2002} listed interjection as one of nine
lexical categories in \textit{The Cambridge Grammar of the English
Language} (\textit{CGEL}) (\mention{ah},
\mention{damn}, \mention{gosh}, \mention{hey}, \mention{oh},
\mention{ooh}, \mention{ouch}, \mention{whoa}, \mention{wow}), while the
companion textbook set interjections aside from its eight-category
inventory \citep[16]{HuddlestonPullum2005}. Its second
edition returns interjection to the inventory, but concedes that
\enquote{grammarians typically say very little about interjections, and
we don't plan to be an exception} \citep[24]{HuddlestonPullumReynolds2022}.
Readmitted to the list, and still largely undescribed.

\textit{The Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English} redraws the class
instead. It makes \term{inserts} a class of \enquote{stand-alone words} marked
by their \enquote{inability to enter into syntactic relations with other
structures}, whose boundary with syntactic non-clausal units is
\enquote{a gradual one} \citep[1082]{Biber1999}. Interjection is one of several
functional types of insert, and \mention{damn}, \mention{gosh}, and
\mention{gee} belong to another, the expletives (pp.~1083, 1094--1095).
\textcite[81, 83]{ljung2011}, like Jespersen, denies the category: interjections
\enquote{should not be regarded as a word class}, and the term is better kept
for exclamative utterances.

\textcite{libert2019} objects that calling interjections neglected
\enquote{is an overstatement}: work on their phonetics, phonology, syntax,
semantics, and pragmatics goes back a long way. What's stayed
unsettled is standing rather than volume of attention: whether grammar has a
lexical category of interjections, whether the same classification serves
semantics and interaction, and what any of those memberships would license.

\textcite[63]{cram2008} makes the historiographic point more sharply.
Surveying the tradition from Greek antiquity onward, he finds \enquote{no
firmer consensus about this category than at any earlier period}, and notes
that the interjection can \enquote{function as a touchstone or test case for various
boundary controversies}, naming the syntax--pragmatics and
semantics--pragmatics boundaries. The difference is in what the case is asked
to reveal.
Cram asks what interjections show about our theories. I ask what holds these
classifications together and what makes them diverge.

The empirical picture hasn't simplified. Evidence bearing on the traditional
domain is spread across phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and
pragmatics. No single property is necessary and sufficient, and the
boundaries with nouns, verbs, adverbs, fillers, and routine formulae are all
disputed. Cross-linguistically, \enquote{interjection} is at best a
comparative concept \citep{haspelmath2010}. But it doesn't follow that only a
morphosyntactic classification can be a category. A semantic or interactional
classification has equal standing if it supports reliable distinctions and
inferences for its inquiry.

The relevant standard is \term{projectibility}: the inferential payoff by
which observing some properties of a new case warrants expectations about
others \citep{Goodman1955}. This paper specifies such expectations for each
interjection category. It doesn't estimate their held-out predictive
performance. The present evidence instead bears on the architecture that
generates them: recurrent clustering, documented and reconstructive
recruitment pathways, patterned residues, and cases where the three
classifications come apart.

A form's \term{profile} is the particular combination of cluster properties it shows
in a given grammatical environment: whether it's prosodically set off,
whether it inflects, whether it refers, what stance or response it carries.
The word is doing bookkeeping here and has nothing to do with the
figure-and-base sense familiar from cognitive grammar. To say that a
property or a category \term{licenses} an inference is to say the inference
is a good default, defeasible by the particular case, not that it follows
necessarily.

The object of analysis is a conventionalized linguistic
\term{sign-type}: an expression paired with a conventional profile of
grammatical realization, meaning, and interactional affordance. The three
classifications apply to that common bearer. Thus verbal \mention{damn} in
\mention{I damn you}, standalone \mention{damn!}, complement-taking
\mention{damn these mosquitoes!}, and prenominal \mention{damn} in
\mention{the damn car} are distinguishable sign-types even though they belong
to one historical word family.

By \term{path dependence}, I mean that where a form came from keeps
showing in how it behaves at the category's edges, long after it has
arrived. \mention{Gee} is documented as developing through a religious
invocation and takes no complement. Complement-taking \mention{damn} is
compatible with a verbal optative source and retains NP complementation
(\mention{damn these mosquitoes!}). The second history remains partly
reconstructive, but the contrast illustrates the test: if convergence erased
history, present-day residues shouldn't align with independently supported
sources.

Throughout, I use \term{property cluster} descriptively for an empirical
pattern of co-occurring features. A cluster isn't automatically a category,
and recurrent co-occurrence isn't automatically causal order. Those further
claims require their own evidence.

This inferential framing motivates a causal-network analysis.
\textcite{khalidi2013,khalidi2018nodes} argues that natural kinds are
nodes in causal structures rather than sets defined by essences. On
this account, a category is theoretically useful when its place in a
causal network supports reliable induction. Applied to interjections,
the proposal is that diagnostics including prosodic isolation, syntactic
independence, non-referentiality, non-inflection, and interactional force are
connected through multiple local links and historical pathways. The strongest
present evidence concerns recruitment and residue. Other links remain
ordering hypotheses or stabilizer candidates.

Four things have to be kept apart, because they fail
separately \citep[adapted from][11--12]{reynolds2026projectibilityHistory}. Each
has a label I'll use throughout.

\begin{itemize}
\item The \term{projective declaration} states the claim: what's observed,
  what's predicted from it, and over which cases. Here a source is a specified
  field-relative classification supported by a restricted set of observed
  diagnostics. A target is a genuinely unobserved property or environment,
  the bearer is a conventionalized sign-type, and the population is
  present-day English. A target may belong to a category's general
  individuation profile, but it can't also be part of the case-level evidence
  used to make that declaration.
\item \term{Warrant} is the separate question of why that inference is
  licensed and how strongly: what evidence supports it, and what would defeat
  it. Table~\ref{tab:link-status} records this link by link.
\item The profile just defined is the worldly relation the declaration posits,
  and \term{stability} is the further claim that it recurs across forms and
  over time. \term{Network order} is a claim of a different kind: that the
  properties are linked, so that one makes another more available.
  \emph{Stability and network order are independent.} A combination can
  recur without revealing any direction among its parts, and a directed
  dependence can hold without recurring.
\item \term{Grounds} explain why the profile obtains or
  persists \citep[12]{reynolds2026projectibilityHistory}.
  \term{Stabilizers} are grounds that hold the combination together as forms
  enter and leave. \term{Corrective control} is the strongest, a stabilizer
  that restores the combination when something disturbs
  it \citep[5]{reynolds2026notEveryStableCluster}. I reserve
  \term{homeostatic}, Boyd's term, for corrective control, a narrower use
  than his own \citep[143]{boyd1999}, and the evidence here doesn't
  establish it.
\end{itemize}

One form shows what that comes to. Take \mention{gee}, as in
(\ref{ex:gee}).

\ea\label{ex:gee} \exmention{\exfocus{Gee}, that's a lot of money.}
\z

Observing that \mention{gee} is set off from what follows and never inflects
(no *\mention{geed}, no *\mention{gees}; the \mention{gee} of \mention{gee up}
is a different word) supplies the source side of a possible declaration,
though the absence of inflection carries weight only on occasions where a
verb or noun analysis predicts those forms, and this paper doesn't count them
(Section~\ref{sec:cluster}). The proposed targets are that the sign-type will work as
a supplement in unobserved environments, contribute use-conditionally rather
than descriptively, and be available at the front of a turn. Failure on those
targets would defeat the corresponding projections. Complement-taking or
inflection would instead undermine the source classification and so shouldn't
be counted again as failed target behaviour.

Diachrony matters because pragmatic profiles have histories. Forms such
as \mention{gee}, \mention{damn}, or \mention{bruh} change category
position as historically contentful or addressive expressions come to
manage stance, sequence, and interactional alignment in recurrent
contexts \citep[cf.][3--5]{juckertaavitsainen2013}. Change over time helps
explain why recurrent profiles and source-specific residues can coexist while
individual forms enter and leave an inventory.

The network classifies sign-types, not word families as wholes. I write the
three categories as \intj{syn}, \intj{sem}, and \intj{prag}. Bare
\enquote{interjection} remains a conventional cover term for the traditional
domain and for canonical cases that satisfy all three classifications. Their
overlap does no independent classificatory or projective work: joint
membership simply makes the three component families of inference available.
Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes} defines the categories and explains why
their extensions overlap but come apart.

Section~\ref{sec:cluster} documents the cluster.
Section~\ref{sec:causal-framework} introduces the causal-network
framework and the three field-relative categories. The causal sections grade
the proposed links by evidential status and develop the strongest present
payoff, path-dependent residue. The projectibility section then states the
forward inferences the architecture generates and the evidence still needed
to establish their held-out success.

\section{The property cluster}\label{sec:cluster}

English interjections exhibit a cluster of co-occurring properties. This
section takes phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics in turn,
drawing primarily on the empirical base documented in \textcite{ameka1992},
\textcite{quirk1985}, and \textcite{HuddlestonPullum2002}. It answers two
questions: what properties cluster, and what local diagnostic inferences each
property suggests. Six properties emerge: prosodic isolation,
non-inflection, syntactic non-integration, non-referentiality, emotive or
stance force, and sequential use, the last being the capacity to constitute a
conversational move on its own. The three categories cut across those six
rather than regrouping them into domain-pure bundles, because each inquiry
preserves different contrasts and proposes different targets. The subscripts
are used from here on, with assignment criteria given in
Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence} where the diagnostic matrix needs them, and
the argument for treating the categories as three deferred to
Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes}. Section~\ref{sec:projectibility} asks
the stronger question: when does a partial observation license inference
across linguistic domains? The causal question, why these properties
travel together, is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure}.

\subsection{Phonology}\label{sec:phonology}

Interjections are prosodically isolated, and \textcite[108]{ameka1992}
states it without hedging: they're \enquote{always separated by a pause from
the other utterances with which they may co-occur} and \enquote{always
constitute an intonation unit by themselves}. I treat the property as
strongly characteristic rather than exceptionless, because the diagnostic
matrix below records partial cases (\mention{um}). That gap between a
categorical statement and its boundary cases is the pattern
Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence} takes up. In writing, this isolation surfaces
as the punctuation that sets supplements off from the rest of the sentence
(commas, dashes, parentheses; \citealp[1350]{HuddlestonPullum2002}).

The corpus evidence below is written, so prosodic isolation is tracked
through proxies: punctuation, standalone position, and absence of
integration into the host clause. These proxies are useful for historical
and large-corpus work, but they're proxies rather than direct acoustic
evidence. They aren't arbitrary ones.
On \textcite[41]{Jackendoff2025Parenthetical}'s account a parenthetical is
typically realized as an intonation phrase, so it ends in a boundary tone made
of \enquote{some combination of pause, lengthening, pitch discontinuity}.
Written commas mark the same breaks, which is why punctuation can stand in
for prosodic isolation.

Interjections may also contain phonemes outside the
speaker's dialect. The interjection \mention{uh-oh} ([\ipa{əʔo}]) is one of
the few cases where a glottal stop appears in dialects that otherwise lack
it. Other atypical sounds include the dental-alveolar clicks in
\mention{tut-tut} ([\ipa{ǀǀ}]; \citealp[147]{wilkins1992}), the voiceless bilabial fricative in
\mention{whew} ([\ipa{ɸɪu}]), and the voiceless velar fricative in
\mention{ugh} ([\ipa{ʌx}] or [\ipa{əx}]), though /x/ isn't a phoneme of
standard American or British English \citep[74, 853]{quirk1985}. Spelling
pronunciations arise for these forms (\mention{tut-tut} said
[\ipa{tʌt tʌt}], \mention{ugh} said [\ipa{ʌɡ}]; \citealp[853]{quirk1985}),
suggesting that the atypical phonemes are marked even for native speakers.

Prosodic isolation is a strong cue: if
a novel form occupies its own intonation unit and resists integration into
the surrounding prosodic contour, the network account proposes syntactic
non-integration and non-referentiality as targets. Their held-out relation to
that cue remains to be tested. Prosodic isolation isn't unique to
interjections, since supplements in general are \enquote{detached
prosodically} from what they attach to \citep[66]{HuddlestonPullum2002}. What
distinguishes interjections is the convergence of prosodic isolation with
the properties below.

\subsection{Morphology}

English interjections characteristically don't inflect or derive. They
resist the morphological operations that characterize other open-class
categories: no plural (*\mention{ouches}), no tense (*\mention{ouched}),
no comparative (*\mention{oucher}), no nominalization
(*\mention{ouchness}) \citep[106]{ameka1992}.\footnote{The stars mark the
interjection. Noun and verb uses of \mention{ouch} are attested in British
child-directed speech (\mention{It's a big ouch}; \mention{I think this
one's just ouched slightly more than you}; \citealp[36]{stange2016}), and
\mention{tut-tut} and
\mention{tsk-tsk} became verbs only once they were given ordinary word
pronunciations \citep[147]{wilkins1992}. Conversion produces a distinct
sign-type (Section~\ref{sec:intro}), and the noun and verb uses here fall
outside \intj{syn}; Section~\ref{sec:residues} treats such exits.} Since
\textcite{libert2019} notes that \enquote{few, if any, papers focus on
the morphology of interjections}, the generalization is weaker than it
looks. For inflection it's a claim about non-occurrence, informative only
where a noun or verb analysis would predict an inflected form, and this paper
doesn't count those occasions; resistance to derivation needs a comparison of
its own. The morphology-gain study (Appendix~\ref{app:morphgain}) documents
source meaning persisting in selected derivative uses and doesn't estimate
how often forms exit.

Some interjections retain remnants of earlier categorial membership.
\mention{Heavens} and \mention{bollocks} preserve the nominal plural
suffix. None of these remnants are productive. The plural in
\mention{heavens} doesn't contrast with a singular *\mention{heaven!} (as
an interjection), and speakers don't extend the pattern to new forms.

Non-inflection is associated with restrictions on ordinary argumenthood in
the recurrent profile. It doesn't entail them: adverbs also don't inflect
\citep[106]{ameka1992}, and invariance alone doesn't prevent an expression
from filling an argument slot. The stronger diagnostic is syntactic:
adverbs characteristically form constituents with other words, while
canonical interjections are syntactically isolated and resist integration
with neighbouring material \citep[152--153, 164]{meinard2015}.

\subsection{Syntax}\label{sec:syntax}

Interjections are used on their own or as \term{supplements}
\citep[1361]{HuddlestonPullum2002}, \enquote{elements which occupy a
position in linear sequence without being integrated into the syntactic
structure of the sentence} \citep[1350]{HuddlestonPullum2002}. In \mention{Damn, we're going to be late!}, the
interjection isn't a complement or modifier of anything in the clause.
It's appended to the beginning as a parenthetical.

Supplement function isn't unique to interjections. Relative clauses, noun
phrases, adjective phrases, and adverb phrases can all function as
supplements \citep[1356--1360]{HuddlestonPullum2002}:

\ea\label{ex:supplements}
  \ea \exmention{\exfocus{Ah}, so you were there after all!} \hfill (interjection, p.~1360)
  \ex \exmention{We called in to see Sue's parents, \exfocus{which made us rather late}.} \hfill (relative clause, p.~1356)
  \ex \exmention{A university lecturer, \exfocus{Dr Brown}, was arrested for the crime.} \hfill (noun phrase, p.~1357)
  \z
\z
What characteristically distinguishes interjection supplements is their
combination with the other cluster properties: non-referentiality,
non-inflection, prosodic isolation, and emotive force. The function is shared; the
category differs.

A handful of interjections exceptionally take complements. For
\mention{damn} in \mention{Damn these mosquitoes!},
\textcite[1361 n.~70]{HuddlestonPullum2002} suggest that \enquote{it may be
best to regard such words as exceptional interjections that combine with an
NP complement to form an interjection phrase} (IntjP). In English it's one of
the very few cases where an interjection heads a phrase larger than a single
word.
But even here the syntax is
constrained: no subject is overt, and there's no tense inflection. The
construction is compatible with a residue of verbal source syntax, a
reconstructive possibility that matters for the
recruitment pathways
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure}. A second kind of residue is
internal rather than external. \mention{Goddammit} preserves a
verb-object-pronoun structure inside what's now a single word, so the syntax
survives as fossilized material rather than as anything the form can still do.
\textcite[40]{cuenca2002} gives \mention{Goddammit} as her example of change
in function and meaning \enquote{accompanied by phonetic weakness and word
integration}. (The internal structure of
lexicalized pairs like
\mention{oh boy} and \mention{holy cow} is less clear and won't be
resolved here.)

Argument-taking needn't reflect inherited verbal syntax, though. In languages
of the Caucasus, clauses with \enquote{here, take it!} interjections often
include direct objects and recipients \citep[386, 397]{maisak2026}. The
% Verified 2026-11-02, maisak_2026 PDF p. 14 = p. 399 ("'take'-predicate"); the gate
% pairs the quote with the preceding citation's pages and reads the nested
% \enquote as braces, so it reports MISSING.
recipient is \enquote{not an argument expected in the syntactic frame of a
\enquote{take}-predicate} (p.~399), and Maisak relates it to the joint
giving-and-taking event the interjection signals rather than to a verb it
came from (pp.~399--400).

Supplement function predicts prosodic isolation and resistance to
syntactic integration:

\ea\label{ex:syntactic-predictions}
  \ea[*]{\exmention{I think that damn we're going to be late.}}
  \ex \exmention{I think that, damn, we're going to be late.}
  \ex[*]{\exmention{Damn and quickly, we left.}}
  \z
\z
The contrast in (\ref{ex:syntactic-predictions}) is prosodic rather than
syntactic: the first two differ only in whether \mention{damn} is set off.
The general version, for parentheticals of every kind, is
\textcite[33, 41--43]{Jackendoff2025Parenthetical}'s IP-Break Hypothesis:
placement tracks intonational-phrase breaks rather than syntactic
constituency, so a host admits a parenthetical where it independently
permits an IP break, provided the parenthetical is itself licensed in that
position. He gives a pair of hosts that are
\enquote{syntactically identical} but differ in where a parenthetical may go,
since \enquote{the difference in the parenthetical's position is a consequence
of the difference in position of the IP breaks in the host}
\citep[42]{Jackendoff2025Parenthetical}.

Supplement function also predicts a semantic consequence: if the form is
syntactically non-integrated, its contribution is independent of the
proposition expressed by the host clause. Remove the interjection and the
truth conditions don't change, the hallmark of non-truth-conditional
meaning \citep[cf.][166]{potts2007}.

\subsection{Semantics}\label{sec:semantics}

English interjections characteristically don't refer. Here,
\term{non-referentiality} means absence of ordinary entity/event
denotation and argumental construal while allowing semantic content.
Nouns and verbs typically pick out entities or events in the world.
Interjections \enquote{do not refer to anything, but simply express the
speaker's emotion or wish} \citep[57]{leech2006}: anger (\mention{damn}),
disgust (\mention{eww}, \mention{yuck}), surprise (\mention{wow}), regret
(\mention{alas}), pain (\mention{ouch}), realization (\mention{eureka}), to
give some of the usual labels.
Some interjections are more propositional than emotive: \mention{yes}
and \mention{no} affirm or deny propositions, and \mention{oh} can
signal recognition of new information rather than expressing feeling.

\textcite[159]{wierzbicka1992} argues that interjections have genuine
semantic content, decomposable via Natural Semantic Metalanguage. The
forms may have content, and \mention{yes} and \mention{no} may operate
on propositions, while still lacking ordinary entity/event denotation.

\textcite[119]{wilkins1992} presses harder, and his title states the
objection: interjections are deictics. On his account they \enquote{convey
complete propositions}, the \enquote{referential arguments in the
propositions conveyed by interjections are provided by context}, and so
interjections are \enquote{shifters (indexicals) by virtue of
being built semantically out of basic deictic elements} such as
\mention{I}, \mention{you}, \mention{now}, and \mention{here}. If that's
right, calling interjections non-referential looks like a straightforward
mistake.

The two claims are about different things, and Wilkins's own wording shows
it: the referential arguments are supplied by context, not by the form.
Non-referentiality, as I use it, is a claim about what a form contributes on
its own, so it leaves the contextual filling untouched. \mention{Ouch!} picks
out no thing or event, and resists being made definite, quantified over, or
used as an argument, whatever indexical structure its explication turns out to
contain. If anything Wilkins asks more of the semantics than I do, since he has
to say what it supplies for the filling to work at all, and the distributional
facts I rely on hold on his account as readily as on mine. What his account does show is that
\enquote{non-referential} is a poor name for the property, kept here only
because it's the established one.

Not everyone reads Wilkins that way. On the conceptualist analyses of
\textcite{wierzbicka1992}, \textcite{ameka1992} and Wilkins (the grouping is
\citealp[174]{wharton2000}'s), interjections encode substantial conceptual
structure. \textcite[211]{wharton2000} argues
from relevance theory that they encode considerably less, being
\enquote{partly natural and partly coded} and falling \enquote{at various
points along a continuum between showing and saying}. That
dispute doesn't need settling here. Neither Wilkins's deictic slots nor
Wharton's activated attitudinal concepts give \mention{ouch!} a thing or event
to pick out, so the distributional property holds on either analysis. But
Wharton's continuum is worth noting for a second reason. What varies along it
is how much of an interjection's import is coded rather than shown, which is a
gradient within the category and not a blurred edge to it
(Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence}).

\textcite[241]{cruz2009} carries the dispute further. He argues that the
heterogeneity of interjections, \enquote{with items incorporated from other
lexical categories}, together with the openness of the class, \enquote{could
indicate the existence of a continuum of more and less conceptual items}, and
that the conceptual ones encode \enquote{not full concepts, but some schematic
material requiring subsequent pragmatic adjustments}. What matters here isn't
the continuum, which Wharton already supplies, but its cause. Cruz derives
gradedness from recruitment out of other categories and from open-class
coinage, which are the mechanisms Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure} treats as
the cluster's sources.

Wierzbicka's explications characterize the use-conditional content that
\intj{sem} (Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes})
tracks. Non-referentiality is a cluster property rather than a
definition.

The contrast with nouns is sharpest where both share a lexical source.
\mention{Jesus} as a noun refers to a person; \mention{Jesus!} as an
interjection doesn't. In the interjection use, as
\textcite[88]{gehweiler2008} puts it, \mention{Jesus} \enquote{has lost
its propositional content, i.e.\ it is no longer used to refer and hence
no longer influences truth conditions}.
This semantic shift is part of the recruitment pathway from noun to
interjection (Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure}).

The projectibility here is mostly negative. A form that doesn't refer
resists being made definite, quantified over, or used as an argument:
*\mention{the damn!}, *\mention{every ouch}, *\mention{Damn surprised me},
and *\mention{There's damn}. These restrictions are non-trivial because
they distinguish interjections from nouns that have undergone only partial
bleaching. Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling} treats the same relation from
the causal side: non-referentiality helps keep the form out of
argument structure, which in turn supports prosodic isolation.

\subsection{Pragmatics}\label{sec:pragmatics}

Interjections typically carry exclamatory or imperative force: \mention{ouch!}
expresses pain, \mention{shh!} requests silence \citep[88, 853]{quirk1985}. But
the pragmatic range extends beyond exclamation. How far beyond is worth a
number. \textcite[18]{elffers2008} reports that in Hofstede's analysis of a
corpus of spoken Dutch, only 7\,\% of interjections
\enquote{turned out to fulfil an expressive illocutionary function},
against 52\,\% classed as expositive, which Hofstede's table glosses as
directive in the conversation. The corpus is Dutch rather than English, so the figure indicates
rather than establishes.\footnote{The nearest English figure is narrower. In a
  one-million-word subcorpus of the spoken British National Corpus,
  \textcite[85]{ljung2011} estimates that reactive uses \enquote{make up less
  than one-fifth} of 513 expletive interjections, all tokens of ten taboo
  items (p.~95). His other uses include conveying the speaker's attitude
  (p.~87), so for these items the figure shows that reaction to a stimulus is
  the minority use, not that expressive use is. His Table~4.3 counts 920
  interjectional tokens of the same ten items in what appears to be the same
  subcorpus (p.~81), and the text doesn't reconcile the two totals.} It points the same way as Dingemanse's interactional
focus \citep[259]{dingemanse2024}. The emotive characterization grammars lead
with covers the minority case. Interjections summon (\mention{hey}; the
greeting \mention{hello} sits at the boundary with routine formulae, see
Section~\ref{sec:boundaries}), mark agreement (\mention{yes},
\mention{yeah}, \mention{amen}), indicate understanding (\mention{oh}, \mention{uh-huh}),
and signal backchanneling, the listener's ongoing attention to a speaker's
turn (\citealp[868, 873--876]{norrick2009}; \citealp[265]{dingemanse2024}).

The most common interjections tagged in the Corpus of Global Web-Based
English \citep[GloWbE;][]{daviesfuchs2015} illustrate this pragmatic breadth: \mention{yes}, \mention{no},
\mention{oh}, \mention{yeah}, \mention{hi}, \mention{hey}, \mention{wow},
\mention{hello}, \mention{ah}, \mention{ha}. The list is dominated by
interactional items, forms whose primary function is to manage the
conversation rather than to contribute propositional content.
\textcite[171]{poggi2009} presses the other way. An interjection, on her
analysis, is a holophrastic signal whose equivalent isn't a word but
\enquote{a communicative act including the meaning of both a performative and
a propositional content}, so that \mention{ouch!} paraphrases as \enquote{I am
feeling pain}. The grain distinction from Section~\ref{sec:semantics} applies
again. Poggi's propositional content belongs to the act the interjection
performs, not to any clause it helps compose. Her contrast between holophrastic
and articulated signals says why. Nouns and verbs characteristically convey part of a
sentence, an interjection a whole one. Prenominal \mention{damn} in
\mention{the damn car}, a member of \intj{sem} outside \intj{syn}, doesn't. That explains the independence noted in
Section~\ref{sec:syntax}: a form contributes nothing to a host clause's truth
conditions if it isn't part of a clause at all. Holophrasis accounts for the
property rather than competing with it.
\textcite[257, 263]{dingemanse2024} estimates that, across 18 languages,
roughly one in seven conversational turns is an interjection. Interactional
tools dominate the most frequent cases: continuers like \mention{mm-hm} that
scaffold extended turns, repair initiators like \mention{huh?} that
\enquote{help people calibrate mutual understanding on the fly}, and
change-of-state tokens like \mention{oh} that \enquote{display knowledge as
it evolves in interaction} (\citealp[257]{dingemanse2024}; cf.\
\citealp[868, 870]{norrick2009}).\footnote{%
  \term{Token} does double duty in work of this kind, and I keep the senses
  apart. In \term{change-of-state token} and \term{response token} it's the
  interactional-linguistic sense: a minimal form deployed as a move in a
  sequence. Elsewhere I use it for the unit of analysis, a lexical item in a
  grammatical environment as against the lexeme that labels a family of
  historically related such items.}

The boundary with \term{routine formulae} is instructive.
\textcite[2--3]{coulmas1981} characterizes routine formulae as
% Verified 2026-11-02 against Coulmas 1981 itself (literature/
% coulmas_1981_conversational-routine-introduction.pdf, pp. 2--3). Ameka's
% reproduction (1992: 108) has "conventionalised" and "standard"; Heine
% (2023: 7) has "prepackaged". Both secondhand versions are inexact.
% check-quotes.py reports this MISSING only because the page break and the
% running head "Introduction 3" fall between "prepatterned" and "expressions".
\enquote{highly conventionalized prepatterned expressions whose occurrence
is tied to more or less standardized communication situations}.
\textcite[108--110]{ameka1992} argues that formulae such as \mention{bye},
\mention{sorry}, and \mention{thank you} differ from interjections in three
ways: they include addressees, they're predictable responses to social
conventions, and they're always speech acts rather than mere reflections of
the speaker's mental state. \textcite[142]{wilkins1992} counters that such
formulae meet his definition of interjection, and that being tied to
specific, very common situations and serving mainly to maintain social
relations makes them \enquote{a distinct pragmatic and semantic subtype of
interjections}, not a separate category.

The disagreement is field-relative in a causal-network sense. For a
grammar, routine formulae pattern near interjections because they share
standalone utterance status and pragmatic function. For speech-act analysis or
interactional pragmatics, they support different predictions: addressee
orientation, occurrence in standard social sequences, and conventional
felicity conditions. The same form can sit close to one node
for one inquiry and farther away for another, depending on which causal
relations and predictions the inquiry tracks.

Which projective declarations are available depends on what was observed. A
semantics-facing classification of a novel stance form may motivate targets in
unobserved grammatical or interactional environments. A routine-formula
classification may motivate addressee orientation or placement in a
conventional social sequence, provided those properties weren't used to
assign the case. The overlap explains why routine formulae sit at the
boundary; the divergence explains why the classifications needn't agree.
Section~\ref{sec:projectibility} fixes the source--target discipline.

\subsection{Co-occurrence, not necessity}\label{sec:cooccurrence}

These properties recur together in the descriptive literature, but not
necessarily. The
literature's own definitions hedge, on every criterion they state.
% Verified verbatim against Wilkins 1992: 124 (running head "124 D. P.
% Wilkins / Interjections as deictics"), his numbered definition (1).
% Note: Gehweiler's block quote at p. 73 pluralizes "construction"; the
% singular here follows Wilkins directly.
\textcite[124]{wilkins1992} defines an interjection as
\enquote{a conventional lexical form which (commonly and) conventionally
constitutes an utterance on its own, (typically) does not enter into
construction with other word classes, is (usually) monomorphemic, and
(generally) does not host inflectional or derivational morphemes}. Four
criteria, a parenthetical hedge on each, and
\textcite[73]{gehweiler2008} adopts the definition while calling those
criteria the class's \enquote{obligatory properties}. No single property
is necessary and sufficient.

One dissent belongs here, because it's the strongest case against that last
sentence. \textcite[287, 292]{libert2012} proposes exactly one criterion:
interjections have a \term{conversational valence} of~1, meaning they can be
used felicitously with no addressee present, where \mention{goodbye} requires
one and so has a valence of~2. If you stub your toe you can say
\mention{ouch!} alone. On this account interjections are
\enquote{a homogeneous word class, clearly delimited by a sole criterion}
\citep[294]{libert2012}.

The criterion works. What it costs is visible in how Libert reaches it, by
redefinition: interjections \enquote{as I have redefined them, have
only one function (as `expressions of feeling'); the function of `marks of
address' is undertaken by another class} \citep[294]{libert2012}. The
addressing uses are moved out of the category rather than shown to satisfy
the criterion, and they take a good deal with them: \mention{hey},
\mention{shh}, and \mention{psst}, and also the continuers, repair
initiators, and response tokens of Section~\ref{sec:pragmatics}, all of
which need someone to be there. A single property is necessary and
sufficient for what's left.

I take that as support for the decomposition proposed below rather than as a
counterexample to co-occurrence. Libert's line between expressions of
feeling and marks of address falls close to the line this paper draws
between \intj{sem} and
\intj{prag}, and he credits
\textcite[109]{ameka1992} with the same intuition while objecting, fairly,
that Ameka's gloss of conative interjections as \enquote{directed at} but
not \enquote{addressed to} people is obscure. So the disagreement isn't
about the facts. It's about what to do with them: Libert narrows the
category until one criterion suffices, and I keep the wider class and let
the properties co-occur without any one of them being definitional.
That isn't a bare preference. In this tradition lexemes fall into categories
\enquote{on the basis of their inflection, the kinds of dependent they take
and the function in larger constructions of the phrases they head}
\citep[30]{HuddlestonPullum2002}, several grammatical properties at once. A
single criterion would make interjection
the only category in the inventory picked out that way, which is a reason to
doubt the standard rather than the category.

Co-occurrence without necessity doesn't settle whether membership itself is
graded. The analysis needs a more local discipline. Each diagnostic has to be
assessed for a specified sign-type: complement-taking \mention{damn} either
takes an NP complement in the construction at issue or it doesn't, and
\mention{heavens} either carries the plural suffix or it doesn't.
Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic-matrix} records categorical analytical
decisions, with parentheses marking evidential qualification or a live
classification dispute. Nothing in the residue, turnover, or projectibility
arguments depends on a further metaphysical claim that every category
boundary is sharp.

The pattern calls for explanation. The causal-network proposal connects the
properties: prosodic isolation may reinforce syntactic non-integration,
non-referentiality reduces the motivation for argument structure, and
pragmatic conventionalization may entrench interactional functions.

The coupling is visible when it breaks. \mention{Damn} as an interjection
(\mention{Damn!}) has the full cluster: prosodic isolation, syntactic
non-integration, non-referentiality, and emotive force. Prenominal
\mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car} retains emotive force
\citep[cf.][171, 182]{potts2007}, and with it non-inflection and
non-referentiality, but loses prosodic isolation and syntactic
non-integration together, because it's embedded inside a noun phrase. Those two properties co-vary across this
contrast, as the proposed coupling link predicts
(Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}). A bare property list records the
contrast; a successful causal account would explain it. Two cases alone don't
establish the link.

Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic-matrix} makes the cluster inspectable.
Each row gives a conventionalized sign-type rather than a word family as a
whole (see Section~\ref{sec:intro}), and the columns show its profile against

Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes}. The table

These six recur across the sections above, but they aren't a complete
inventory: imitative coinage can supply new \intj{syn} sign-types, but
productivity belongs to the inventory rather than to any one sign-type, so it
has no column here. The last three columns
are separate classifications, not sums of the first six, so they need their own
criteria. A sign-type is
\intjshort{syn} if it functions as a supplement, taking no
complement and forming no constituent with neighbouring material;
\intjshort{sem} if its contribution is expressive and
use-conditional, leaving the truth conditions of its host untouched;
\intjshort{prag} if it's conventionally usable as a minimal interactional
resource in a specified sequential environment. This last criterion assigns
the category from observed source environments; projection to recipient
treatment or a withheld environment is a separate declaration.
The semantic criterion is about meaning type rather than position, which is
why a construction can satisfy it while sitting inside a host clause.
Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes} argues for the three-way division; here

\section{The causal-network framework}\label{sec:causal-framework}

The property cluster documented above has the shape
Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence} described: recurrent co-occurrence in the
descriptive literature, no necessary and sufficient conditions, disputed
boundaries, and hedged universals. Grammars have said little about the
class, the first edition of the student grammar finding nothing
% Verified 2026-11-02 in the PDF text of literature/huddleston_pullum_2005_
% students_introduction_english_grammar.pdf ("about which there really isn't
% anything interesting for a grammar to say"); check-quotes.py reads the .md
% conversion, which lacks the passage, and reports MISSING.
\enquote{interesting for a grammar to say} \citep[16]{HuddlestonPullum2005}.
It's tempting to conclude that a class with no definition behind it has
nothing to license, but the inference doesn't hold automatically.
A category may instead earn its place by supporting warranted projections.
The question is whether a causal-network analysis can specify such projections
for the three interjection categories without treating any of them as an
essence.

\subsection{The framework}

The relations that unify a kind may be local mutual-reinforcement
relations, one-directional pathways, or converging historical routes
\citep{khalidi2013,khalidi2018nodes}. Each of
the three has work to do below: the coupling links of
Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling} are mutual reinforcement, the
non-referentiality ratchet is one-directional, and the recruitment pathways of
Section~\ref{sec:recruitment} are converging historical routes. What matters is
whether a category marks a position where properties and processes are
connected tightly enough to support induction from observed to unobserved
properties. That's the proposal to be evaluated, not a consequence of calling
the relations a network.

I take the structure from Khalidi and not the metaphysics. Nothing below turns
on whether interjection is a kind in a philosopher's sense; what the category
has to do is support non-trivial, field-specific projections for linguistic
inquiry. The present evidence motivates several such declarations and bears
directly on recruitment and residue; it doesn't yet estimate the declarations'
held-out performance.

The four objects distinguished in Section~\ref{sec:intro} are easier to see
running on a single form. Take \mention{oof} in standalone use, as in
(\ref{ex:oof}).

\ea\label{ex:oof} \exmention{\exfocus{Oof}. That's going to hurt.}
\z

\begin{itemize}
\item A declaration built from restricted source evidence says this: having
  observed that the sign-type stands prosodically separate and doesn't
  inflect, expect it to resist determiners, to lack ordinary entity or event
  denotation, and to contribute use-conditionally rather than descriptively,
  in present-day English and in this use rather than across the whole word
  family.
\item The warrant is whatever supports that expectation. Part of it is
  analogy to \mention{ouch} and \mention{wow} and the links set out below.
  Negative evidence could add to it, from comparable uses, identified
  independently of the target, in which determiners, plurals, and tense forms
  fail to appear on occasions where a noun or verb analysis predicts them.
  This paper doesn't count those occasions. The same class supplies
  counterevidence. \mention{Ouch} turns up as a noun and a verb in
  child-directed speech \citep[36]{stange2016}, and \mention{wow},
  \mention{boo}, \mention{ooh}, and \mention{shoo} have inflected derivatives
  (Appendix~\ref{app:morphgain}). These converted uses fall outside the
  declaration because its scope is fixed by prosodic separation and
  non-inflection, independently of determiner resistance; a scope narrowed
  only after failure would be a new claim needing a new
  test \citep[16]{reynolds2026projectibilityHistory}. None of this evidence
  concerns \mention{oof} itself, which leaves its determiner behaviour as the
  held-out target.
\item The profile is the combination the declaration posits. Stability and
  network order are further and independent claims: that the combination
  recurs across forms and over time, and that the properties are linked
  rather than merely co-occurring. Neither follows from one form.
\item Grounds explain how the form got there and why it stays.
  \textcite{oed_oof} derives \mention{oof} as an imitative formation, which
  puts its entry on the onomatopoeic route of Section~\ref{sec:recruitment}
  rather than on either bleaching path. A stabilizer would be whatever keeps the
  combination together as forms enter and leave, and corrective control would be
  a process that restored it if something pushed \mention{oof} out.
\end{itemize}

The question is how strong a causal claim the descriptive co-occurrence in
Section~\ref{sec:cluster} supports. One proposed local relation connects
prosodic isolation and syntactic non-integration: a form
produced as its own intonation unit is less available for complementation
or modification, while a form outside clause structure is more likely to
receive independent prosodic packaging. \mention{Damn!} standing alone has
both properties; prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car} has
neither, because sitting inside a noun phrase costs it the intonational
boundary that accompanies the standalone use. Written-corpus proxies don't
establish either causal direction, so Table~\ref{tab:link-status} treats the
coupling as a candidate rather than a result.

Other proposed processes are interactional or directional. Uptake may affect
future use; repeated use may affect expectation; expectation may affect later
uptake. \mention{Mm-hm} illustrates the hypothesized loop: it serves as a
continuer when recipients treat it as returning the floor, and recurrent use
in that slot may entrench that affordance.
Loss of referential content makes argument structure less motivated;
syntactic deactivation pushes a form toward standalone use; repeated
conversational deployment entrenches stance, response, repair, or
sequence-management functions. These relations would explain why the
diagnostics travel together. The evidence below supports some of them,
motivates others, and leaves inventory-level maintenance open.

The historical evidence adds a further point. Semantic bleaching,
syntactic deactivation, and onomatopoeic or interactional coinage can all
recruit forms into a similar pragmatic profile, but they don't do so by
the same route or with the same residues. A bleached invocation (\mention{gee}), a
deactivated optative (\mention{damn}), and an onomatopoeic or
interactional coinage (\mention{oof}, \mention{mm-hm}) may
converge on prosodic independence, supplement function,
non-referential or use-conditional meaning, and interactional force while
retaining different traces of their sources. The result is a recurrent
cross-category configuration whose proposed causal relations have different
temporal and explanatory profiles.

If borne out, that structure would warrant the declarations in
Section~\ref{sec:projectibility}. The present evidence isn't enough to
establish their held-out success or corrective
control \citep[in the sense of][6]{reynolds2026notEveryStableCluster},
since it doesn't identify a tracked category-level variable or a response
pathway that restores the interjectional profile when it's perturbed
\citep[cf.][]{weinberger2026HomeostasisCausalControl}.

\subsection{Causal networks vs.\ prototype theory}\label{sec:prototype}

Prototype theory (\citealp{rosch1975}; \citealp[43--53]{taylor2003}) was developed for exactly
the kind of data Section~\ref{sec:cluster} documents, and it handles
that data well: graded membership, better-and-worse exemplars, and
fuzzy boundaries via overlapping similarity spaces. It also correctly
predicts that boundary disputes (interjections vs.\ fillers, vs.\
routine formulae) should recur instead of being resolvable by
definition. These are genuine explanatory achievements, and the
causal-network account doesn't replace them.

The accounts differ in what they buy that data with. Prototype accounts that
model the effects as degrees of membership buy it that way. The causal-network account doesn't need them,
because each field-relative cut is determinate and the disputes concern which
cut an inquiry should be using (Section~\ref{sec:boundaries}). That contrast
generates a test rather than a present result: apparent gradience should
decompose under the conditions that determine the relevant classification
rather than persist as irreducible fuzziness.

Determinate cuts are compatible with gradience inside a category.
\textcite[206]{aarts2007} keeps the two apart for adjectives:
\enquote{\mention{utter} is as much an adjective as \mention{thin} is; it is
merely less prototypically a member of that class}. His model is a compromise
between the discrete Aristotelian view and \enquote{an unconstricted
Sorites-style smooth cline with no clear transition-point between the
categories} (p.~225). I adopt it for each field-relative cut, though Aarts
draws his boundaries on morphosyntactic criteria alone (p.~225).

\textcite[5]{zimmerackermann2025} model centrality as a continuum instead,
with core and periphery as \enquote{end points of a continuum} and
deviations adding up: \enquote{A lexeme that exhibits several peripheral
features is further away from the centre than another that only deviates in
terms of a single aspect}. That puts deviations in syntax, morphology, and
meaning on one scale. On the account here the three memberships aren't
combined into one distance: prenominal \mention{damn} is a member of
\intj{sem} outright and simply outside \intj{syn}. Their own names example,
prototypical nouns from a syntactic perspective and peripheral ones from a
morphological perspective (p.~5), shows the same dependence on perspective
inside a single field.

A causal-network account adds a different kind of question. Prototype
theory explains why borderline cases should be expected: categories are
organized around better and worse exemplars. It doesn't explain why
the properties cluster in this particular pattern or what should happen
when one of their links is disrupted.

The difference matters because the causal-network account generates
predictions about change, conditioning, and residues. It predicts that
forms recruited by different pathways should retain different traces of
their source constructions, that appropriate conditioning may decompose some
apparent gradience, and that some properties may be more central to network
order than others (hub-property asymmetry). Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
finds unequal support: residue is the strongest probe, country conditioning
is more limited, and hub-property asymmetry remains open.

Pathway residues provide a further discriminator. If the proposed causal
links are real, messy boundary cases shouldn't be messy in arbitrary
ways. Complement-taking may point to a verbal source, subject to independent
history; euphemistic phonological deformation should point to taboo-managed
sources, of which invocation is only one (\mention{darn}, \mention{fudge},
and \mention{shoot} are deformed too; \citealp[306]{heine2023interactives});
regional concentration is compatible with regional or indexical entrenchment
but doesn't establish it. A synchronic prototype description supplies no
analogous retrodictive test tied to independently warranted histories.

Diachronic prototype and centre--periphery accounts do reason this way.
\textcite[8]{zimmerackermann2025} trace German nouns that lack genitive
\mention{-s} to loanword integration, and \textcite[48]{heine2023interactives}
explains why some interactives belong to two types by their relative degrees
of grammaticalization. Neither organizes its history around field-relative
classifications of the kind distinguished here.

\subsection{What causal structure adds}

Several analytical stances generate different predictions for
interjections. The weakest is a \term{property list}: interjections share
prosodic isolation, non-inflection, non-referentiality, and emotive
force. That's descriptively useful, but it makes no prediction about
what happens when one property changes.

Feature specifications are stronger alternatives, for example
[\textsc{+peripheral}, \textsc{-argument-taking},
\textsc{+expressive}]. This generates some of the same distributional
consequences: no inflection follows from the absence of phi-features,
and no argument-taking follows from the relevant syntactic feature
bundle. But feature specifications treat those consequences as
entailments of the feature bundle, not as effects of identifiable
processes.

A constructional account is a stronger competitor. It can represent
many of the same pairings of form, meaning, and use
\citep{goldberg1995constructions,goldberg2006,croft2001}. The
causal-network claim is compatible with that representational move. It
asks a different question: which positions in a constructional inventory
are projectible, why do their properties cohere, and which changes
should make them come apart? Construction grammar models the inventory;
the projectibility question asks where the inventory supports
cross-constructional and cross-field inference. The two main pressure
points are pathway residues and field-relative decomposition. If those
payoffs fail under further testing, the causal-network account collapses
to a descriptive inventory rather than an explanatory category analysis.

The causal-network account adds five claims that property lists, feature
bundles, and constructional representations don't settle by themselves:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Co-movement. Prosodic isolation, supplement function,
  non-referentiality, and pragmatic force recur in a patterned way. The
  account asks which links explain why observing one property licenses
  expectations about another.
\item Asymmetry. Some properties are upstream or more diagnostic than
  others. Prosodic packaging may make syntactic integration less
  available, while the reverse relation may have a different status. A flat
  feature bundle doesn't by itself represent that proposed ordering.
\item Persistence. A category profile may remain available while
  individual forms enter and leave. Inventory turnover is compatible with
  that stability claim but doesn't establish it.
\item Boundary behaviour. Different recruitment pathways should leave
  different residues at the category boundary. Feature theory can list
  residual properties but doesn't explain why they align with
  particular histories.
\item Failure. When a prediction fails, the pattern of failure should
  constrain the corresponding link or declaration. \mention{Fie}'s
  diagnostics decline over the same broad period without the predicted
  ordering. That result limits ordered erosion; it doesn't by itself identify
  simultaneous or community-wide attrition.
\end{enumerate}

If a feature-based account can replicate co-movement, asymmetry,
persistence, boundary behaviour, and failure without network order,
causal structure is doing no additional explanatory work. The empirical
pressure point is whether the probes in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
support those claims.

\subsection{Three field-relative categories}\label{sec:fieldrelative-nodes}

The causal-network account also explains why projectibility is
field-relative. The explanatory interests that categories serve

% check-quotes.py reports MISSING.
\enquote{diverge somewhat within different scientific disciplines and
subdisciplines} \citep[71]{khalidi2013}. If kinds are nodes in causal
structures \citep{khalidi2013,khalidi2018nodes}, different fields may track
different nodes because they ask different explanatory and predictive
questions. The resulting categories
need not be domain-pure or merely interest-relative. An inquiry fixes the
scope of a declaration; evidence and worldly organization determine whether
the resulting classification and projections succeed.

\textcite[3]{ponsonnet2025} takes a nominalist position: interjections
\enquote{are not a natural class that would exist prior to our defining
them}, and definitions are \enquote{more or less suited to a scientific goal
or another}. I agree that the goal picks the definition. But whether a
definition chosen to assemble a comparative dataset also supports projections
to unexamined cases is a question about the forms, and it needs evidence of
its own.

The proper-noun/proper-name distinction supplies a close linguistic
parallel. \term{Proper noun} is principally useful to grammar, while
\term{proper name} is principally useful to semantics, pragmatics, and
onomastics. Neither is domain-pure. Proper names have grammatical structure,
and proper nouns have conventional referential tendencies. Their extensions
still come apart: \mention{the University of Toronto} is a name whose head is
a common noun, while a proper noun can be used predicatively or
metalinguistically without straightforwardly naming its usual bearer. Neither
classification is a defective version of the other.

The interrogative/question distinction makes the same point across grammar
and interaction. Syntacticians track \term{interrogative}, a clause type with
properties such as subject--auxiliary inversion and fronting of interrogative
words. Pragmatists track \term{question}, an action that requests information,
makes an answer relevant, and organizes uptake. The two often coincide and
also come apart: \mention{Can you pass the salt?} is interrogative in form and
ordinarily a request in use.

Interjections create a similar problem with less terminological help.
Syntax, semantics, and interactional pragmatics all use the
word \enquote{interjection}, but each field tracks a slightly different
category: different contrasts are preserved and different projections are
at stake. The common bearer is the conventionalized sign-type defined in
Section~\ref{sec:intro}, so the three classifications apply to objects of the
same type. I distinguish them with subscripts:%

\item \intj{sem} is the semantics-facing category, overlapping with

  falling outside \intj{syn}.
\item \intj{prag} is the interaction-facing category, overlapping with
  what conversation analysts call response tokens

exclusive provenance of the categories' properties. Each classification may
depend on relations among form, grammar, meaning, use, convention, and
history. The three needn't be equally rich or symmetrical.

Three recent accounts put one classification, or one setting, first.
\textcite[1, 7--8]{heine2023interactives} argues that interjections belong to
a cross-linguistic \enquote{grammatical category of its own},
\term{interactives}, defined prototypically as \enquote{an invariable deictic
form that is in some way set off from the surrounding text semantically,
syntactically and prosodically and can neither be negated nor questioned}.
\textcite[159, 164]{mushin2023} argue that \enquote{at least some of these
formal and semantic criteria are inherently problematic, as they are built on
traditional models of grammar that require words to be assigned to classes,
syntax to be restricted to phrases and clauses}, and
propose integrating a grammar of conversational turns into descriptive
practice.
\textcite[4]{dingemanse2023preprint} puts the third view most briefly:
\enquote{The true home of interjections is in conversation}.

The subscript notation makes explicit that these are overlapping but
non-identical categories, each associated with different proposed projections
and causal relations. Bare \enquote{interjection} is a conventional cover term,
not a fourth category. Canonical sign-types belong to all three, but the
intersection has no standing or projective force beyond the component
classifications. The remaining sections analyze their relations rather than
trying to vindicate the overlap as another category.

A field gets a category here when it uses \enquote{interjection} for a class
whose extension departs from the others' and which carries proposed
projections of its own. History and phonology are the obvious candidates for
a fourth. Each of the three categories has a history, and the \mention{fie}
study in Section~\ref{sec:residues} tracks all three over the same period.
The paper's historical terms are these classifications applied at successive
dates. Ameka's secondary interjections, for instance, are \enquote{forms that
belong to other word classes} used as interjections \citep[105]{ameka1992},
a synchronic criterion, and the retrodictions of Section~\ref{sec:residues}
organize recruitment routes. Phonology picks out a marked stratum, but one
wider than the interjections: ideophones also stand out \enquote{in terms of
prosody, phonotactics and morphosyntax} \citep[3]{dingemanse2018}, and
\textcite[3]{dingemanse2023preprint} notes that \enquote{while some degree
of phonological or phonotactic deviance is common, it is not definitional of
interjections}. Neither analysis, as developed here, yields a further
classification with its own extension and projections. Prosodic isolation,
the phonological property that matters most here, belongs to \intj{syn}'s
packaging.

\subsection{Recruitment pathways}\label{sec:recruitment}

Section~\ref{sec:cluster} already pointed to three recruitment
pathways: semantic bleaching from nouns, syntactic deactivation from
verbs, and nonce coinage via onomatopoeia. The first is historically
demonstrated; the second and third are proposed pathways with clear
examples but without the extended diachronic documentation that Path~1
has. The first two are analysed in detail here; the third is taken up
in Section~\ref{sec:turnover}.

The pragmatic issue is how changes in communicative use become
categorially consequential: grammaticalization and pragmaticalization
matter here because they alter a form's communicative job as well as its
position in a syntactic tree
(cf.\ \citealp[26]{TraugottDasher2002}; \citealp[2--3]{heinekuteva2002lexicon}).

\subsection{Path 1: religious noun to interjection}\label{sec:path-religious}

\textcite{gehweiler2008} traces the development of \mention{gee} from
\mention{Jesus} through three positions:

\ea\label{ex:bleaching}
  \ea Proper name used as invocation (\mention{Jesus!} addressed to the
    deity)
  \ex Secondary interjection: exclamatory use, no longer addressing the
    deity (\mention{Jesus!} as an exclamation)
  \ex Primary interjection: clipping yields \mention{gee!}, which detaches
    from the source noun
  \z
\z
The two transitions don't work the same way, and Gehweiler is emphatic
about the difference. She explains the first as gradual and speaker-based,
through Traugott and Dasher's invited inferencing, though
\enquote{advanced by the syntactic isolation of \mention{Jesus}}, a factor
she counts as hearer-based \citep[84--85]{gehweiler2008}. The second she treats as abrupt and
hearer-based: tabooistic clipping produced \mention{gee!}, and she insists
that this step \enquote{was not gradual} and \enquote{can therefore not be
explained with reference to Invited Inferencing or syntactic position}
\citep[88]{gehweiler2008}.
The positions layer cluster properties unevenly. Prosodic isolation is
already present at the invocation, because a vocative like
\mention{Jesus!} occupies its own intonation unit (contrast the
integrated argument in \mention{Put your faith in Jesus}). What changes
is the content: non-referentiality arrives with the secondary interjection, when the speaker
is no longer addressing the deity. Phonological divergence then
makes the etymological connection opaque, leaving a standalone interjection
that many speakers don't connect to its source.
For the first transition the invocation is the gateway, and what it supplies
is syntactic: an utterance-initial slot set apart from the rest of the
utterance, which Gehweiler, together with invited inferencing, credits with
enabling the categorial change \citep[85]{gehweiler2008}. She denies that the same
explanation extends to the second.

That discontinuity is an asset for the path-dependence claim rather than
an embarrassment for it. A gradual slide would leave a form with no
particular mark of where it came from; an abrupt re-analysis leaves exactly
the kind of residue the account needs, since the phonological opacity of
\mention{gee!} is a permanent trace of the clipping that produced it. Once
the invocation has lost its referential force, the phonological connection
to the source is the last link to the nominal category, and the clipping
cuts it. Gehweiler's record fits an abrupt cut: \mention{gee!} is first
attested in 1895, and \enquote{there are no transitional, ambiguous uses
attested} \citep[85--86]{gehweiler2008}. Euphemistic forms of religious
names are much older; \mention{gog} for \mention{God} is attested from the
fourteenth century \citep[81]{gehweiler2008}.

Two cautions matter for the general claim. The boundary between
invocation and exclamation is hard to operationalize in historical
texts, since prosodic and interactional evidence is indirect. And
\mention{gee!} arose under Victorian taboo pressure
\citep[85--86]{gehweiler2008}, so external pressure may have done some of
the work that internal category pressures would otherwise be credited
with. The causal-network claim doesn't require Gehweiler's sequence
to be an autonomous internal cline. It requires the weaker and more
historically plausible claim that social pressure, invited inference,
and reanalysis can jointly push a source noun toward the same
interjectional profile.

Two processes operate within this path.
\textcite[71--72, 88]{gehweiler2008} distinguishes gradual Invited
Inferencing (speaker-based: the expressive use is conversationally
implicated, then conventionalized) from abrupt reanalysis (hearer-based:
once clipping had obscured the source, hearers no longer recognized
\mention{Jesus!} as the semantic base of \mention{gee!}).
Both feed the same cluster, but they operate at different timescales and
involve different agents. Their convergence shows that the same present-day
classification can be reached by different routes; it doesn't by itself
establish synchronic network order.

The first transition generates a specific prediction: partial bleaching
should be detectable by the absence of downstream properties. A form that
has reached the secondary interjection but no further should be
non-referential, retain the source phonology, and resist full prosodic
independence.
The prediction is testable and goes beyond historical description.

\subsection{Path 2: verb to interjection}

A second proposed trajectory enters through syntactic deactivation rather
than phonological modification. \mention{Damn} has a verbal source, and the
optative construction supplies a plausible gateway. Optatives include
\mention{God save the Queen} and \mention{God help you}, where a main-clause
subjunctive survives in a narrow range of formulaic frames
\citep[944]{HuddlestonPullum2002}. \mention{God damn him} has that shape, and
the wish interpretation of \mention{damn him} is compatible with reanalysis
after omission of the subject.

The proposed path is reconstructive rather than a demonstrated token-by-token
derivation. The optative already contains plain-form \mention{damn}, so no
tense morphology is lost in the proposed transition. What survives in
\mention{damn these mosquitoes!} is an NP complement compatible with verbal
source syntax. In \textit{CGEL}'s tentative synchronic analysis,
\mention{damn} here is an interjection taking an NP complement, forming an
interjection phrase rather than a VP \citep[1361 n.~70]{HuddlestonPullum2002},
so its complement-taking qualification has to remain visible.

If the reconstruction is right, the process is decategorialization involving
reduced verbal argument structure and increasing conventional expressive
force. As a verb, \mention{damn} contributes content involving condemnation.
Standalone \mention{damn!} conventionally displays a stance of frustration.
The present-day contrast and complement residue motivate the path, but a
fuller diachronic series would be needed to establish its sequence.

% Norde's general definition is in her chs 3--4, not held as of 2026-11-02;
% ch. 6 (pp. 186--227) is. Recheck "her cases start from grams" against them.
This doesn't count as degrammaticalization in Norde's sense, since the
changes she treats that way start from grams and bound morphemes
\citep[cf.][186]{norde2009}, whereas the source here is a lexical verb. Nor
does the form join \enquote{a major (inflected) word class}, a parameter she
scores separately \citep[192]{norde2009}. It's better treated as pragmatic
conventionalization with syntactic deactivation: the verbal source
loses argument-structural obligations as the interjectional use gains
procedural and interpersonal force.

For other verb-derived forms the imperative is a candidate gateway.
Imperatives characteristically lack an overt subject and can stand alone
prosodically. Discourse-marker uses of \mention{come on} and \mention{look}
show reduced argument-structural obligations in the relevant sign-types.
Those synchronic patterns are compatible with deactivation, but they don't by
themselves establish a historical series.

The proposed contrast generates a testable residue prediction: sign-types
that retain complementation should retain more source-compatible grammatical
behaviour than sign-types with no residual argument structure. The present
examples motivate that prediction; they don't yet establish a category-wide
correlation.

\subsection{Convergent pathways}

The two paths enter the cluster from different directions, one through
phonological modification of a bleached noun, the other through syntactic
deactivation of a verb, but both exploit a gateway construction that
already provides standalone or peripheral packaging: the invocation for
Path~1, and, on the reconstruction above, the optative or imperative for
Path~2. Both can yield similar present-day profiles. The documented strength
is asymmetric: the invocative history is established in substantially more
detail than the proposed verbal path.

Both paths can be situated within \textcite[40]{TraugottDasher2002}'s
subjectification trajectory (content~$>$~procedural,
nonsubjective~$>$~intersubjective), but they instantiate it differently.
Path~1 is grammaticalization in Traugott's technical sense: the invocation
carries content meaning (addressing an entity) plus subjective stance
(reverence); the secondary interjection loses content and gains procedural
meaning (expressive force); clipping then detaches the form from its source,
and hearers interpret it as a conventional expression, not an invocation.
Gehweiler finds \enquote{no further subjectification} in that last step,
since \mention{gee!} is \enquote{neither more nor less subjective} than
\mention{Jesus!}; only a phrase such as \mention{gee I wonder!} is more
intersubjective \citep[88--89]{gehweiler2008}. Path~2, as described above, is
decategorialization: loss of verbal morphosyntax with gain of expressive
force.

Onomatopoeia (Section~\ref{sec:turnover}) is categorically different.
Nonce coinages enter the cluster with no prior lexical history (no bleaching, no
deactivation, no subjectification). Three diachronically distinct
processes (grammaticalization, pragmaticalization, nonce coinage)
can yield sign-types assigned to one or more of the same synchronic
categories. Grammaticalization theory alone doesn't subsume all three. A
causal-network account offers a common representation of the recurrent
outcomes and retains the distinct routes. Whether all are linked by the
same synchronic ordering relations remains an empirical question.

\subsection{Synchronic coupling}\label{sec:synchronic-coupling}

Recruitment hypotheses concern how items enter the cluster. The synchronic
question is whether the cluster has network order now, and what might keep
that order stable. Four proposed links are relevant:
prosodic-syntactic coupling, a non-referentiality ratchet,
interactional routine entrenchment, and regional/indexical conditioning.
They don't have the same status. The first two order structural
and semantic diagnostics. The third is the central stabilizer candidate
for \intj{prag}. The fourth remains a
maintenance hypothesis.

\subsubsection{Prosodic isolation and syntactic non-integration}

These two properties reinforce each other, but the evidence is asymmetric.
Prosodic separation plausibly makes
syntactic integration harder, since a form in its own intonation unit is less
available as a complement or modifier. But the corpora used below are
written, and prosodic isolation is tracked there through proxies
(Section~\ref{sec:phonology}), so they can't establish that direction.

The diachronic record does support the syntactic half.
\textcite[85]{gehweiler2008} finds that invocational \mention{Jesus}
\enquote{typically stands utterance-initially and~-- like other
interjections~-- set apart from the rest of the utterance, or even on its
own}, and argues that \enquote{only because of this syntactic isolation of
the proper name~-- and Invited Inferencing, of course~-- could the secondary
interjection \mention{Jesus!} develop from the proper name}. That's
evidence that syntactic isolation, together with invited inferencing, enabled
a categorial change, not that prosody enabled the syntax. She also limits it: as Section~\ref{sec:path-religious} noted, she takes the
step from \mention{Jesus!} to \mention{gee!} to lie outside both invited
inferencing and syntactic position \citep[88]{gehweiler2008}.

The same coupling is visible synchronically. Standalone
\mention{Damn!} has both properties: it occupies its own intonation unit
and has no role in clause structure. In \mention{I think that, damn,
we're going to be late}, \mention{damn} remains possible only as a
parenthetical supplement, set off from the \mention{that}-clause. In
\mention{the damn car}, the form loses both properties at once: it's
inside the noun phrase and receives the prosody of the phrase.

\textcite{dingemanseakita2017} explain a similar co-variation in ideophones by
a common cause instead. Because both properties are often cited as defining
features of ideophones, treating their co-occurrence as explaining the
relation \enquote{would border on tautology}, so they derive both from a mode
of representation that depicts rather than describes (p.~526), and propose a
generalized version for interjections (p.~529). Their cases look like
\mention{the damn car}: in Bambara, an ideophone in non-final position loses
its high intonation and its status as an expressive adverb together
(p.~506), which they read as a change in how depictive meaning is realized.
Standalone and prenominal \mention{damn} both index the speaker's attitude,
but their generalized account concerns how indexical and descriptive material
are combined in one speech stream, and the two constructions differ in
exactly that. So \mention{damn} doesn't yet decide between a direct link and
a common cause.

The prediction is concrete. If a novel form is reliably
packaged as its own intonation unit, it should resist complementation,
modification by ordinary phrase-internal material, and coordination with
integrated phrases. If it's pulled into a host phrase, prosodic isolation should
weaken together with \intj{syn} status.

\subsubsection{The non-referentiality ratchet}

Bleaching removes referential content, which weakens the semantic
motivation for argument structure. A form with no entity or event to
construe is less available as an argument, complement, or modifier. Once
that syntactic role is lost, prosodic isolation becomes easier to keep
in place. The result is a one-directional cascade. Once argument
structure is lost, the form occupies a different functional niche, so
ordinary pressures toward referential syntax are reduced.

This ratchet supports a different set of predictions from the
prosodic-syntactic link. A non-referential stance form should resist
definiteness, quantification, and argument position:
*\mention{the damn!}, *\mention{every ouch}, *\mention{Damn surprised
me}. It should also resist embedding as propositional content
(\mention{*She said that damn}) unless it's quoted as a form. If
referential content becomes active again, the form should move toward
noun, verb, or routine-formula regions of the network. The ratchet
keeps the form near the interjectional region by blocking return
to the source category, while prosodic-syntactic coupling links the
central structural properties.

\subsubsection{Interactional routine entrenchment}

The interaction-facing category needs a candidate stabilizer of its own.
Conversation repeatedly places interjectional forms in recognizable
sequential slots: continuers, repair initiators, change-of-state tokens,
confirming answers to polar questions, where 81\,\% of confirmations are
interjections \citep[195]{stivers2019}, and stance displays
\citep[189--190]{dingemanse2020}. \textcite[257--258]{dingemanse2024}
argues that the first three are the \enquote{real workhorses} of the class,
central rather than peripheral for high-frequency interjections.

Repeated deployment in these slots favours a particular package of
properties. Two levels are in play here, and the argument needs both. On a
given occasion, a continuer such as \mention{mm-hm} works because it can be
recognized without taking over the turn or adding propositional content.
A change-of-state token such as \mention{oh} works because it can mark
an information update before the speaker builds further at-issue
content. A stance token such as \mention{bruh} works because it can
display evaluation while leaving the surrounding proposition untouched.
Across many occasions, that occasion-level fitness is supposed to accumulate,
so that the interactional routine favours short, standalone, prosodically
independent, non-referential material in the inventory at large. The
accumulation is an assumption here rather than a finding. The probes in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} bear on the inventory-level pattern, not on the
step from one level to the other.

If borne out, this candidate stabilizer would support the projectibility of
\intj{prag}. If a novel form is recruited as a
minimal stance, response, repair, or backchannel resource, one should
expect turn-initial or standalone availability, independent prosodic
packaging, resistance to embedding under reported speech, and
availability for uptake as an interactional move. The same candidate
could also explain why the function can persist when category membership
weakens: a full clause such as \mention{I agree} can do agreement work,
but it lacks the reduced, non-propositional packaging that makes
\mention{mm-hm} a central \intj{prag} case.

\subsubsection{Regional/indexical conditioning}

Some interjections are sharply conditioned by region, register, or
community, and some may also do identity work. \mention{Lah} in
Singapore and Malaysia, \mention{yaar} in India and Pakistan,
\mention{haba} in Nigeria, and \mention{bruh}, which \textcite[8]{louf2023}
find among vocabulary associated with African American English, are
plausible cases.

But they aren't the same kind of case, and the difference matters for
which node the conditioning operates on. \mention{Haba} is free-standing:
\textcite{unuabonahdaniel2020} avoid the terms utterance-initial and
utterance-final for it and its Nigerian congeners \enquote{because they are
free standing and are far more detached from the utterances than PMs}
\citep[69]{unuabonahdaniel2020}. \mention{Lah} is clause-final and
prosodically dependent on its host, and this holds on either current account of
it: \textcite[447]{lim2007} reports that Gupta takes the particles to
\enquote{function as the carrier of the intonation contour of the utterance},
while the rival analysis gives them lexically-specified tone inherited from a
substrate. That rival is weakest for \mention{lah} in particular, since
\mention{lah} is one of the few particles \textcite[463]{lim2007} doesn't
match to Cantonese, and among its candidate sources the Hokkien particles
have \enquote{neutral tone and weak stress} while Bazaar Malay particles
\enquote{carry no tone} \citep[462--463]{lim2007}. Either way \mention{lah} fails
prosodic isolation, so it satisfies \intj{sem} and \intj{prag} without
\intj{syn} (Figure~\ref{fig:node-overlap}). Regional conditioning can
in principle recruit a sign-type to the interaction-facing category without
recruiting it to the grammar-facing one, a pattern compatible with
\mention{lah} but not \mention{haba}. The GloWbE evidence in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, modelled in
Appendix~\ref{app:conditioning}, shows additional country-level variation in
overall item frequencies. It
doesn't by itself establish social indexing in the stronger
sociolinguistic sense: uptake, stance, enregisterment, style shifting,
metapragmatic awareness, or distribution by speaker group within
comparable contexts.

Regional/indexical conditioning is a maintenance hypothesis.
The open question is whether it stabilizes the cluster profile (property
co-occurrence) or individual items (inventory persistence). If it
stabilizes the profile, regional/indexical conditioning helps constitute
the node. If it stabilizes only the item, it operates as a
lexical-persistence process alongside the interjection cluster.

When a regional interjection is adopted by out-group speakers as ironic
quotation, the causal-network account predicts a contrast between
inventory persistence and profile stabilization. If out-group
\mention{lah} loses prosodic isolation or emotive force while retaining
its social-marking function, regional/indexical conditioning stabilizes
the inventory alone. If it retains the full profile, the link is part of
the causal structure of the category. This comparison remains for future
work.

The account proposes two structural ordering links
(prosodic-syntactic coupling and the non-referentiality ratchet), one
interactional-pragmatic stabilizer candidate (routine entrenchment), and
one regional/indexical maintenance hypothesis. If correct, the first three
would help explain why prosodic, syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic
diagnostics travel together synchronically. Their unequal evidential status
has to remain visible in the probes and conclusion.

\subsection{Causal residues at the category boundary}\label{sec:residues}

The proposed interjection network is a convergence region, but convergence
needn't erase history. Forms recruited through different pathways may share
parts of a present-day profile while retaining traces of their source
constructions. Where the history is independently documented, those traces
can distinguish pathway signatures from otherwise unexplained irregularity.

This supplies a second kind of proposed inference. Section~\ref{sec:projectibility}
states forward declarations from observed to unobserved properties.
Historical pragmatics also permits a retrodictive counterpart: a residual
property can warrant an inference about its source pathway when the historical
evidence is independent rather than reconstructed from the same residue. A
causal-network account represents that relation explicitly.

failures should align with independently documented historical routes.

network, so two independent routes reach the claim that unfinished recruitment
leaves syntactic traces.

\subsection{Inventory turnover}\label{sec:turnover}

Pathway residues bear on how interjectional forms entered the network. Turnover
asks the complementary descriptive question: how does the lexical inventory
change? Inventory replacement is compatible with a persistent category
position, but it doesn't by itself establish profile stability or
projectibility.

The Corpus of Historical American English (COHA) illustrates this
turnover. In the 1820s the six most frequent interjection lemmas are
\mention{no}, \mention{oh}, \mention{yes}, \mention{nay}, \mention{o},
and \mention{ah}. By the 2010s half that list has gone: \mention{nay},
\mention{o}, and \mention{ah} are replaced by \mention{yeah},
\mention{hey}, and \mention{uh}, while \mention{no}, \mention{oh}, and
\mention{yes} hold their places throughout (Figure~\ref{fig:turnover}).
\mention{Nay} falls from 175 tokens per million to 2.2; \mention{yeah},
absent from the 1820s altogether, rises from nothing to 723.%

countries]{daviesfuchs2015} shows that candidate interjectional forms
are distributed unevenly across country-labelled web sections, with \mention{lah} almost
exclusively in Singapore and Malaysia, \mention{yaar} in India and
Pakistan, and \mention{haba} in Nigeria. The raw-string concentrations
support a descriptive geographic claim, subject to the measurement limits in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}; they don't establish sociolinguistic
conditioning.

Onomatopoeic coinage is also a proposed source of new interjectional
sign-types. Two
clarifications, because \term{onomatopoeia} is often handled as though it
named a category alongside noun and verb. It doesn't. It names a relation
between a form and a sound, and forms standing in that relation are spread
across categories: \mention{bang} is a noun in \mention{a loud bang}, a
verb in \mention{he banged the door}, and an interjection in
\mention{Bang! You're dead}. So the claim isn't that a category of
onomatopoeias gets absorbed into the interjections. It's that imitation is
one of the routes by which new forms arrive at the interjectional profile.

Nor is the route closed in principle. The stock of imitable sounds isn't fixed by the
stock of animals: devices supply \mention{beep}, \mention{ping}, and
\mention{whirr}, and any sound can be re-rendered, which is how comics gave
English \mention{bam} and \mention{pow} beside older forms. Interjections,
on one view, form a relatively open class because they \enquote{can be
rather freely created by onomatopoeia}, as in comic-strip \mention{yucck}
and \mention{gr-r-r-r} \citep[74]{quirk1985}.

Research on ideophones draws the line elsewhere.
\textcite[3, 19]{dingemanse2018} treats onomatopoeia as the subset of
ideophones that imitate sound, words that \enquote{depict, using the iconic
affordances of speech}, and \textcite[529]{dingemanseakita2017} set
interjections apart as \enquote{primarily indexical} rather than depictive.
\textcite[150, 167]{meinard2015} concludes that onomatopoeias aren't
interjections at all: \enquote{Isolated onomatopoeias focus on an object of
conceptualization and fulfill the referential function of language}, whereas
interjections are about the speaker and serve conative, phatic, or expressive
functions.

Standalone \mention{Bang!} meets the distributional criterion for
\intj{syn}, and \textit{CGEL} has no ideophone category; ideophones are, in
Diffloth's assessment, \enquote{conspicuously undeveloped and poorly
structured in the languages of Europe} (Diffloth 1972, as cited in
\citealp[12]{dingemanse2018}). But \mention{Bang!} depicts a sound rather
than displaying a stance, so it falls outside \intj{sem}. A state-display use
like \mention{oof} is a candidate for \intj{sem}, as \mention{ouch} is.
Whether a coinage depicts or displays bears on its semantic classification,
though the use-conditional diagnostics still have to be checked. Either way,
direct imitative coinage can supply \intj{syn} sign-types without an
intervening noun or verb stage.

Interactional recruitment is another candidate route, and it generates a
testable prediction: a newly conventionalized interjectional
use may acquire several parts of the recurrent profile without traversing the
documented noun-to-interjection or proposed verb-to-interjection paths. Take
\mention{bruh}. \textcite{oed_bruh} records it as a noun from 1894, as a
title before a man's name, and from 1967 as a familiar form of address, where
every quotation is vocative (\mention{Hey, bruh}, \mention{Be cool, bruh}). The
standalone use addressed to nobody is later, and the entry doesn't record it as
an interjection at all. Its rise shows up in COCA, from 0.01 per million in
1990--94 to 1.09 in 2015--19.\footnote{Tokens sit overwhelmingly in television
  and film (1.44 per million) and are absent from the spoken section, so the
  record is scripted representation of speech rather than recorded speech. COHA
  has the form absent before the 1980s and nine tokens in all. Figures are raw
  string frequencies; see \path{analysis/hand-extracted/}.} That use is
prosodically isolated, non-inflecting, non-referential, and syntactically
non-integrated, even though the same word family includes the nominal lexemes
just described. It illustrates a candidate recruitment case; the corpus counts
alone don't establish persistence of a category position.

\textcite[191]{dingemanse2020} calls signs that equivocate between sound and
speech, such as sighs, sniffs, and clicks, liminal signs, and treats their
in-between status as \enquote{an essential part of their form and function}.
Liminal signs also feed the inventory: they \enquote{may develop into
full-blown interjections as they become increasingly conventionalised and
on-record}, which \textcite[12]{dingemanse2023preprint} calls \enquote{one
source path for interjections}, the route Table~\ref{tab:link-status} lists as
liminal coinage.
Cultural evolutionary processes \enquote{sample
the landscape of possible resources} \citep[191]{dingemanse2020}. The
causal-network proposal describes that landscape as a property space whose
structure may reflect the recruitment pathways and synchronic links above.

The descriptive cluster, recruitment evidence, and candidate links now make
the paper's inferential question precise: what should partial evidence
license, and what would establish that it does?

\section[Projectibility]{Projectibility as a testable hypothesis}\label{sec:projectibility}

Section~\ref{sec:residues} treated retrodictive inference from residue to an
independently supported recruitment history. Forward projectibility has a
different direction: from a restricted set of observations about a sign-type
to a property or environment not yet observed. This section states those
declarations and their failure conditions. It doesn't report a direct
held-out evaluation.

The distinction matters because three questions can otherwise collapse. A
category may be independently well motivated; a causal account may explain
some of its internal relations; and its classification may still fail to
improve prediction of new cases. Conversely, failure of one declaration
wouldn't abolish all three categories. The relevant claims have to be tested
and demoted separately.

\subsection{Partial-observation inference}\label{sec:proj-partial}

Projectibility becomes circular if it says only this: if X is an
interjection, it has interjection properties. That's trivially true of
any category. A legitimate declaration fixes the source evidence for a
particular case and withholds its target. The target can belong to the
category's broader individuation profile, but it can't be one of the
observations used to classify that case.

Consider the recently conventionalized standalone use of \mention{bruh}.
Standalone position, prosodic separation, morphological bareness, and stance
meaning are useful observations, but they can't all be counted first as
admission evidence and then as predictions. A declaration has to specify which
classification was independently assigned, which observations supported that
assignment in this case, and which new environment or property is withheld.
Different declarations may use different subsets of the same
category's wider diagnostic profile.

The category label adds no information after the complete vector from which it
was deterministically computed. That isn't a sensible test of category
utility. The relevant comparison asks whether a category- or network-structured
representation generalizes from restricted, noisy, or missing evidence to
independently measured targets better than an appropriate weaker
representation.

Analyst and participant claims also have to be separated.
\term{Category-driven projection} is the analyst's epistemic use of a
classification. \term{Cue-driven inference} would be a claim about what
speakers or hearers anticipate from partial signals. The category label need
not occur in a participant's psychology or cause the target behaviour. The
present paper specifies analyst-facing declarations. Whether participants
make corresponding online inferences requires separate psycholinguistic or
interactional evidence and remains open.

inference, not as rival definitions. Table~\ref{tab:declarations} fixes one
non-trivial declaration for each. The source column identifies the restricted

These declarations need not be equally strong. Many \intj{syn} targets may
follow almost directly from observed morphosyntax and be available to any
framework that represents the component properties. The semantic and
interactional targets reach farther, but that makes their empirical burden
greater rather than their success automatic.

Cross-category declarations are possible too: independently assigned
\intj{syn} may warrant an expectation about \intj{sem} or
\intj{prag}, and conversely. There are six directed relations, they needn't be
symmetrical, and the paper doesn't assume that all succeed. Each retained edge
requires source evidence that excludes its target and a failure rule. The
three-way overlap adds nothing further: canonical co-membership merely makes
the successful component projections jointly available. That claim has a
defeat condition of its own. If canonical members showed a held-out
projection beyond what the three component classifications jointly support,
the claim of projective redundancy would fail, and the intersection would
become a candidate category in its own right. \textcite[557]{krajinovic2025}
make a related demand for explanatory surplus: a proposed aspectual category,
the iamitive, may be warranted if, in some language, none of the
language-internal mechanisms they identify can explain a marker's new
meanings.

The three constructions of \mention{damn} show the same point inside a
single word family.

Standalone
\mention{damn!} belongs to all three categories: the form is prosodically
isolated, syntactically non-integrated, use-conditional, and available
as a stance marker. For \mention{damn these mosquitoes!}, the picture
is mixed: its \intj{syn} classification is qualified because
\textit{CGEL} tentatively treats it as an exceptional interjection heading an
IntjP with an NP complement;
\intj{sem} holds because the meaning is expressive. Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic-matrix}
doesn't assign \intj{prag} because this sign-type isn't evidenced as a
minimal backchannel or turn-management resource.

For prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}, the categories
diverge further: \intj{syn} fails entirely (the
form is syntactically integrated inside a noun phrase), but
\intj{sem} holds (use-conditional, perspective-dependent). Neither
\textit{CGEL}'s syntax nor Potts's semantics needs to reproduce the other's
extension. The three-category analysis represents the divergence without
forcing a single verdict across inquiries.

\subsection{What would establish the forward claim}

A direct evaluation should hold out an entire lexical form family or
constructional family, not random tokens from an already familiar
item--variety combination. Source classifications have to be assigned without
access to target outcomes. The comparison isn't category membership added
after its complete diagnostic vector. The test instead compares structured generalization from
restricted evidence with a category-free local-cue model of
comparable complexity and with the target base rate. Targets that are
absences, such as no determiner or no complement, need their own
denominator. They're scored only on occasions where a rival analysis would
predict the form, and the base rate is computed over those occasions;
otherwise a form that never meets the occasion passes by default.

For interactional targets, a spoken corpus can supply independently measured
next-turn response, recipient uptake, and floor-transfer trajectories. For
semantic repeatability, a separate judgement or production design may be
needed. Evaluation should be paired on the same held-out families and assess
both discrimination and calibration. No useful held-out advantage, serious
miscalibration, or systematic failure among supposedly central members would
defeat the corresponding declaration. Until such a study is completed, the
forward projectibility claim remains prospective.

\subsection{Category vs.\ function}

\textit{CGEL}'s distinction between category and function sharpens the
projectibility test: what should the \intj{syn} classification add
beyond the supplement function label? Supplements of any category
(noun phrases [NPs], adjective phrases [AdjPs], relative clauses) share
prosodic isolation and syntactic
non-integration. The \intj{syn} account declares a wider package:

\begin{itemize}
\item Non-referentiality. Supplements can be referential
  (\mention{a university lecturer, Dr Brown, \ldots}); interjections
  characteristically can't.
\item Emotive/interpersonal force. Not all supplements express
  stance (\mention{Dr Brown} expresses none); interjections
  characteristically do (\mention{ugh}, \mention{yay}).
\item Free coinage. Onomatopoeia supplies a productive route into some
  interjectional sign-types. Comics gave English \mention{bam} and
  \mention{pow}; the same route isn't characteristic of relative clauses or
  ordinary NP supplements.
\item Non-inflection. NP supplements inflect freely
  (\mention{a lecturer}, \mention{two lecturers}); interjections don't
  (*\mention{ouches}).
\end{itemize}

The function label tells us about syntactic integration. The category
classification proposes a wider family of inferences. The category/function distinction matters
beyond the \textit{CGEL} tradition: any framework that distinguishes what kind of
thing an expression is from what job it's doing in a particular clause
will face the same question. Whether the classification earns its keep
requires the held-out comparison described above; the component contrasts
motivate that test but don't substitute for it.

\subsection{What sets the domain of projection}\label{sec:proj-domain}

Categories don't all project in the same domain, and what fixes the domain
should be where the relevant dependencies run. Nouns offer familiar
morphosyntactic examples: \mention{lecturer} takes a determiner, inflects for
number, and fills an argument slot. The three interjection categories instead
divide the proposed grammatical, semantic, and interactional target families
among themselves.

The interactional literature motivates the hypothesis that
\intj{prag} will support the richest projections.
\textcite[257--259, 263]{dingemanse2024} argues from corpus data that frequent, functionally central
interjections include continuers, repair initiators, and change-of-state
tokens rather than only emotive exclamations. That makes sequential position,
floor transfer, and recipient treatment natural targets. It doesn't show
that \intj{prag} classification predicts held-out outcomes better than the
local cues from which it was assigned.

\mention{Oh} illustrates the proposed asymmetry. Its \intj{syn}
classification concerns packaging and distribution; its \intj{prag}
classification concerns change-of-state and response uses. The same sign-type
can make two projective families available. Its co-membership
doesn't create a third source of inference.

The contrast with nouns and verbs concerns where the density lies, not how
much structure each node carries.
\intj{syn} is thin next to those nodes, lacking
the network of agreement, argument realization, derivation, and inflectional
behaviour associated with major lexical categories. The empirical question is
whether its thinner distributional package still generalizes usefully to
withheld frames.

Why might interactional targets be richest? Because routine entrenchment, if
it operates as proposed in Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}, connects
short, standalone forms with recurrent sequential jobs. Prosodic--syntactic
coupling instead motivates grammatical packaging targets. Regional or
indexical conditioning may explain uneven inventory persistence but remains a
maintenance hypothesis. These differently graded links motivate projections
in different domains; they don't establish their accuracy.

This connects to the content/procedural distinction. The central
meaning hypothesis for many interjectional sign-types is procedural rather
than denotational \citep[cf.][10, 40]{TraugottDasher2002}, with expressive
content of the kind \textcite[166--167]{potts2007} describes. The
\intj{sem} category isolates that meaning contribution and makes immediacy
and repeatability explicit targets. The same general relation between causal
domain and projective domain appears elsewhere. \term{Animacy} projects grammatically (argument realization,
reference tracking); \term{colour} projects ecologically (edibility,
toxicity) but usually doesn't project grammatically in the way animacy
can \citep[cf.][726]{seifart2010}.

The relative richness of the three projective families is a result
for future comparison, not a premise. Nor does \intj{syn}'s grammatical
standing depend on winning that comparison: its independent distributional
motivation can survive failure of a proposed cross-domain edge. What would
fail is the stronger claim that this classification earns additional standing
through forward projectibility.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Empirical probes and boundary conditions}

Section~\ref{sec:projectibility} stated forward declarations whose held-out
success remains prospective. The existing corpus and historical probes bear
on different claims: recruitment, residue, country-level frequency variation,
inventory turnover, and failure. They don't jointly constitute a direct test
of projectibility or corrective control.

The strongest probe is pathway residue. If interjections are recruited by
different causal pathways, residual properties should line up with
recruitment history. The defeat condition is random distribution:
retained complements, euphemistic deformation, atypical phonology, and
regional skew should show no relation to source pathways. The evidence
is uneven. Gehweiler's \mention{gee} history documents an invocative route
and its euphemistic phonological residue. Complement-taking \mention{damn} is
compatible with a verbal or optative source, but the sequence is
reconstructive. The preregistered derivative study adds a narrower exit
result: selected \mention{wow}, \mention{boo}, \mention{ooh}, and
\mention{shoo} derivatives retain source-interjection semantics in their
included VP-head uses. Together these findings support path dependence more
strongly than they establish a category-wide causal network.

A second probe asks whether country contributes variation to item frequencies.
It's weaker than testing whether conditioning decomposes a category
boundary, which would require credible item-type-by-country comparisons. A
per-million rate also doesn't separate grammatical availability from
preference or from how often suitable occasions arise, so a country
difference in rate leaves differences in category boundaries unresolved.
GloWbE frequency data \citep{daviesfuchs2015} provide the narrower probe: for
the 10 most frequent interjection-tagged items plus regional and boundary
items, frequency per million was extracted by country (20 countries), and the
coefficient of variation (CV; standard deviation divided by the mean) was
computed across countries.

The descriptive counts show marked geographic concentration. Regional
interjections (\mention{lah}, \mention{yaar}, \mention{haba}) are
highly concentrated: \mention{lah} occurs at 9.35 per million in Malaysia
and 6.72 in Singapore but near zero elsewhere; \mention{yaar} at 2.88
in Pakistan and 2.33 in India but near zero elsewhere; \mention{haba}
has 373 raw-string hits in Nigeria, an upper bound of about 9 per million,
and is near zero elsewhere. Filtering by hand and counting five spellings,
\textcite[69]{unuabonahdaniel2020} report 248 tokens, 5.8 per million.

Core items also vary: \mention{ah} has the highest
CV among core items (0.71), with Singapore (60.42) and Jamaica (53.16)
at eight times the rate of Kenya (7.62). Even broadly distributed items
like \mention{yeah} (CV 0.55) show a 6:1 ratio between the United
States (103.78) and Tanzania (16.85). Only \mention{yes} and
\mention{no} approach uniformity (CVs of 0.21--0.22). Boundary items
vary too: the fillers \mention{um} (CV 0.61) and
\mention{uh} (CV 0.76) and the routine formula \mention{bye} (CV 0.61)
all show geographic variation.

These coefficients are estimated from twenty countries, so they carry more
uncertainty than a bare figure suggests. Bootstrapping over the twenty
sections identifies which comparisons survive it. The
uniformity of \mention{yes} and \mention{no} is the firm result: their
intervals sit below eleven of the thirteen other core items, overlapping only
\mention{hello} and \mention{ha}. The ordering within the rest of the core
band isn't a result at all. No two of those items have separable intervals,
so \mention{uh} at 0.76 isn't distinguishable from \mention{yeah} at 0.55,
and the figures should be read as a single undifferentiated band rather than
a ranking. For the regional items the coefficient is the wrong summary
altogether: with a form absent from eighteen of twenty sections the mean sits
near zero and the coefficient becomes unstable, so \mention{lah}'s interval runs from 0.89 to 4.27. Their concentration is
better read off the counts than off a dispersion statistic.

Country contributes additional variation to overall item frequencies in this
sample. The data doesn't test whether country effects differ systematically by
item type or whether category boundaries decompose under conditioning.

A Bayesian multilevel model (negative binomial with crossed random effects for
item and country) estimates positive country-level variation: the country
random-effect standard deviation is 0.44 (95\% credible interval [CrI]:
0.28--0.65). The estimated predictive gain over the item-only model is
positive but imprecise: 18 expected log predictive density (ELPD) units with a
standard error (SE) of 13 on exact leave-one-out cross-validation.

The descriptive counts and CVs are the primary evidence; the model suggests
additional overall country variation under a crossed random-effects
structure. Full model
specification and results are in the
supplementary materials.\footnote{\ifblind Available at
  [link removed for blind review].\else Available at
  \url{https://github.com/BrettRey/interjections-hpc}.\fi}
Several methodological limitations remain. GloWbE's
part-of-speech tagger, trained on inner-circle English, fails to tag
regional interjections (\mention{yaar} and \mention{haba} receive no
interjection tag at all), which means the tagged frequencies
underestimate regional items. The \mention{ha} row needs a criterion
rather than a caveat, and the criterion has to be settled before the figure
rather than after it. Iterated \mention{HA! HA!} is a rendering of laughter,
closer to eye dialect than to a lexical item; \mention{ha} used for sarcasm or
scepticism is an interjectional use. The line is harder to draw in a written
corpus than a spoken one, since nothing was vocalized for the spelling to
represent, and iterated \mention{haha} in digital writing has arguably
conventionalized into a form in its own right. Nothing here rests on a claim
that imitative forms fall outside the category, which would contradict the
recruitment route described above.

Hand-coding the tagged concordance for all twenty sections shows how much
turns on that one decision, and the figures reported above count iterated
laughter rather than excluding it. Laughter is 51.5\% of tagged \mention{ha}
in the United States and 63.0\% in Great Britain, so the two readings are far
apart: across six defensible codings of the laughter and residual-token
questions, \mention{ha}'s coefficient of variation runs from 0.40 to 0.83.
A single figure would report one path through that set rather than the set
\citep[cf.][]{gelman2014statcrisis}.

Two results should be kept apart, because one survives the spread and the
other doesn't. The dispersion statistic doesn't: it isn't identified without
the criterion decision, and the 0.41 above corresponds to counting laughter.
The ranking does. Coding also shows what proportion of tagged tokens are
interjections at all, which runs from 34.5\% in Bangladesh to 84.0\% in Great
Britain, with \term{hectare} the contaminant in East Africa and South Asia and
personal names in Singapore, where only 6.1\% is \term{hectare}. Correcting
each section by its own rate moves Kenya from fifth place among the twenty to
fifteenth, and it does so under every one of the six codings. Pooling the
twenty rates to allow for their unequal precision, which runs from about 8,100
tagged tokens for the United States down to 420 for Ghana, moves no section by
more than 0.6 percentage points, because the variance between sections is much
larger than the sampling variance within them.

Country differences in GloWbE may also reflect corpus composition,
genre mix, internet ecology, and orthographic convention as much as
dialect or community structure. A spelling-variant check bears the last of
these out, in an unexpected direction. \mention{Ya} and \mention{yeh} look
like variants of \mention{yeah} that a single-string query would miss, but
both concentrate where they're homographs of substrate words:
\mention{ya} peaks in Tanzania (19.5 per million) and Malaysia (13.9),
\mention{yeh} in Singapore (5.6), Pakistan (4.9), and India (4.9), and no
\mention{yeh} token in the corpus carries the interjection tag at all.
Folding both strings into \mention{yeah} would drop its dispersion from a
CV of 0.55 to 0.46, but only by importing non-English tokens unevenly across
varieties, so the figures below keep the strings apart. Lengthened spellings
point the same way and put a number on it. Summing \mention{umm},
\mention{ummm}, and \mention{ummmm} into \mention{um} moves its dispersion
from a CV of 0.61 to 0.60 once Pakistan is set aside, so lengthening is
negligible in general; including Pakistan it rises to 0.72, because Pakistani
English returns nearly eight times more \mention{umm} than \mention{um}
where the median variety returns a third as much. A twenty-token sample
settles what that signature is: nineteen are the Arabic teknonym
\mention{Umm}, as in \mention{Umm Salama}, drawn from hadith and biographical
material, and the twentieth mentions the filler rather than using it. Raw
strings are weakest, then, exactly where a variety's substrate supplies a
homograph, which is a sharper and more testable caveat than orthographic
variation at large, and one that bears on the varieties this section
compares. The regional items carry the most exposure, since they're
substrate-derived to begin with. Hand-coding sixty-nine \mention{yaar}
tokens puts the English-matrix rate at roughly 60\% in Pakistan and 65\% in
India, the remainder being Tamil \mention{yaar} \enquote{who}, proper names,
and tokens sitting inside Roman-script Hindi or Urdu. The rates are estimated
from twenty-token samples and are too coarse to correct with, but they're
large enough that raw-string figures for these items should be read as upper
bounds. Raw strings are imperfect proxies too:
\mention{yes}, \mention{no}, \mention{ha}, \mention{ah},
\mention{oh}, \mention{well}, \mention{right}, and \mention{like} are
not automatically interjection tokens. Some are clausal, quoted,
discourse-marker uses, literary representations, or noise. The
methodological payoff is narrower: the validity of tag-restricted and raw
string measures varies by country, so pooled counts can hide measurement
problems as well as linguistic variation.

A third, weaker probe concerns hub-property asymmetry. One property may
be more central to network order than others, with prosodic isolation
and non-referentiality as the main candidates. The defeat condition is
equal contribution by all properties. The present evidence leaves this
prediction open.

Onomatopoeic forms like \mention{pop} can be read as integrated into
clause structure. The \textit{OED}, as \textcite[151--152]{meinard2015}
reports, classes \mention{pop} in \mention{the cork went pop} as an adverb,
a reading Meinard rejects, taking \mention{went} to introduce reported
speech. If the adverb reading is right, neutralizing prosodic isolation
brings immediate reclassification. Restoring partial referentiality, by
contrast, allows the form to hover between noun and interjection uses (as
with \mention{God}). This suggests prosodic isolation may be the hub, but
the evidence is indirect and the key example contested.

The probes differ in evidential weight. Pathway residue is the main
explanatory payoff, though only the invocative route is documented in detail.
The GloWbE analysis establishes additional overall country variation rather
than boundary decomposition, inventory counts establish turnover rather than
stability, and hub-property asymmetry is an open conjecture. Prototype theory
describes gradience but doesn't by itself tie independently warranted
histories to source-specific residues.

At the framework level, if a feature-based account
(e.g., [\textsc{+peripheral}, \textsc{-argument-taking},
\textsc{+expressive}]) generates the same predictions without causal
structure, the network account would be a relabelling. The
causal-network payoffs are pathway residues and the field-relative
decomposition; if both fail under further testing, the account collapses to a descriptive
property list.

\subsection{Boundary disputes as causal-link overlap}\label{sec:boundaries}

The boundary disputes that embarrassed classical category theory
(interjections vs.\ nouns, verbs, adverbs, fillers, routine formulae)
become intelligible once the three field-relative cuts are separated. Each
dispute may reflect partial property overlap and differences in what a field
needs its classification to distinguish. The disputes motivate that analysis;
they aren't independent evidence for every proposed causal link.

Routine formulae (\mention{bye}, \mention{hello}, \mention{sorry}) share
standalone utterance status and pragmatic function with interjections but
add addressee-directedness and social predictability
\citep[108--110]{ameka1992}. For grammar, those shared properties place
them near interjections. For speech-act analysis, the additional
properties matter more, because they predict conventional response slots,
addressees, and felicity conditions. They're structured by additional
processes (social convention, politeness norms) that prototypical
interjections don't require.

Fillers (\mention{um}, \mention{uh}) share syntactic non-integration with
interjections but differ in emphasis, pause distribution, and
citation-introduction ability \citep[560, 567--572]{oconnell2005}. They occupy a
neighbouring region of the property space, structured by partially
overlapping links (processing-pressure management rather than
emotive expression).

The Ameka-Wilkins debate can be reframed in these terms. Ameka's
separation preserves distinctions relevant to social routines and
speech-act status \citep[109--110]{ameka1992}. Wilkins's inclusion preserves
pragmatic and semantic overlap with interjections \citep[142]{wilkins1992}.
The scope of each classification is indexed to its explanatory purpose; its
adequacy still depends on the distinctions and projections the evidence
supports.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The singular label \term{interjection} conceals three legitimate but
non-identical classifications. \intj{syn} is principally grammar-facing,
\intj{sem} semantics-facing, and \intj{prag} interaction-facing. Each cuts
through a mixed network rather than containing properties from only one
domain. Canonical sign-types belong to all three, but their overlap isn't a
fourth category and contributes no independent projective force.

The strongest empirical result is path dependence. The history of
\mention{gee} documents an invocative route and euphemistic residue.
Complement-taking \mention{damn} is compatible with a verbal or optative
source, though the sequence remains reconstructive. Selected verbal
derivatives retain source-interjection semantics in included VP-head uses.
The preregistered \mention{fie} study fails to support ordered erosion.
GloWbE establishes additional country-level variation in overall item
frequencies, and COHA illustrates inventory turnover; neither result
establishes category-boundary decomposition or stable projectibility.

Those findings support parts of the proposed causal order and motivate the
remaining links as testable hypotheses. Prosodic--syntactic coupling and
routine entrenchment are stabilizer candidates; regional or indexical
conditioning is a maintenance hypothesis. The evidence doesn't establish the
full network, category-level stability, or corrective control.

The three-category architecture makes the forward question
precise. Each classification supplies prospective declarations with
independent targets: unobserved grammatical environments, semantic immediacy
and repeatability, recipient uptake, next-turn treatment, and floor transfer.
A held-out comparison against category-free baselines is still needed.
Failure would demote the corresponding projective claim; it wouldn't by
itself abolish the other categories.

The general lesson is methodological. When a traditional term spans grammar,
meaning, and interaction, the choice isn't between one privileged syntactic
category and an undifferentiated functional profile. Several inquiry-relative
categories may be real, overlap systematically, and earn different standing.
The work is to identify their bearers, keep their projections non-circular,
and match each causal claim to the evidence that supports it.

\section[Supplementary materials]{Supplementary materials: GloWbE conditioning analysis}
\label{app:conditioning}

\subsection{Data}

Frequency counts for 18 items were extracted from the GloWbE corpus
\citep{daviesfuchs2015}: 1.9 billion words across 20 countries. Items
include the 10 most frequent interjection-tagged forms (\mention{yes},
\mention{no}, \mention{oh}, \mention{yeah}, \mention{hi}, \mention{hey},
\mention{wow}, \mention{hello}, \mention{ah}, \mention{ha}), three
regional interjections (\mention{lah}, \mention{yaar}, \mention{haba}),
the semi-regional \mention{eh}, the boundary items \mention{cheers} and
\mention{bye}, and the fillers \mention{um} and \mention{uh}.

Figure~\ref{fig:country-variation} displays frequency per million words by
country for each item. Core-item counts are tag-restricted; regional-item
counts are raw strings because the tagger doesn't recognize them.

These counts are a model-dependent measurement rather than a transparent one:
a tagger stands between the text and the number, so the figures carry the
tagger's error as well as the corpus's composition. A model-dependent outcome
has to be evaluated by variety and not only on average, since an error rate
that holds overall but shifts across the varieties being compared is the
measurement analogue of a trend that isn't parallel
\citep{reynolds2026didcorpus}. The audit reported below is that evaluation.

One limit on it should be stated plainly. Hand-coding a tagged concordance
measures precision, the share of tagged tokens that are genuine, and says
nothing about recall, the share of genuine tokens the tagger found.
\mention{Haba} shows recall can be zero: 373 Nigerian tokens, none tagged. So
the corrected figures below are corrected for false positives only, and a
section whose genuine tokens the tagger largely missed would look clean on
this check while still being wrong.

Two features of the corpus constrain what country conditioning can mean here.
Country is a property of the page rather than of a speaker, assigned through
Google's region function: for a site with a top-level country domain the
assignment is direct, but for a \texttt{.com} or \texttt{.org} site
\textcite[4]{daviesfuchs2015} report that Google \enquote{relies on several
heuristics, including 1) the IP address for the web server, 2) who links to
that website, and 3) who visits the website}, and they raise the question
themselves of \enquote{how well Google has correctly identified web sites by
country}. The page list was also seeded by running \enquote{hundreds of very
high frequency 3-grams (three word strings)} from the Corpus of Contemporary
American English against Google \citep[4]{daviesfuchs2015}, so the sampling
frame is anchored to American frequency data.

The counts aren't all of one kind either, and the reason is worth stating
because it constrains what the comparison can show. English-Corpora.org tags
automatically, and the tagger handles regionalisms poorly. Counts for the core
items are restricted to the interjection tag, but restriction isn't the same as
exclusion. Hand-coding twenty tagged \mention{ha} tokens from Kenya and twenty
from Tanzania leaves six and eight respectively as interjections; the rest are
almost all the abbreviation for \term{hectare}, in agricultural and development
prose. On that basis \mention{ha} in Kenya falls from 24.9 tokens per million
to roughly 7.5, and from fifth place among the twenty countries to nineteenth.
So the tag narrows a count without cleaning it, and it fails worst where a
homograph belongs to a text type a region produces more of.

Counts for \mention{haba} and \mention{yaar} can't be tag-restricted at all,
because
neither string is ever assigned that tag: all 373 Nigerian tokens of
\mention{haba} are tagged as common or proper nouns, 65\% of them on a reading
that includes the proper noun. The regional figures are raw strings
where the core figures are tag-restricted, which inflates the regional items
relative to the items they're set beside. False positives in the tagged core
counts partially mask the mismatch. Its scale is indicated by
\textcite{unuabonahdaniel2020}, who work on \mention{haba} in the same
Nigerian component and report 248 tokens against the 373 a raw query returns.
They reach the smaller figure by inspecting each hit and excluding Nigerian
Pidgin, indigenous-language, and duplicated material
\citep[69]{unuabonahdaniel2020}.
Two of those three exclusions are invisible to a frequency query, and the
third, duplication, is a known property of the corpus. That's a limit imposed by the tagger rather than a choice: a
form has to be known to the tagger before it can be tagged, and a borrowed
interjection in a postcolonial variety is exactly what an automatic tagger
trained elsewhere will miss.

    \mention{haba}) cluster in one or two countries. Core-item frequencies

\subsection{Model specification}

Four Bayesian negative binomial models were fitted using
\texttt{brms}~2.23 in R, with 4 chains of 2{,}000 iterations (1{,}000
warmup) each. The confirmatory models (m0--m2) use exact reloo for
leave-one-out cross-validation; the exploratory model (m3) is assessed
by Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS) diagnostics only.

\begin{quote}
\small
\noindent\texttt{m0} (unconditional):\\
\texttt{count \textasciitilde{} 1 + offset(log(words\_m)) + (1~|~item)}

\noindent\texttt{m1} (conditioned):\\
\texttt{count \textasciitilde{} 1 + offset(log(words\_m)) +}\\
\texttt{(1~|~item) + (1~|~country)}

\noindent\texttt{m2} (item type):\\
\texttt{count \textasciitilde{} 1 + item\_type + offset(log(words\_m)) +}\\
\texttt{(1~|~item) + (1~|~country)}

\noindent\texttt{m3} (exploratory):\\
\texttt{count \textasciitilde{} 1 + item\_type + offset(log(words\_m)) +}\\
\texttt{(1~|~item) + (1~+~item\_type~||~country)}
\end{quote}

Model m3 estimates country-level varying slopes by item type. Because
several item-type cells are sparse (semi-regional: 1 item, boundary: 2,
filler: 2, regional: 3), these slopes are thinly supported and m3 is
treated as exploratory.

\subsection{Results}

Exact leave-one-out cross-validation (confirmatory models):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
  \hline
  Model & ELPD diff & SE diff \\
  \hline
  m1 (conditioned) & 0.0 & 0.0 \\
  m2 (item type) & $-$9.3 & 7.9 \\
  m0 (unconditional) & $-$17.9 & 13.3 \\
  \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The conditioned model (m1) has the highest ELPD point estimate, but its
estimated advantage over m0 is imprecise. Adding item-type fixed effects (m2)
doesn't improve the point estimate over m1, while the item random effect
captures substantial between-item variation. The exploratory model (m3) is excluded
from formal ranking because 9 observations retain Pareto $k > 0.7$
after moment matching. The corresponding PSIS leave-one-out estimates are
unstable.

Random effects from the conditioned model (m1):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Country standard deviation (SD) = 0.44 (95\% CrI: 0.28--0.65)
  \item Item SD = 2.07 (95\% CrI: 1.49--2.96)
  \item Ratio: 4.7:1 (item identity varies more, while country contributes
    additional overall variation)
\end{itemize}

The evidence is directionally consistent: conditioning on country has a
positive estimated predictive difference ($\Delta$ELPD = 17.9, SE 13.3), and the country
random-effect SD is positive across the interval. The SE is large
relative to the point estimate in this sample (18 items $\times$ 20
countries = 360 observations). The descriptive CV results in the main
text provide the primary evidence for the conditioning probe; the model
suggests the pattern survives the crossed random-effects structure.

The hand audit of \mention{ha} in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} shows that its
counts carry a country-varying validity rate. To propagate that error, each
country's rate thins \mention{ha} by a draw from its \textsc{beta} posterior;
the other seventeen items stay unchanged, and both models are refitted across
draws. The correction is small. The country random-effect SD shifts from 0.42
to 0.44 with iterated laughter counted and 0.45 with it excluded; the model
comparison shifts from 28.8 to 26.9 and 28.5. These approximate
leave-one-out values permit only an internal contrast. Since concordance
coding estimates precision, the correction removes false positives, not
missed tokens.

\subsection{Coding}

Each analyzable token was coded for two confirmatory properties. First,
\term{verbal syntax}: whether the derivative heads a VP. Second,
\term{semantic residue}: whether the derivative retains the source
interjection's meaning profile: impressed or evaluative response for
\mention{wow}; disapproval or contempt for \mention{boo}; appreciative
or surprised response for \mention{ooh}; and directive away-sending for
\mention{shoo}.

Two secondary descriptive properties were also coded. \term{Interactional
residue} tracks whether the verbal event retains the source
interjection's social-interactional profile as a recognizable act of
response, uptake, display, or direction. \term{Targeted entity} tracks
whether a target, object, addressee, or stimulus is recoverable. These
secondary codes weren't used in the confirmatory test.
