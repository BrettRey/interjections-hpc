\begin{abstract}
Is \term{interjection} a genuine lexical category of English? Grammars
have doubted it, and the doubt is about standing rather than about how
much has been written. This paper argues that the category earns its
place, and that what earns it isn't a defining property but
projectibility: observing some properties of a case licenses predictions
about others (a defeasible default, not an entailment). Membership licenses
inferences about
prosodic packaging, supplement function,
use-conditional stance, sequential position, response function, and
interactional uptake. Those inferences are pragmatic rather than
truth-conditional, which is a result about the category and not a change
of subject.

The paper argues that they're licensed by a stable causal network among
those properties. Invocative, verbal, and onomatopoeic routes recruit forms into a
similar profile, while leaving different pathway residues.
Regional/indexical conditioning supplies a further source of boundary
structure. Supplementary Corpus of Global Web-Based English (GloWbE) and
Corpus of Historical American English (COHA) probes illustrate boundary
behaviour, exit residue, and attrition. The result is a path-dependent
account of category membership. What membership warrants is bounded: for a
lexical item in a grammatical environment in present-day English, observing
part of the cluster licenses expectations about the rest, because the
prosodic, syntactic, semantic, and interactional diagnostics are linked.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

English interjections are small forms with large consequences. A
speaker's \mention{oh}, \mention{wow}, \mention{hey}, \mention{damn}, or
\mention{mm-hm} can package a response, display stance, open or sustain a
sequence, initiate repair, or invite uptake. Grammars have never been
settled about what to do with them. This paper asks whether
\term{interjection} is a genuine lexical category of English, and what
a form's membership in it licenses us to predict.

In 1586, William Bullokar wrote the earliest grammar of English. It
included interjections, which he defined as words that betoken
\enquote{a sudden passion of the mind} \citep[373]{bullokar1586}.

Four centuries later the standing is less secure.
\textcite[90]{jespersen1924} treated interjection as a manner in which
words of other categories may be used, which is to deny it categoryhood.
\textcite[67]{quirk1985} kept the category but called it
\enquote{marginal and anomalous}.
\textcite[22]{HuddlestonPullum2002} listed interjection as one of nine
lexical categories in \textit{The Cambridge Grammar of the English
Language} (\textit{CGEL}) (\mention{ah},
\mention{damn}, \mention{gosh}, \mention{hey}, \mention{oh},
\mention{ooh}, \mention{ouch}, \mention{whoa}, \mention{wow}), while the
companion textbook set it aside as \enquote{the minor category of
interjections \dots\ about which there really isn't anything interesting
for a grammar to say} \citep[16]{HuddlestonPullum2005}. Its second
edition returns interjection to the inventory, but concedes that
\enquote{grammarians typically say very little about interjections, and
we don't plan to be an exception} \citep[24]{HuddlestonPullumReynolds2022}.
Readmitted to the list, and still largely undescribed.

\textcite{libert2019} objects that calling interjections neglected
\enquote{is an overstatement}: work on their phonetics, syntax, semantics,
and pragmatics goes back a long way. What's stayed
unsettled is standing rather than volume of attention, namely whether
interjections constitute a lexical category, and what membership would
license.

\textcite[63]{cram2008} makes the historiographic point more sharply.
Surveying the tradition from Isidore of Seville onward, he finds \enquote{no
firmer consensus about this category than at any earlier period}, and notes
that interjections \enquote{function as a touchstone or test case for various
boundary controversies}, naming the syntax--pragmatics and
semantics--pragmatics boundaries. The difference is in what the case is asked
to reveal.
Cram asks what interjections show about our theories. I ask what holds this
category together.

The empirical picture hasn't simplified. The evidence bearing on membership is
spread across phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics, and no
one of them settles the question. The
category resists clean definition, since no single property is necessary
and sufficient, and its boundaries with nouns, verbs, adverbs,
fillers, and routine formulae are all disputed.
Cross-linguistically, \enquote{interjection} is at best a comparative
concept \citep{haspelmath2010}.

Those difficulties are what make the category question worth asking. If
interjections are a category, the relevant payoff isn't an essence or a
single defining feature. It's \term{projectibility}: the inferential
payoff by which observing some properties of a new case licenses
predictions about others \citep{Goodman1955}. For interjections,
prosodic isolation, supplement function, or stance display should
license expectations about non-inflection, non-referential or
use-conditional meaning, sequential position, response function, and
interactional uptake.

That's meant in at least two distinct ways. A learner or hearer
meeting an unfamiliar form reads the cues and anticipates its behaviour,
without needing to recognize it as an interjection. A linguist assigns the form to the
category and derives the predictions from it explicitly. Both draw on the same coupling.
Section~\ref{sec:proj-partial} separates them where the difference matters.

A form's \term{profile} is the particular combination of cluster properties it shows
in a given grammatical environment: whether it's prosodically set off,
whether it inflects, whether it refers, what stance or response it carries.
The word is doing bookkeeping here and has nothing to do with the
figure-and-base sense familiar from cognitive grammar. To say that a
property or a category \term{licenses} an inference is to say the inference
is a good default, defeasible by the particular case, not that it follows
necessarily.

The object of analysis is the category position, not the membership list
alone. Individual items such as \mention{wow}, \mention{damn},
\mention{bruh}, and \mention{fie} matter because they show what can be
inferred from membership: likely prosody, syntactic independence,
limits on argumenthood, stance or response function, and possible routes
into or out of the profile.

By \term{path dependence}, I mean that where a form came from keeps
showing in how it behaves at the category's edges, long after it has
arrived. \mention{Damn} came in from a verb and still takes a complement
(\mention{damn these mosquitoes!}); \mention{gee} came in by clipping a
religious invocation and takes nothing at all. Both sit squarely in the
profile, and they'd be indistinguishable if convergence erased history. It doesn't. The leftovers of a form's source let an analyst reason backwards
from how it behaves now to where it came from.

Throughout, I use \term{property cluster} descriptively for the empirical
pattern of co-occurring features. What's claimed for English interjections is
bounded accordingly: the inferences membership licenses most strongly concern
use, stance, sequence, and uptake rather than denotation.

This inferential framing motivates a causal-network analysis.
\textcite{khalidi2013,khalidi2018nodes} argues that natural kinds are
nodes in causal structures rather than sets defined by essences. On
this account, a category is theoretically useful when its place in a
causal network supports reliable induction. Applied to interjections,
the claim is that several diagnostics, including prosodic isolation,
syntactic independence, non-referentiality, non-inflection, and
interactional force, cohere because several causal pathways converge on
the same pragmatic node. The resulting profile is thin but projectible, a
claim Section~\ref{sec:proj-domain} makes good.

One form shows what that comes to. Take \mention{gee}, as in
(\ref{ex:gee}).

\ea\label{ex:gee} \exmention{\exfocus{Gee}, that's a lot of money.}
\z

Observing that \mention{gee} is set off from what follows and never inflects,
with no *\mention{geed} and no *\mention{gees}, licenses expectations not yet
checked: that it will work as a supplement, that its contribution will be
use-conditional rather than descriptive, and that it will turn up at the front
of a turn. Those expectations would be defeated if \mention{gee} began taking a
complement, on the model of *\mention{gee that it's expensive}, or acquired
inflection.

Diachrony matters because pragmatic profiles have histories. Forms such
as \mention{gee}, \mention{damn}, or \mention{bruh} change category
position as historically contentful or addressive expressions come to
manage stance, sequence, and interactional alignment in recurrent
contexts \citep[cf.][]{juckertaavitsainen2013}. Change over time helps
explain why the pragmatic profile is stable while individual forms enter,
leave, and retain source-specific residues.

The network classifies lexical items in grammatical environments, not
word families as wholes. A word family can contain distinct historically
related lexemes. The \mention{damn} word family includes verbal
\mention{damn} in \mention{I damn you} and standalone interjectional
\mention{damn!}. Prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}
belongs to that family too, but the analysis needn't decide here whether
it's a noun-phrase-internal extension of the interjection lexeme or a
separate adjectival lexeme. What matters is that it's syntactically
integrated inside a noun phrase while retaining use-conditional
expressive force.

Three nodes in that network are in play here: a syntactic node, a semantic or
use-conditional node, and an interactional-pragmatic node. The label
\enquote{interjection} covers the forms that occupy all three at once. I write these
as \intj{syn},
\intj{sem}, and
\intj{prag}. The sections below first
describe the diagnostic cluster, then explain why these nodes overlap
and why they come apart.

Section~\ref{sec:cluster} documents the cluster.
Section~\ref{sec:causal-framework} introduces the causal-network
framework that explains why the interjection profile supports pragmatic
inference. The rest of the paper identifies the network order that
structures the cluster, extends \textcite{gehweiler2008}'s single-case
analysis to a general claim about path dependence, and argues that
interjections exhibit a distinctive form of projectibility, primarily
pragmatic rather than truth-conditional.

\section{The property cluster}\label{sec:cluster}

English interjections exhibit a cluster of co-occurring properties. This
section takes phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics in turn,
drawing primarily on the empirical base documented in \textcite{ameka1992},
\textcite{quirk1985}, and \textcite{HuddlestonPullum2002}. It answers two
questions: what properties cluster, and what local diagnostic inferences each
property supports. These inferences show that individual properties are
structured rather than accidental. Six properties emerge: prosodic isolation,
non-inflection, syntactic non-integration, non-referentiality, emotive or
stance force, and sequential use, the last being the capacity to constitute a
conversational move on its own. The three nodes cut across those five rather than regrouping them, because
each field needs the category to predict something different. The subscripts
are used from here on, with assignment criteria given in
Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence} where the diagnostic matrix needs them, and
the argument for treating the nodes as three deferred to
Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes}. Section~\ref{sec:projectibility} asks
the stronger question: when does a partial observation license inference
across linguistic domains? The causal question, why these properties
travel together, is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure}.

\subsection{Phonology}\label{sec:phonology}

Interjections are prosodically isolated, and \textcite[108]{ameka1992}
states it without hedging: they're \enquote{always separated by a pause from
the other utterances with which they may co-occur} and \enquote{always
constitute an intonation unit by themselves}. I treat the property as
strongly characteristic rather than exceptionless, because the diagnostic
matrix below records partial cases (\mention{um}). That gap between a
categorical statement and its boundary cases is the pattern
Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence} takes up. In writing, this isolation surfaces as punctuation
(commas, dashes, exclamation marks), setting the interjection off from the
clause it accompanies \citep[1350]{HuddlestonPullum2002}.

The corpus evidence below is written, so prosodic isolation is tracked
through proxies: punctuation, standalone position, and absence of
integration into the host clause. These proxies are useful for historical
and large-corpus work, but they're proxies rather than direct acoustic
evidence. They aren't arbitrary ones.
On \textcite[41]{Jackendoff2025Parenthetical}'s account a parenthetical is
realized as an intonation phrase, so it ends in a boundary tone made of
\enquote{some combination of pause, lengthening, pitch discontinuity}, and the
flanking commas are what record those boundaries. Punctuation tracks prosodic
isolation because it notates the same breaks.

Interjections may also contain phonemes outside the
speaker's dialect. The interjection \mention{uh-oh} ([\ipa{əʔo}]) is one of
the few cases where a glottal stop appears in dialects that otherwise lack
it. Other atypical sounds include the alveolar clicks in
\mention{tut-tut} ([\ipa{ǀǀ}]), the voiceless bilabial fricative in
\mention{whew} ([\ipa{ɸɪu}]), and (for dialects that no longer use it)
the voiceless velar fricative in \mention{ugh} ([\ipa{əx}])
\citep[853]{quirk1985}. Spelling pronunciations often arise for these forms
(\mention{tut-tut} said [\ipa{tət tət}], \mention{ugh} said [\ipa{əɡ}]),
suggesting that the atypical phonemes are marked even for
native speakers.

Prosodic isolation is a strong cue: if
a novel form occupies its own intonation unit and resists integration into
the surrounding prosodic contour, a listener can predict syntactic
non-integration and non-referentiality. The atypical phonemes reinforce this
inference. But prosodic isolation isn't unique to interjections, since
vocatives, appositives, and other supplements share it \citep[cf.][]{wilkins1992}. What
distinguishes interjections is the convergence of prosodic isolation with
the properties below.

\subsection{Morphology}

English interjections characteristically don't inflect or derive. They
resist the morphological operations that characterize other open-class
categories: no plural (*\mention{ouches}), no tense (*\mention{ouched}),
no comparative (*\mention{oucher}), no nominalization
(*\mention{ouchness}) \citep[106]{ameka1992}. Since
\textcite{libert2019} notes that \enquote{few, if any, papers focus on
the morphology of interjections}, the apparent absence of morphological
productivity may partly reflect a gap in the research rather than a
firm empirical generalization.

Some interjections retain remnants of earlier categorial membership.
\mention{Heavens} and \mention{bollocks} preserve the nominal plural
suffix. None of these remnants are productive. The plural in
\mention{heavens} doesn't contrast with a singular *\mention{heaven!} (as
an interjection), and speakers don't extend the pattern to new forms.

Non-inflection predicts
non-argumenthood. Forms that don't inflect can't control verb agreement or take
a determiner, so they can't fill the syntactic slots that require these
operations. But non-inflection alone isn't diagnostic: adverbs also don't
inflect \citep[106]{ameka1992}. The stronger diagnostic is syntactic:
adverbs characteristically form constituents with other words, while
canonical interjections occur as standalone supplements and resist
integration with neighbouring material \citep[152]{meinard2015}.

\subsection{Syntax}\label{sec:syntax}

Interjections characteristically function as \term{supplements},
\enquote{elements which occupy a position in linear sequence without
being integrated into the syntactic structure of the sentence}
\citep[1350]{HuddlestonPullum2002}. In \mention{Damn, we're going to be late!}, the
interjection isn't a complement or modifier of anything in the clause.
It's appended to the beginning as a parenthetical.

Supplement function isn't unique to interjections. Relative clauses, noun
phrases, adjective phrases, and adverb phrases can all function as
supplements \citep[1356--1360]{HuddlestonPullum2002}:

\ea\label{ex:supplements}
  \ea \exmention{\exfocus{Ah}, so you were there after all!} \hfill (interjection, p.~1360)
  \ex \exmention{We called in to see Sue's parents, \exfocus{which made us rather late}.} \hfill (relative clause, p.~1356)
  \ex \exmention{A university lecturer, \exfocus{Dr Brown}, was arrested for the crime.} \hfill (noun phrase, p.~1357)
  \z
\z
What distinguishes interjection supplements is their combination with
the other cluster properties: non-referentiality, non-inflection,
prosodic isolation, and emotive force. The function is shared; the
category differs.

A handful of interjections exceptionally take complements.
\textcite[1361fn]{HuddlestonPullum2002} analyse \mention{damn} in
\mention{damn these mosquitoes!} as the head of an interjection phrase
(IntjP) taking a noun phrase (NP) complement, one of the very few cases where an
interjection heads a phrase larger than a single word. But even here the syntax is
constrained: no subject is present or intended, and there's no tense
inflection. The construction is better treated as a residue of verb
syntax than as full verbal behaviour, a point that matters for the
recruitment pathways
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure}. A second kind of residue is
internal rather than external. \mention{Goddammit} preserves a
verb-object-pronoun structure inside what is now a single word, so the syntax
survives as fossilized material rather than as anything the form can still do.
\textcite[40]{cuenca2002} reads it the same way from the grammaticalization
side, listing \mention{Goddammit} among the cases where change in function and
meaning is \enquote{accompanied by phonetic weakness and word integration}. (The internal structure of
lexicalized pairs like
\mention{oh boy} and \mention{holy cow} is less clear and won't be
resolved here.)

Supplement function predicts prosodic isolation and resistance to
syntactic integration:

\ea\label{ex:syntactic-predictions}
  \ea[*]{\exmention{I think that damn we're going to be late.}}
  \ex \exmention{I think that, damn, we're going to be late.}
  \ex[*]{\exmention{Damn and quickly, we left.}}
  \z
\z
The contrast in (\ref{ex:syntactic-predictions}) is prosodic rather than
syntactic: the first two differ only in whether \mention{damn} is set off.
\textcite[41--42]{Jackendoff2025Parenthetical} generalizes the point beyond
interjections as the IP-Break Hypothesis: placement tracks intonational-phrase
breaks rather than syntactic constituency, so a host admits a parenthetical
where it independently permits an IP break, provided the parenthetical is
itself licensed in that position \citep[43]{Jackendoff2025Parenthetical}. He gives pairs of hosts that are
\enquote{syntactically identical} but differ in where a parenthetical may go,
since \enquote{the difference in the parenthetical's position is a consequence
of the difference in position of the IP breaks in the host}
\citep[42]{Jackendoff2025Parenthetical}.

Supplement function also predicts a semantic consequence: if the form is
syntactically non-integrated, its contribution is independent of the
proposition expressed by the host clause. Remove the interjection and the
truth conditions don't change, the hallmark of non-truth-conditional
meaning \citep[cf.][166]{potts2007}.

\subsection{Semantics}\label{sec:semantics}

English interjections characteristically don't refer. Here,
\term{non-referentiality} means absence of ordinary entity/event
denotation and argumental construal while allowing semantic content.
Nouns and verbs typically pick out entities or events in the world.
Interjections express internal states of their users
\citep{leech2006}: anger (\mention{damn}),
disgust (\mention{eww}, \mention{yuck}), surprise (\mention{wow}), regret
(\mention{alas}), pain (\mention{ouch}), realization (\mention{eureka}).
Some interjections are more propositional than emotive: \mention{yes}
and \mention{no} affirm or deny propositions, and \mention{oh} can
signal recognition of new information rather than expressing feeling.

\textcite{wierzbicka1992} argues that interjections have genuine
semantic content, decomposable via Natural Semantic Metalanguage. The
forms may have content, and \mention{yes} and \mention{no} may operate
on propositions, while still lacking ordinary entity/event denotation.

\textcite[119]{wilkins1992} presses harder, and his title states the
objection: interjections are deictics. On his account they \enquote{convey
complete propositions}, the \enquote{referential arguments in the
propositions conveyed by interjections are provided by context}, and so
interjections are \enquote{shifters (indexicals) by virtue of
being built semantically out of basic deictic elements} such as
\mention{I}, \mention{you}, \mention{now}, and \mention{here}. If that's
right, calling interjections non-referential looks like a straightforward
mistake.

The two claims are about different things, and Wilkins's own wording shows
it: the referential arguments are supplied by context, not by the form.
Non-referentiality, as I use it, is a claim about what a form contributes on
its own, so it leaves the contextual filling untouched. \mention{Ouch!} picks
out no thing or event, and resists being made definite, quantified over, or
used as an argument, whatever indexical structure its explication turns out to
contain. If anything Wilkins asks more of the semantics than I do, since he has
to say what it supplies for the filling to work at all, and the distributional
facts I rely on hold on his account as readily as on mine. What his account does show is that
\enquote{non-referential} is a poor name for the property, kept here only
because it's the established one.

Not everyone reads Wilkins that way. On the conceptualist analyses of
\textcite{wierzbicka1992}, \textcite{ameka1992} and Wilkins, interjections
encode substantial conceptual structure. \textcite[211]{wharton2000} argues
from relevance theory that they encode considerably less, being
\enquote{partly natural and partly coded} and falling \enquote{at various
points along a continuum between showing and saying}. That
dispute doesn't need settling here. Neither Wilkins's deictic slots nor
Wharton's activated attitudinal concepts give \mention{ouch!} a thing or event
to pick out, so the distributional property holds on either analysis. But
Wharton's continuum is worth noting for a second reason. What varies along it
is how much of an interjection's import is coded rather than shown, which is a
gradient within the category and not a blurred edge to it
(Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence}).

\textcite[241]{cruz2009} carries the dispute further. He argues that the
heterogeneity of interjections, \enquote{with items incorporated from other
lexical categories}, together with the openness of the class, \enquote{could
indicate the existence of a continuum of more and less conceptual items}, and
that the conceptual ones encode \enquote{not full concepts, but some schematic
material requiring subsequent pragmatic adjustments}. What matters here isn't
the continuum, which Wharton already supplies, but its cause. Cruz derives
gradedness from recruitment out of other categories and from open-class
coinage, which are the mechanisms Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure} treats as
the cluster's sources.

Wierzbicka's explications characterize the use-conditional content that
\intj{sem} (Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes})
tracks. Non-referentiality is a cluster property rather than a
definition.

The contrast with nouns is sharpest where both share a lexical source.
\mention{Jesus} as a noun refers to a person; \mention{Jesus!} as an
interjection doesn't. In the interjection use, as
\textcite[88]{gehweiler2008} puts it, \mention{Jesus} \enquote{has lost
its propositional content, i.e.\ it is no longer used to refer and hence
no longer influences truth conditions}.
This semantic shift is part of the recruitment pathway from noun to
interjection (Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure}).

The projectibility here is mostly negative. A form that doesn't refer
resists being made definite, quantified over, or used as an argument:
*\mention{the damn!}, *\mention{every ouch}, *\mention{Damn surprised me},
and *\mention{There's damn}. These restrictions are non-trivial because
they distinguish interjections from nouns that have undergone only partial
bleaching. Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling} treats the same relation from
the causal side: non-referentiality helps keep the form out of
argument structure, which in turn supports prosodic isolation.

\subsection{Pragmatics}\label{sec:pragmatics}

Interjections typically carry exclamatory or directive force: \mention{ouch!}
expresses pain, \mention{shh!} demands silence \citep[88]{quirk1985}. But
the pragmatic range extends beyond exclamation. How far beyond is worth a
number. \textcite[18]{elffers2008} reports that in Hofstede's analysis of a
corpus of spoken Dutch, only 7.04\,\% of interjections
\enquote{turned out to fulfil an expressive illocutionary function},
against 52\,\% classed as expositive, which he glosses as directive within the
conversation. The corpus is Dutch rather than English, so the figure indicates
rather than establishes. It points the same way as Dingemanse's interactional
focus. The emotive characterization grammars lead with covers the minority
case. Interjections greet
(\mention{hey}, and, at the boundary with routine formulae,
\mention{hello}; see Section~\ref{sec:boundaries}), mark agreement (\mention{yes},
\mention{amen}), indicate understanding (\mention{oh}, \mention{uh-huh}),
and signal backchanneling, the listener's ongoing attention to a speaker's
turn \citep{dingemanse2020, stivers2019}.

The most common interjections tagged in the Corpus of Global Web-Based
English \citep[GloWbE;][]{daviesfuchs2015} illustrate this pragmatic breadth: \mention{yes}, \mention{no},
\mention{oh}, \mention{yeah}, \mention{hi}, \mention{hey}, \mention{wow},
\mention{hello}, \mention{ah}, \mention{ha}. The list is dominated by
interactional items, forms whose primary function is to manage the
conversation rather than to contribute propositional content.
\textcite[171]{poggi2009} presses the other way. An interjection, on her
analysis, is a holophrastic signal whose equivalent is not a word but
\enquote{a communicative act including the meaning of both a performative and
a propositional content}, so that \mention{ouch!} paraphrases as \enquote{I am
feeling pain}. The grain distinction from Section~\ref{sec:semantics} applies
again. Poggi's propositional content belongs to the act the interjection
performs, not to any clause it helps compose. Her contrast between holophrastic
and articulated signals says why. Nouns and verbs characteristically convey part of a
sentence, an interjection a whole one. Prenominal \mention{damn} in
\mention{the damn car} is an interjection that doesn't. That explains the independence noted in
Section~\ref{sec:syntax}: a form contributes nothing to a host clause's truth
conditions if it isn't part of a clause at all. Holophrasis accounts for the
property rather than competing with it.
\textcite{dingemanse2024} estimates that roughly one in seven
conversational turns is an interjection, and interactional tools
dominate the most frequent cases: continuers like
\mention{mm-hm} that scaffold extended turns, repair initiators like
\mention{huh?} that calibrate mutual understanding, and change-of-state
tokens like \mention{oh} that display evolving knowledge\footnote{%
  \term{Token} does double duty in work of this kind, and I keep the senses
  apart. In \term{change-of-state token} and \term{response token} it's the
  interactional-linguistic sense: a minimal form deployed as a move in a
  sequence. Elsewhere I use it for the unit of analysis, a lexical item in a
  grammatical environment as against the lexeme that labels a family of
  historically related such items.}
\citep[cf.][]{norrick2009}.

The boundary with \term{routine formulae} is instructive.
\textcite[2--3]{coulmas1981} defines routine formulae as
% "conventionalised" is Coulmas's British spelling, preserved as quoted;
% verified via Ameka's verbatim reproduction at p. 108, same page range.
\enquote{highly conventionalised prepatterned expressions whose occurrence
is tied to more or less standard communication situations}:
\mention{bye}, \mention{hello}, \mention{sorry}, \mention{thank you}.
\textcite[109--110]{ameka1992} argues these differ from interjections in
three ways: they include addressees, they're predictable responses to social
conventions, and they're always speech acts rather than mere reflections of
the speaker's mental state. \textcite[142]{wilkins1992} counters that these
differences make routine formulae \enquote{a distinct pragmatic and semantic
subtype of interjections}, not a separate category.

The disagreement is field-relative in a causal-network sense. For a
grammar, routine formulae pattern near interjections because they share
prosodic isolation and pragmatic function. For speech-act analysis or
interactional pragmatics, they support different predictions: addressee
orientation, occurrence in standard social sequences, and conventional
felicity conditions. The same form can sit close to one node
for one inquiry and farther away for another, depending on which causal
relations and predictions the inquiry tracks.

What a projective claim licenses depends on what was observed. Observing a novel
non-propositional stance form licenses expectations about standalone
positioning, supplement-like syntax, backchannel availability, and
resistance to embedding under reported speech (\mention{*She said that
damn}). Observing a novel routine formula licenses expectations about
addressee-directedness and placement in conventional social sequences.
The overlap explains why routine formulae sit at the boundary; the
divergence explains why the boundary is disputed.
Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure} asks which links make those claims
reliable.

\subsection{Co-occurrence, not necessity}\label{sec:cooccurrence}

These properties co-occur statistically, not necessarily. The
literature's own definitions hedge, on every criterion they state.
% Verified verbatim against Wilkins 1992: 124 (running head "124 D. P.
% Wilkins / Interjections as deictics"), his numbered definition (1).
% Note: Gehweiler's block quote at p. 73 pluralizes "construction"; the
% singular here follows Wilkins directly.
\textcite[124]{wilkins1992} defines an interjection as
\enquote{a conventional lexical form which (commonly and) conventionally
constitutes an utterance on its own, (typically) does not enter into
construction with other word classes, is (usually) monomorphemic, and
(generally) does not host inflectional or derivational morphemes}. Four
criteria, a parenthetical hedge on each, and
\textcite[73]{gehweiler2008} adopts the definition while calling those
criteria the class's \enquote{obligatory properties}. No single property
is necessary and sufficient.

One dissent belongs here, because it's the strongest case against that last
sentence. \textcite[282]{libert2012} proposes exactly one criterion:
interjections have a \term{conversational valence} of~1, meaning they can be
used felicitously with no addressee present, where \mention{goodbye} requires
one and so has a valence of~2. If you stub your toe you can say
\mention{ouch!} alone. On this account interjections are
\enquote{a homogeneous word class, clearly delimited by a sole criterion}
\citep[294]{libert2012}.

The criterion works. What it costs is visible in how Libert reaches it, by
redefinition: interjections \enquote{as I have redefined them, have
only one function (as `expressions of feeling'); the function of `marks of
address' is undertaken by another class} \citep[294]{libert2012}. The
addressing uses are moved out of the category rather than shown to satisfy
the criterion, and they take a good deal with them: \mention{hey},
\mention{shh}, and \mention{psst}, and also the continuers, repair
initiators, and response tokens of Section~\ref{sec:pragmatics}, all of
which need someone to be there. A single property is necessary and
sufficient for what's left.

I take that as support for the decomposition proposed below rather than as a
counterexample to co-occurrence. Libert's line between expressions of
feeling and marks of address falls close to the line this paper draws
between \intj{sem} and
\intj{prag}, and he credits
\textcite[109]{ameka1992} with the same intuition while objecting, fairly,
that Ameka's gloss of conative interjections as \enquote{directed at} but
not \enquote{addressed to} people is obscure. So the disagreement isn't
about the facts. It's about what to do with them: Libert narrows the
category until one criterion suffices, and I keep the wider class and let
the properties co-occur without any one of them being definitional.
That isn't a bare preference. Categories in this tradition are established by
clusters of grammatical properties rather than by single criteria, since a
definition has to identify \enquote{the grammatical properties that distinguish
them from the concepts with which they contrast}
\citep[30]{HuddlestonPullum2002}. A single criterion would make interjection
the only category in the inventory picked out that way, which is a reason to
doubt the standard rather than the category.

Co-occurrence without necessity gets read as fuzziness, so it's worth saying
what it doesn't commit me to. It doesn't make membership a matter of degree.
Each diagnostic is determinate for a given form in a given environment:
\mention{damn} in \mention{damn these mosquitoes!} either takes a complement or
it doesn't, \mention{heavens} either carries the plural suffix or it doesn't,
and Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic-matrix} records values rather than degrees.
Determinacy is what makes the rest of the paper possible. The residues of
Section~\ref{sec:residues} and the turnover probes of
Section~\ref{sec:turnover} both measure a boundary moving, and a boundary can
only be seen to move if it sits somewhere in particular at each time.

What varies is typicality, not membership. Members differ in how many cluster
properties they carry and in which field-relative nodes they satisfy, so the
predictions membership licenses vary in strength, and a form near a boundary is
more exposed to drift than one sitting well inside. That puts the gradience in
stability rather than in membership, which is where the causal story of
Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure} can act on it.

What's fluid is the edge across time, and \textcite[144]{boyd1999} builds that
in. \enquote{The properties that determine the conditions for falling under} a
kind term \enquote{may vary over time (or space)} while the term keeps its
definition, which he calls \enquote{the historicity of the individuation
conditions for the definitional property cluster}. His target is the demand
that kinds be defined by \enquote{unchanging necessary and sufficient
membership conditions} \citep[164]{boyd1999}, a demand about permanence rather
than about precision.

Boyd doesn't make the further commitment to sharpness. He treats the
extensional vagueness of a property-cluster kind as irresolvable in principle,
which is indeterminacy rather than inaccessibility. I assume instead that the edge
is sharp but not finitely locatable, and that the fuzzy appearance comes from
measurement and from aggregation across speakers \citep{reynolds2025sorites}.
Two qualifications stop that assumption doing more work than it should. The
sharpness would have to be maintained rather than stipulated, by acquisition,
entrenchment, alignment, and transmission, which makes how sharp a boundary is
an empirical question about how strong those pressures are. And aggregation
blurs: a population of speakers with sharp but individually placed thresholds
behaves like a category with a fuzzy edge, so the corpus gradience reported in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} is neutral between the two pictures rather than
evidence for either. None of the arguments here need the stronger claim. They
need only what the previous paragraph asserts, that each diagnostic have a
determinate value for a given form in a given environment.

The pattern calls for explanation. Network order couples the
properties: prosodic isolation reinforces syntactic non-integration,
non-referentiality removes the need for argument structure, and
pragmatic conventionalization entrenches the emotive function.

The coupling is visible when it breaks. \mention{Damn} as an interjection
(\mention{Damn!}) has the full cluster: prosodic isolation, syntactic
non-integration, non-referentiality, and emotive force. Prenominal
\mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car} retains emotive force, and with it
non-inflection and non-referentiality, but loses prosodic isolation and
syntactic non-integration together, because it's embedded inside a noun
phrase \citep[cf.][]{potts2007}. Those two go together or not at all, which
is what a coupling link predicts (Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling})
and what a bare list of six properties would leave as coincidence. A bare property list can't explain this
pattern; the links between the properties can.

Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic-matrix} makes the cluster inspectable.
Each row gives a lexical item in a grammatical environment rather than
a word family as a whole (see Section~\ref{sec:intro}), and the columns show its
profile against the six cluster properties and the three field-relative

\section{The causal-network framework}\label{sec:causal-framework}

The property cluster documented above has the shape
Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence} described: statistically reliable co-occurrence,
no necessary and sufficient conditions, disputed boundaries, and hedged
universals. That shape is what any lexical category looks like when no
definition is holding it together. The tradition
drew the natural conclusion, that a class with no definition behind it has
nothing to license, and grammars have said little accordingly
\citep[16]{HuddlestonPullum2005}. The inference doesn't hold. Membership
licenses predictions about use, and those predictions cross phonology, syntax,
semantics, interaction, and diachrony. Causal-network analysis explains why
they're available without treating the category as an essence.

\subsection{The framework}

The relations that unify a kind may be local mutual-reinforcement
relations, one-directional pathways, or converging historical routes
\citep{khalidi2013,khalidi2018nodes}. Each of
the three has work to do below: the coupling links of
Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling} are mutual reinforcement, the
non-referentiality ratchet is one-directional, and the recruitment pathways of
Section~\ref{sec:recruitment} are converging historical routes. What matters is
that the category marks a place in a causal network, a position where
properties, processes, and interventions are connected tightly enough to
support induction, which is what lets us predict properties of new instances
from observed properties of old ones.

I take the structure from Khalidi and not the metaphysics. Nothing below turns
on whether interjection is a kind in a philosopher's sense; what the category
has to be is projectible for linguistic inquiry, a position whose causal links
explain why particular observations support particular predictions. The sharp
edge assumed in Section~\ref{sec:cooccurrence} is no obstacle either, since
\textcite[66]{khalidi2013} holds that cluster kinds \enquote{need not be
structured in such a way as to allow intermediate cases}.

The four objects distinguished in Section~\ref{sec:intro} are easier to see
running on a single form. Take \mention{oof} in standalone use, as in
(\ref{ex:oof}).

\ea\label{ex:oof} \exmention{\exfocus{Oof}. That's going to hurt.}
\z

\begin{itemize}
\item The declaration is what calling it an interjection commits us to: having
  observed that it stands prosodically separate and doesn't inflect, expect it
  to resist determiners, to lack ordinary entity or event denotation, and to
  contribute use-conditionally rather than descriptively, in present-day
  English, in its standalone use rather than across the whole word family.
\item The warrant is whatever supports that expectation, and here it is thin:
  analogy to \mention{ouch} and \mention{wow}, and the links set out below.
\item The profile is the combination the declaration names. Stability and
  network order are further and independent claims: that the combination
  recurs across forms and over time, and that the properties are linked
  rather than merely co-occurring. Neither follows from one form, which is
  what Section~\ref{sec:cluster} and the links below supply.
\item Grounds explain how the form got there and why it stays.
  \textcite{oed_oof} derives \mention{oof} as an imitative formation, which
  puts its entry on the onomatopoeic route of Section~\ref{sec:recruitment}
  rather than on either bleaching path. A stabilizer would be whatever keeps the
  combination together as forms enter and leave, and corrective control would be
  a process that restored it if something pushed \mention{oof} out.
\end{itemize}

The question is how strong a causal claim the
co-occurrence documented in Section~\ref{sec:cluster} supports. Some relevant properties form local stabilizing relations. Prosodic
isolation and syntactic non-integration reinforce one another: a form
produced as its own intonation unit is less available for complementation
or modification, while a form outside clause structure is more likely to
receive independent prosodic packaging. \mention{Damn!} standing alone has
both properties; prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car} has
neither, because sitting inside a noun phrase costs it the intonational
boundary that had kept it out of the clause. Repeated use in that position
can make standalone interpretation increasingly expectable for speakers and
hearers.

Other processes are interactional or directional. Uptake affects future
use; repeated use affects expectation; expectation affects later uptake.
\mention{Mm-hm} shows the loop closing: it serves as a continuer because
hearers treat it as one, which is why producing it hands the floor back,
which is why it keeps being produced in that slot.
Loss of referential content makes argument structure less motivated;
syntactic deactivation pushes a form toward standalone use; repeated
conversational deployment entrenches stance, response, repair, or
sequence-management functions. These relations explain why the
diagnostics travel together. They establish network order, and they make
maintenance plausible.

The historical evidence adds a further point. Semantic bleaching,
syntactic deactivation, and onomatopoeic or interactional coinage can all
recruit forms into a similar pragmatic profile, but they don't do so by
the same route or with the same residues. A bleached invocation (\mention{gee}), a
deactivated optative (\mention{damn}), and an onomatopoeic or
interactional coinage (\mention{oof}, \mention{mm-hm}) may
converge on prosodic independence, supplement function,
non-referential or use-conditional meaning, and interactional force while
retaining different traces of their sources. The resulting category is
organized by causal relations with different temporal and
explanatory profiles.

That structure is enough for projectibility, whatever domain the
projections turn out to fall in (Section~\ref{sec:proj-domain}). It isn't
enough to establish corrective control, since the evidence considered here
doesn't
identify a tracked category-level variable, an error signal, or a response
pathway that restores the interjectional profile when it's perturbed
\citep[cf.][]{weinberger2026HomeostasisCausalControl}.

\subsection{Causal networks vs.\ prototype theory}\label{sec:prototype}

Prototype theory \citep{rosch1975,taylor2003} was developed for exactly
the kind of data Section~\ref{sec:cluster} documents, and it handles
that data well: graded membership, better-and-worse exemplars, and
fuzzy boundaries via overlapping similarity spaces. It also correctly
predicts that boundary disputes (interjections vs.\ fillers, vs.\
routine formulae) should recur instead of being resolvable by
definition. These are genuine explanatory achievements, and the
causal-network account doesn't replace them.

The accounts differ in what they buy that data with. Prototype theory buys it
with degrees of membership. The causal-network account doesn't need them,
because each field-relative cut is determinate and the disputes concern which
cut an inquiry should be using (Section~\ref{sec:boundaries}). That's the
source of a prediction prototype theory doesn't make: apparent gradience
should decompose under conditioning rather than persist as irreducible
fuzziness.

A causal-network account adds a different kind of question. Prototype
theory explains why borderline cases should be expected: categories are
organized around better and worse exemplars. It doesn't explain why
the properties cluster in this particular pattern or what should happen
when one of their links is disrupted.

The difference matters because the causal-network account generates
predictions about change, conditioning, and residues. It predicts that
forms recruited by different pathways should retain different traces of
their source constructions, that apparent gradience should decompose
under variety conditioning, and that some properties may be more central
to network order than others (hub-property asymmetry). These
predictions are probed for interjections in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Pathway residues provide a further discriminator. If the proposed causal
links are real, messy boundary cases shouldn't be messy in arbitrary
ways. Retained complements should point to verbal sources; euphemistic
phonological deformation should point to taboo-managed invocative
sources; regional concentration should point to regional or indexical
recruitment. Prototype theory offers no analogous retrodictive test.

\subsection{What causal structure adds}

Several analytical stances generate different predictions for
interjections. The weakest is a \term{property list}: interjections share
prosodic isolation, non-inflection, non-referentiality, and emotive
force. That's descriptively useful, but it makes no prediction about
what happens when one property changes.

Feature specifications are stronger alternatives, for example
[\textsc{+peripheral}, \textsc{-argument-taking},
\textsc{+expressive}]. This generates some of the same distributional
consequences: no inflection follows from the absence of phi-features,
and no argument-taking follows from the relevant syntactic feature
bundle. But feature specifications treat those consequences as
entailments of the feature bundle, not as effects of identifiable
processes.

A constructional account is a stronger competitor. It can represent
many of the same pairings of form, meaning, and use
\citep{goldberg1995constructions,goldberg2006,croft2001}. The
causal-network claim is compatible with that representational move. It
asks a different question: which positions in a constructional inventory
are projectible, why do their properties cohere, and which changes
should make them come apart? Construction grammar models the inventory;
the projectibility question asks where the inventory supports
cross-constructional and cross-field inference. The two main pressure
points are pathway residues and field-relative decomposition. If those
payoffs fail under further testing, the causal-network account collapses
to a descriptive inventory rather than an explanatory category analysis.

The causal-network account adds five claims that property lists, feature
bundles, and constructional representations don't settle by themselves:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Co-movement. Prosodic isolation, supplement function,
  non-referentiality, and pragmatic force recur in a patterned way. The
  account asks which links explain why observing one property licenses
  expectations about another.
\item Asymmetry. Some properties are upstream or more diagnostic than
  others. Prosodic packaging can make syntactic integration less
  available, while the reverse relation has a different status. A flat
  feature bundle can't represent that ordering.
\item Persistence. The category profile can remain available while
  individual forms enter and leave, which is a claim about stability rather
  than about what maintains it.
\item Boundary behaviour. Different recruitment pathways should leave
  different residues at the category boundary. Feature theory can list
  residual properties but doesn't explain why they align with
  particular histories.
\item Failure. When a prediction fails, the pattern of failure should
  identify which link or declared inference has broken. If \mention{fie}
  loses all three nodes together under community-wide attrition, the
  result is a boundary condition on ordered erosion rather than evidence of corrective
  control.
\end{enumerate}

If a feature-based account can replicate co-movement, asymmetry,
persistence, boundary behaviour, and failure without network order,
causal structure is doing no additional explanatory work. The empirical
pressure point is whether the probes in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
support those claims.

\subsection{Three field-relative nodes}\label{sec:fieldrelative-nodes}

The causal-network account developed by
\textcite{khalidi2013,khalidi2018nodes} also explains why
projectibility is field-relative. If kinds are nodes in causal
structures, different fields may track different nodes because they ask
different explanatory and predictive questions. The resulting categories
remain realist: each inquiry tracks the causal relations that matter for
its explanations and predictions.

The familiar linguistic parallel is the interrogative/question
distinction. Syntacticians track \term{interrogative}: a clause type
with properties such as subject--auxiliary inversion, fronting of
interrogative words, and polar-question syntax. Pragmatists track \term{question}:
a speech act that requests information, makes an answer
relevant, and organizes uptake. The two often coincide, and they also come apart:
\mention{Can you pass the salt?} is syntactically interrogative and
pragmatically a request, while \mention{I wonder where she went} can
raise an information issue without having interrogative clause syntax.

Interjections create a similar problem with less terminological help.
Syntax, semantics, and interactional pragmatics all use the
word \enquote{interjection}, but each field tracks a slightly different
node: different properties, different causal links, different
predictions. I distinguish these with subscripts:%

  the syntactic and pragmatic nodes because both terms are established

  relevant literatures: \term{expressive} from \textcite{potts2007}
  for the semantic node, and response token from
  \textcite{stivers2019} or \term{liminal sign} from

  The Latin

\item \intj{syn}, the syntactician's
  category (\textit{CGEL}, Quirk et al.). Distributional cluster: supplement

\item \intj{sem}, the semanticist's
  category, overlapping with \textcite{potts2007}'s \term{expressives}.
  Use-conditional meaning cluster: independent of at-issue content,
  nondisplaceable, perspective-dependent, descriptively ineffable
  \citep[166]{potts2007}. Often recruited through semantic bleaching.
  Different extension: prenominal \mention{damn} in
  \mention{the damn car} is \intj{sem} but not
  \intj{syn}.
\item \intj{prag}, the interactional-pragmatic

  \intj{prag} but debatably

\subsection{Recruitment pathways}\label{sec:recruitment}

Section~\ref{sec:cluster} already pointed to three recruitment
pathways: semantic bleaching from nouns, syntactic deactivation from
verbs, and nonce coinage via onomatopoeia. The first is historically
demonstrated; the second and third are proposed pathways with clear
examples but without the extended diachronic documentation that Path~1
has. The first two are analysed in detail here; the third is taken up
in Section~\ref{sec:turnover}.

The pragmatic issue is how changes in communicative use become
categorially consequential: grammaticalization and pragmaticalization
matter here because they alter a form's communicative job as well as its
position in a syntactic tree
\citep[cf.][]{TraugottDasher2002, heinekuteva2002lexicon}.

\subsection{Path 1: religious noun to interjection}\label{sec:path-religious}

\textcite{gehweiler2008} traces the development of \mention{gee} from
\mention{Jesus} through three positions:

\ea\label{ex:bleaching}
  \ea Proper name used as invocation (\mention{Jesus!} addressed to the
    deity)
  \ex Secondary interjection: exclamatory use, no longer addressing the
    deity (\mention{Jesus!} as an expression of surprise)
  \ex Primary interjection: phonological modification yields
    \mention{Jeez} and \mention{Gee}, and \mention{gee!} detaches from
    the source noun
  \z
\z
The two transitions don't work the same way, and Gehweiler is emphatic
about the difference. She explains the first as gradual and speaker-based,
through Traugott and Dasher's invited inferencing, and
\enquote{advanced by the syntactic isolation of \mention{Jesus}}
\citep[85]{gehweiler2008}. The second she treats as abrupt and
hearer-based: tabooistic clipping produced \mention{gee!}, and she insists
that this step \enquote{was not gradual} and \enquote{can therefore not be
explained with reference to Invited Inferencing or syntactic position}
\citep[88]{gehweiler2008}.
The positions layer cluster properties unevenly. Prosodic isolation is
already present at the invocation, because a vocative like
\mention{Jesus!} occupies its own intonation unit (contrast the
integrated argument in \mention{Put your faith in Jesus}). What changes
is the content: non-referentiality arrives with the secondary interjection, when the speaker
is no longer addressing the deity. Phonological divergence then
makes the etymological connection opaque, leaving a standalone interjection
that many speakers don't connect to its source.
For the first transition the vocative is the gateway, and what it supplies
is syntactic: an utterance-initial slot set apart from the rest of the
utterance, which is the isolation Gehweiler credits with enabling the
categorial change \citep[85]{gehweiler2008}. She denies that the same
explanation extends to the second.

That discontinuity is an asset for the path-dependence claim rather than
an embarrassment for it. A gradual slide would leave a form with no
particular mark of where it came from; an abrupt re-analysis leaves exactly
the kind of residue the account needs, since the phonological opacity of
\mention{gee!} is a permanent trace of the clipping that produced it. The
first transition is constrained in the way Gehweiler describes: once the
invocation loses its referential force, the phonological connection to the
source is the last link to the nominal category, and weakening that link by
phonological modification enables full independence. Gehweiler's corpus data documents the
constraint: the phonological modifications (\mention{Jeez},
\mention{Gee}, \mention{Gosh}, \mention{Golly}) cluster in the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, with the transition from
invocation to primary interjection happening relatively rapidly once the
modifications appear \citep[80--85]{gehweiler2008}.

Two cautions matter for the general claim. The boundary between
invocation and exclamation is hard to operationalize in historical
texts, since prosodic and interactional evidence is indirect. The
seventeenth- and eighteenth-century clustering of euphemistic forms
may reflect external taboo pressure as well as internal category
pressures. The causal-network claim doesn't require Gehweiler's sequence
to be an autonomous internal cline. It requires the weaker and more
historically plausible claim that social pressure, invited inference,
and reanalysis can jointly push a source noun toward the same
interjectional profile.

Two processes operate within this path.
\textcite[76]{gehweiler2008} distinguishes gradual Invited Inferencing
(speaker-based: the expressive use is conversationally implicated, then
conventionalized) from abrupt reanalysis (hearer-based: the hearer parses
the exclamation as a standalone unit rather than an invocation).
Both feed the same cluster, but they operate at different timescales and
involve different agents. Their convergence supports the network-order
claim: the same node can be reached by different routes.

The first transition generates a specific prediction: partial bleaching
should be detectable by the absence of downstream properties. A form that
has reached the secondary interjection but no further should be
non-referential while retaining the source phonology and resisting full
prosodic independence.
The prediction is testable and goes beyond historical description.

\subsection{Path 2: verb to interjection}

A second trajectory enters the cluster via syntactic deactivation rather
than phonological modification. \mention{Damn} originates as a verb, and it
enters by way of the optative, the minor clause type of \mention{God save
the Queen} and \mention{God help you}, where a main-clause subjunctive
survives in a narrow range of formulaic frames
\citep[944]{HuddlestonPullum2002}. \mention{God damn him} has that shape,
and what the interjectional use drops is that subject rather than an
understood addressee: \mention{damn him} is a wish, not an instruction.
Tense goes with the subject, while an NP complement survives in a few
constructions (\mention{damn these mosquitoes!}).
The complement retention shows that the syntactic deactivation is
partial: the form carries some verb properties into the interjection
cluster. In \textit{CGEL}'s
terms, it's the head of an IntjP, not a verb phrase (VP)
\citep[1361fn]{HuddlestonPullum2002}.

The process is decategorialization: loss of verbal morphosyntax
(subject, tense) and gain of expressive force.
As a verb, \mention{damn} contributes content meaning, the condemning itself,
and uttering it performs the act. As \intj{syn},
\mention{damn!} contributes procedural meaning, a stance of frustration.

This doesn't count as degrammaticalization in the strict sense, since the
form doesn't gain the morphosyntactic substance of a major lexical category
\citep[cf.][]{norde2009}. It's better treated as pragmatic
conventionalization with syntactic deactivation: the verbal source
loses argument-structural obligations as the interjectional use gains
procedural and interpersonal force.

For other verb-derived interjections the gateway is the imperative rather
than the optative. Imperatives also lack an overt subject and stand alone
prosodically, so the interjectional use completes a deactivation the
construction has already begun. \mention{Come on} and \mention{look} (in their
discourse-marker uses) have lost subject, tense, and complement-taking
ability. They're standalone interjections
with no residual argument structure.

\mention{Damn} shows partial syntactic
deactivation (complement retained, subject and tense lost), while
\mention{come on} and \mention{look} represent full deactivation (no
complement, no argument structure at all). The degree of deactivation
correlates with the degree of integration into the interjection cluster.

This correlation generates a graded prediction: a form that retains
complement-taking ability should also retain other verb-like properties
(e.g., prosodic integration in some constructions), while a fully
deactivated form should show the complete interjection profile. The
ordering relation predicts the inferential package available at each
stage.

\subsection{Convergent pathways}

The two paths enter the cluster from different directions, one through
phonological modification of a bleached noun, the other through syntactic
deactivation of a verb, but both exploit a gateway construction that
already provides prosodic isolation: the vocative for Path~1, and for
Path~2 the optative or the imperative. Both converge on the same property profile.
The convergence is what the causal-network account predicts: different
recruitment processes can bring items into the same projectible profile,
producing the same cluster of co-occurring properties through different
routes.

Both paths can be situated within \textcite{TraugottDasher2002}'s
subjectification trajectory (content~$>$~procedural,
nonsubjective~$>$~intersubjective), but they instantiate it differently.
Path~1 is grammaticalization in Traugott's technical sense: the invocation
carries content meaning (addressing an entity) plus subjective stance
(reverence); the secondary interjection loses content and gains procedural
meaning (expressive force); phonological modification completes the shift
to fully procedural, intersubjective meaning, with hearers interpreting the
form as a conventional expression, not an invocation. Gehweiler's own
verdict on that last step is worth keeping in view: because it turns on
hearer re-analysis rather than speaker-invited inference, it's the one
transition her account doesn't treat as subjectification
\citep[88]{gehweiler2008}. Path~2, as described above, is
decategorialization: loss of verbal morphosyntax with gain of expressive
force.

Onomatopoeia (Section~\ref{sec:turnover}) is categorically different.
Nonce coinages enter the cluster with no prior lexical history (no bleaching, no
deactivation, no subjectification). Three diachronically distinct
processes (grammaticalization, pragmaticalization, nonce coinage)
converge on the same synchronic category. Grammaticalization theory
alone can't subsume all three. A causal-network account can: the shared
point is the node they converge on, with outcomes recruited by different
processes but linked by the same synchronic ordering relations.

\subsection{Synchronic coupling}\label{sec:synchronic-coupling}

Recruitment explains how items enter the cluster. The synchronic
question is what gives the cluster network order now, and what might
keep that order stable. Four synchronic links are relevant:
prosodic-syntactic coupling, a non-referentiality ratchet,
interactional routine entrenchment, and regional/indexical conditioning.
They don't have the same status. The first two order structural
and semantic diagnostics. The third is the central stabilizer candidate
for \intj{prag}. The fourth remains a
maintenance hypothesis.

\subsubsection{Prosodic isolation and syntactic non-integration}

These two properties reinforce each other, but the evidence is asymmetric.
Prosodic separation plausibly makes
syntactic integration harder, since a form in its own intonation unit is less
available as a complement or modifier. But the corpora used below are
written, and prosodic isolation is tracked there through proxies
(Section~\ref{sec:phonology}), so they can't establish that direction.

The diachronic record does support the syntactic half.
\textcite[85]{gehweiler2008} finds that invocational \mention{Jesus}
\enquote{typically stands utterance-initially and~-- like other
interjections~-- set apart from the rest of the utterance, or even on its
own}, and argues that \enquote{only because of this syntactic isolation of
the proper name~-- and Invited Inferencing, of course~-- could the secondary
interjection \mention{Jesus!} develop from the proper name}. That's
evidence that syntactic isolation, together with invited inferencing, enabled
a categorial change, not that prosody enabled the syntax. She also limits it: as Section~\ref{sec:path-religious} noted, she takes the
step from \mention{Jesus!} to \mention{gee!} to lie outside both invited
inferencing and syntactic position \citep[88]{gehweiler2008}.

The same coupling is visible synchronically. Standalone
\mention{Damn!} has both properties: it occupies its own intonation unit
and has no role in clause structure. In \mention{I think that, damn,
we're going to be late}, \mention{damn} remains possible only as a
parenthetical supplement, set off from the \mention{that}-clause. In
\mention{the damn car}, the form loses both properties at once: it's
inside the noun phrase and receives the prosody of the phrase.

The prediction is concrete. If a novel form is reliably
packaged as its own intonation unit, it should resist complementation,
modification by ordinary phrase-internal material, and coordination with
integrated phrases. If it's pulled into a host phrase, prosodic isolation should
weaken together with \intj{syn} status.

\subsubsection{The non-referentiality ratchet}

Bleaching removes referential content, which weakens the semantic
motivation for argument structure. A form with no entity or event to
construe is less available as an argument, complement, or modifier. Once
that syntactic role is lost, prosodic isolation becomes easier to keep
in place. The result is a one-directional cascade. Once argument
structure is lost, the form occupies a different functional niche, so
ordinary pressures toward referential syntax are reduced.

This ratchet supports a different set of predictions from the
prosodic-syntactic link. A non-referential stance form should resist
definiteness, quantification, and argument position:
*\mention{the damn!}, *\mention{every ouch}, *\mention{Damn surprised
me}. It should also resist embedding as propositional content
(\mention{*She said that damn}) unless it's quoted as a form. If
referential content becomes active again, the form should move toward
noun, verb, or routine-formula regions of the network. The ratchet
keeps the form near the interjection node by blocking return
to the source category, while prosodic-syntactic coupling links the
central structural properties.

\subsubsection{Interactional routine entrenchment}

The interactional-pragmatic node needs a candidate stabilizer of its own.
Conversation repeatedly places interjectional forms in recognizable
sequential slots: continuers, repair initiators, change-of-state tokens,
stance displays, and response tokens \citep{stivers2019,dingemanse2020}.
\textcite{dingemanse2024} argues that these interactional uses are
central rather than peripheral for high-frequency interjections.

Repeated deployment in these slots favours a particular package of
properties. Two levels are in play here, and the argument needs both. On a
given occasion, a continuer such as \mention{mm-hm} works because it can be
recognized without taking over the turn or adding propositional content.
A change-of-state token such as \mention{oh} works because it can mark
an information update before the speaker builds further at-issue
content. A stance token such as \mention{bruh} works because it can
display evaluation while leaving the surrounding proposition untouched.
Across many occasions, that occasion-level fitness is supposed to accumulate,
so that the interactional routine favours short, standalone, prosodically
independent, non-referential material in the inventory at large. The
accumulation is an assumption here rather than a finding. The probes in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} bear on the inventory-level pattern, not on the
step from one level to the other.

This candidate stabilizer supports the projectibility of
\intj{prag}. If a novel form is recruited as a
minimal stance, response, repair, or backchannel resource, one should
expect turn-initial or standalone availability, independent prosodic
packaging, resistance to embedding under reported speech, and
availability for uptake as an interactional move. The same candidate
stabilizer also explains why the function can persist when category membership
weakens: a full clause such as \mention{I agree} can do agreement work,
but it lacks the reduced, non-propositional packaging that makes
\mention{mm-hm} a central \intj{prag} case.

\subsubsection{Regional/indexical conditioning}

Some interjections are sharply conditioned by region, register, or
community, and some may also do identity work. \mention{Lah} in
Singapore and Malaysia, \mention{yaar} in India and Pakistan,
\mention{haba} in Nigeria, and \mention{bruh} in African American
English \citep{louf2023} are plausible cases.

They are not, however, the same kind of case, and the difference matters for
which node the conditioning operates on. \mention{Haba} is free-standing:
\textcite{unuabonahdaniel2020} avoid the terms utterance-initial and
utterance-final for it and its Nigerian congeners \enquote{because they are
free standing and are far more detached from the utterances than PMs}
\citep[69]{unuabonahdaniel2020}. \mention{Lah} is not. It is clause-final and
prosodically dependent on its host, and this holds on either current account of
it: \textcite[447]{lim2007} reports that Gupta takes the particles to
\enquote{function as the carrier of the intonation contour of the utterance},
while the rival analysis gives them lexically-specified tone inherited from a

Regional/indexical conditioning is a maintenance hypothesis.
The open question is whether it stabilizes the cluster profile (property
co-occurrence) or individual items (inventory persistence). If it
stabilizes the profile, regional/indexical conditioning helps constitute
the node. If it stabilizes only the item, it operates as a
lexical-persistence process alongside the interjection cluster.

When a regional interjection is adopted by out-group speakers as ironic
quotation, the causal-network account predicts a contrast between
inventory persistence and profile stabilization. If out-group
\mention{lah} loses prosodic isolation or emotive force while retaining
its social-marking function, regional/indexical conditioning stabilizes
the inventory alone. If it retains the full profile, the link is part of
the causal structure of the category. This comparison remains for future
work.

The interjection cluster has two structural ordering links
(prosodic-syntactic coupling and the non-referentiality ratchet), one
interactional-pragmatic stabilizer candidate (routine entrenchment), and
one regional/indexical maintenance hypothesis. Together, the first three
explain why prosodic, syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic diagnostics
travel together synchronically. They also underwrite the probes in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: disrupting different links should produce
different patterns of residue, conditioning, or erosion.

\subsection{Causal residues at the category boundary}\label{sec:residues}

The interjection cluster is a convergence point, but convergence doesn't
erase history. Forms recruited through different pathways can share the
same pragmatic profile while retaining traces of the source construction
that brought them there. Those traces explain why the category is both
recognizable and persistently irregular. The irregularities are pathway
signatures rather than noise around a prototype.

This gives the category a second kind of inference. Section~\ref{sec:projectibility}
develops forward projectibility: observing some properties of a novel
form licenses predictions about other properties. Historical pragmatics
also needs a retrodictive counterpart to projectibility:
observing a residual property can license an inference about the pathway
that produced it. A causal-network account can make that inference
because it links properties to the recruitment pathways of
Section~\ref{sec:recruitment}, ordering relations, and source
constructions.

leaves syntactic traces.

When a community stops actively using a form, evidence for the pathway

\subsection{Stability through turnover}\label{sec:turnover}

Pathway residues record how interjectional forms entered the network. Turnover shows the complementary fact: the interjection
profile can remain available while its lexical inventory changes.
Individual forms enter and leave, but the broad profile remains
available for new material.

The Corpus of Historical American English (COHA) illustrates this
turnover. In the 1820s the six most frequent interjection lemmas are
\mention{no}, \mention{oh}, \mention{yes}, \mention{nay}, \mention{o},
and \mention{ah}. By the 2010s half that list has gone: \mention{nay},
\mention{o}, and \mention{ah} are replaced by \mention{yeah},
\mention{hey}, and \mention{uh}, while \mention{no}, \mention{oh}, and
\mention{yes} hold their places throughout (Figure~\ref{fig:turnover}).
\mention{Nay} falls from 175 tokens per million to 2.2; \mention{yeah},
absent from the 1820s altogether, rises from nothing to 723.%

The items change while the projectible category position remains

Regional variation shows the profile's availability under different
local inventories.
The GloWbE corpus \citep[1.9~billion words across 20 English-speaking
countries]{daviesfuchs2015} shows that candidate interjectional forms
are distributed unevenly across varieties, with \mention{lah} almost
exclusively in Singapore and Malaysia, \mention{yaar} in India and
Pakistan, and \mention{haba} in Nigeria. The corpus evidence establishes
distributional conditioning; the linguistic analysis identifies the
shared profile that makes those forms comparable.

The cluster is also continuously replenished by onomatopoeic coinage. Two
clarifications, because \term{onomatopoeia} is often handled as though it
named a category alongside noun and verb. It doesn't. It names a relation
between a form and a sound, and forms standing in that relation are spread
across categories: \mention{bang} is a noun in \mention{a loud bang}, a
verb in \mention{he banged the door}, and an interjection in
\mention{Bang! You're dead}. So the claim isn't that a category of
onomatopoeias gets absorbed into the interjections. It's that imitation is
one of the routes by which new forms arrive at the interjectional profile.

Nor is the route closed. The stock of imitable sounds isn't fixed by the
stock of animals: devices supply \mention{beep}, \mention{ping}, and
\mention{whirr}, and any sound can be re-rendered, which is how comics gave
English \mention{bam} and \mention{pow} beside older forms. Nonce
interjections can be freely coined \citep[74]{quirk1985}, and new coinages
immediately adopt the cluster profile: they're prosodically isolated,
non-referential, emotively laden, and syntactically non-integrated.

This generates a testable prediction: a newly conventionalized
interjectional use should exhibit the full cluster profile without
having traversed the documented noun-to-interjection or
verb-to-interjection paths. Take \mention{bruh}. \textcite{oed_bruh} records it as a noun from 1894, as a
title before a man's name, and from 1967 as a familiar form of address, where
every quotation is vocative (\mention{Hey, bruh}, \mention{Be cool, bruh}). The
standalone use addressed to nobody is later, and the entry doesn't record it as
an interjection at all. Its rise shows up in COCA, from 0.01 per million in
1990--94 to 1.09 in 2015--19.\footnote{Tokens sit overwhelmingly in television
  and film (1.44 per million) and are absent from the spoken section, so the
  record is scripted representation of speech rather than recorded speech. COHA
  has the form absent before the 1980s and nine tokens in all. Figures are raw
  string frequencies; see \path{analysis/hand-extracted/}.} That use is
prosodically isolated, non-inflecting, non-referential, and syntactically
non-integrated, even though the same word family includes the nominal lexemes
just described. The category position remains
available even as its members turn over.

\textcite{dingemanse2020} characterizes such items as liminal signs,
signs at the boundary between sound and speech, whose liminality is
functional, not defective. Cultural evolutionary processes \enquote{sample
the landscape of possible resources} \citep[191]{dingemanse2020}. The
causal-network account describes that landscape as a property space
structured by the recruitment pathways and synchronic links above.

The recruited, ordered, and replenished cluster is now ready for the
paper's central inferential question: what can partial evidence license?

\section[Projectibility]{Projectibility: what the category lets you predict}\label{sec:projectibility}

Section~\ref{sec:cluster} showed what each property, individually, lets
you infer. Section~\ref{sec:causal-structure} identified the ordering
and stabilizing links that couple those properties. This section asks a
different question: what does the package license, and for whom?
Section~\ref{sec:residues}
treated retrodictive inference from residue to recruitment history; here
projectibility means forward inference from observed properties of
a form to properties not yet observed.

That formulation matters for the question of \emph{whose} inference is at
issue. Speakers and hearers use partial cues to anticipate how a form will
behave before they've observed its full distribution, and they do so without
holding the category label; analysts run the same inference explicitly, from
category membership, for field-specific purposes.
Section~\ref{sec:proj-partial} separates the two.

This section first shows how partial observations support participant
inference, then how the same structure yields different predictions for
syntax, semantics, and interactional pragmatics, and finally what fixes the
domain those predictions fall in. As in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, bare \enquote{interjection}
continues to refer to forms in the extension of all three field-relative nodes
unless a subscript specifies otherwise.

The running case is the word family \mention{damn}. Standalone
interjectional \mention{damn!}, complement-taking \mention{damn these
mosquitoes!}, and prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}
belong to the same word family. They needn't share a lexeme.

\subsection{Partial-observation inference}\label{sec:proj-partial}

Projectibility becomes circular if it says only this: if X is an
interjection, it has interjection properties. That's trivially true of
any category. Causal-network projectibility is different. Observing some
cluster properties in a novel form licenses inference to other
properties you haven't yet observed.

Consider the standalone interjectional use of \mention{bruh}, a
recently conventionalized form. A learner or hearer encountering that
use for the first time observes a partial package: standalone position,
prosodic isolation, morphological bareness, and stance meaning. From
those observations, the three field-relative nodes license a battery of
further expectations:

\ea\label{ex:bruh}
  \ea \intj{syn}: the interjectional use
    won't inflect within that use (*\mention{bruhs}, *\mention{bruhed}),
    will resist syntactic integration (*\mention{I think that bruh,
    we're late}), and won't take a determiner or function as an argument
    (*\mention{the bruh}, *\mention{Bruh surprised me}). This leaves
    room for separate nominal or verbal lexemes in the same word family.
  \ex \intj{prag}: the form should be available for
    turn-initial or standalone interactional use.
  \ex \intj{sem}: its expressive meaning is
    expected to be use-conditional and speaker-oriented.
  \z
\z
These expectations vary in strength, and the asymmetry matters:

\begin{itemize}
\item Near-entailments (a): the
  \intj{syn} predictions mostly extrapolate
  within the same domain from observed distributional facts. Any
  framework recognizing the relevant morphosyntactic properties
  generates many of the same predictions. These are real but not specific
  to the causal-network account.
\item Cross-domain inferences (b, c): the
  \intj{prag} prediction (interactional-pragmatic
  distribution from phonological form) and the
  \intj{sem} prediction (use-conditional meaning
  from stance expression) involve genuine leaps across domains. These are specific to the causal-network account: they rely on
  the cluster's internal links, not on any single property.
\end{itemize}
All three are licensed because identifiable links order the cluster's
properties. The non-referentiality ratchet
(Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}) links loss of referential content with
syntactic non-integration. Prosodic-syntactic coupling then links
syntactic non-integration with prosodic isolation. The inferences are
grounded in network order, not in definitions.

Who does the projecting, though? A hearer meeting \mention{Bummer!} or
\mention{groovy} for the first time doesn't run a taxonomy and then read
off consequences. So it's worth separating two levels that the argument so
far has run together.

\term{Category-driven projection} is the analyst's. Given that a form
belongs to the interjection cluster, expect the linked properties. That's
projectibility in Goodman's sense, claimed throughout the preceding
sections. \term{Cue-driven inference} is the participant's. The
\mention{bruh} case above describes it without naming it: what the hearer
has is standalone position, prosodic isolation, morphological bareness, and
stance meaning, from which the stance reading follows. No category
label need be represented for the reading to go through.

The two aren't rivals. Cue-driven inference is reliable \emph{because} the cluster is
coupled: the links in Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling} are what make a
partial package informative about the rest. Analyst and participant are two
consumers of one causal structure, drawing on it for different purposes and
with different explicitness. So \mention{Bummer!} and \mention{groovy} tell
against an account on which the category label does the inferential work,
and for one on which the coupling does. The label is the analyst's
bookkeeping device for a structure participants exploit without naming.

Fields differ in which links they track
(Section~\ref{sec:proj-fieldrelative}); analyst and participant differ in
whether the category figures in the inference at all. The two indices are
independent. Nothing here says that speakers tacitly represent
interjection-hood, which the account doesn't require.

\subsection{What each node projects}\label{sec:proj-fieldrelative}

The three interjection nodes established in
Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes} do their work as different packages of
inference, not as rival definitions.

\intj{syn} projects syntactic behaviour:
supplement function, resistance to \mention{that}-complementation,
resistance to coordination with non-interjections, and no ordinary
argument structure. These predictions are grounded in the
prosodic-syntactic coupling link from
Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}. \intj{sem}
projects meaning type, and here the projection has to reach past the
properties that admitted the form. Independence, nondisplaceability,
perspective dependence, and descriptive ineffability are what
Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes} uses to assign the node, so
predicting them would only restate membership.
\textcite{potts2007} lists two further properties of expressive content on
the following page, and neither does admission work here. One is immediacy,
where expressives \enquote{achieve their intended act simply by being
uttered}. The other is repeatability, where repeated use has an effect
\enquote{of strengthening the emotive content, rather than one of
redundancy} \citep[167]{potts2007}. Both are testable and both can fail.
\mention{Damn, damn, damn} intensifies where \mention{The car broke, the car
broke, the car broke} doesn't, and a form that flattened into redundancy
under repetition would defeat the prediction. \intj{prag}
projects interactional-pragmatic behaviour, and the same discipline applies.
Turn-initial or standalone positioning and stance display are admission
criteria. What they let us predict is uptake: that the form will be treated
as a complete move, so that a recipient's next turn responds to it rather
than waiting for its continuation, and that it will be usable as a
backchannel without taking the floor. Both are observable in sequence, and a
form routinely treated as an incomplete turn would defeat them.

The nodes overlap because some links, especially prosodic isolation
and non-referentiality, contribute to more than one node. They come
apart because other links are field-specific. A novel form categorized
as \intj{syn} (prosodically isolated,
non-inflecting, syntactically non-integrated) should tend also to be
\intj{sem} (use-conditional, speaker-oriented)
and \intj{prag} (turn-initial, available for
backchanneling). Central cases such as \mention{wow}, \mention{ouch},
and \mention{hey} fit that default. Boundary cases locate the decoupling, \mention{mm-hm} among them
\citep{oconnell2005}.

The three constructions of \mention{damn} show the same point inside a
single word family.

For standalone
\mention{damn!}, all three nodes agree: the form is prosodically
isolated, syntactically non-integrated, use-conditional, and available
as a stance marker. For \mention{damn these mosquitoes!}, the picture
is mixed: \intj{syn} holds (it heads an IntjP)
but with limited complement-taking;
\intj{sem} holds (the meaning is expressive);
\intj{prag} is weakened (it's not a backchannel or
turn-management device).

For prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}, the nodes
diverge further: \intj{syn} fails entirely (the
form is syntactically integrated inside a noun phrase), but
\intj{sem} holds (use-conditional, perspective-
dependent). Neither \textit{CGEL}'s syntax nor Potts's semantics alone
predicts this gradient. The three-node analysis does, because it tracks
which links are active in each construction.

\subsection{Category vs.\ function}

\textit{CGEL}'s distinction between category and function sharpens the
projectibility point: what does the \intj{syn} category label add
beyond the supplement function label? Supplements of any category
(noun phrases [NPs], adjective phrases [AdjPs], relative clauses) share
prosodic isolation and syntactic
non-integration. But \intj{syn} adds a package of
predictions that no other category-in-supplement-function supports:

\begin{itemize}
\item Non-referentiality. Supplements can be referential
  (\mention{a university lecturer, Dr Brown, \ldots}); interjections
  characteristically can't.
\item Emotive/interpersonal force. Not all supplements express
  stance (\mention{Dr Brown} expresses none); interjections
  characteristically do (\mention{ugh}, \mention{yay}).
\item Free coinage. No other category in supplement function
  can be freely coined via onomatopoeia. Comics gave English
  \mention{bam} and \mention{pow}; nothing comparable mints new
  relative clauses.
\item Non-inflection. NP supplements inflect freely
  (\mention{a lecturer}, \mention{two lecturers}); interjections don't
  (*\mention{ouches}).
\end{itemize}

The function label tells you about syntactic integration. The category
label tells you about inference: it licenses a package of predictions
that the function label alone can't provide. The category/function distinction matters
beyond the \textit{CGEL} tradition: any framework that distinguishes what kind of
thing an expression is from what job it's doing in a particular clause
will face the same question. The causal-network answer is that the
category earns its keep by licensing inferences that the function
doesn't.

\subsection{What sets the domain of projection}\label{sec:proj-domain}

Categories don't all project in the same domain, and what fixes the domain
is where the causal links run. Nouns project morphosyntactically:
\mention{lecturer} takes a determiner, inflects for number, and fills an
argument slot. Categorizing something as a noun tells you almost nothing
about its pragmatics, since nouns aren't a pragmatic category. Interjections
come out the other way, and the reason is the same reason.

For interjections, the situation is reversed. The three nodes overlap,
and the richest projectibility belongs to
\intj{prag}, the interactional-pragmatic
cluster.
\textcite{dingemanse2024} reaches the same conclusion from interactional
data: the most frequent and functionally central interjections are
interactional tools (continuers, repair initiators, change-of-state
tokens) rather than emotive exclamations, and their primary
projectibility is about sequential position and conversational
management. For forms at the intersection of all three nodes, the strongest inferences
are about use, not denotation. \mention{Oh} shows the
asymmetry. From \intj{syn} you get what any
supplement gives you: prosodic isolation, no inflection, no argument structure.
From \intj{prag} you get a turn position,
availability as a response token, and the display of evolving knowledge
described in Section~\ref{sec:pragmatics}. Same form, and the two nodes differ
in how much they buy.

The contrast with nouns and verbs concerns where the density lies, not how
much structure each node carries.
\intj{syn} is thin next to those nodes, lacking
the network of agreement, argument realization, derivation, and inflectional
behaviour that makes them so stable. What the three nodes divide between them
is the kind of prediction each supports, set out in
Section~\ref{sec:proj-fieldrelative}.

Why pragmatic in this case? Because causal links generate predictions
within the domains they operate in. The prosodic-syntactic coupling from
Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}
predicts prosodic and syntactic behaviour, with no corresponding
prediction about morphological paradigms or truth-conditional content.
Regional/indexical conditioning predicts uneven persistence across
varieties, which is why \mention{lah} and \mention{yaar} are entrenched in some
Englishes and absent from others, with no corresponding prediction about
inflectional regularity. The strongest established links among the three
interjection nodes are prosodic and interactional; the regional or
indexical link remains a maintenance hypothesis. These links and
hypotheses underwrite predictions in their respective domains.

The same point holds outside interjections. Noun projectibility is
morphosyntactic because the underlying processes (agreement, structural
analogy, discourse frequency) operate in morphosyntactic domains.

This connects to the content/procedural distinction. The central
projectibility of interjections is procedural and use-conditional rather
than denotational \citep{TraugottDasher2002}. The
\intj{sem} (expressive) cluster is the semantic
reflex: use-conditional meaning is the semantic signature of procedural
content. The same pattern shows up elsewhere. \term{Animacy} projects grammatically (argument realization,
reference tracking); \term{colour} projects ecologically (edibility,
toxicity) but usually doesn't project grammatically in the way animacy
can \citep[cf.][]{seifart2010}. In each case, the domain of
projectibility follows from the underlying processes.

The richest projectibility is pragmatic, but
\intj{syn} still belongs in the grammar
because it's projectible and has its own causal links. Neutralize
prosodic isolation and the form is reclassified, which is what happens to
prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}. The syntactic category
licenses distributional inference: it predicts supplement function,
resistance to embedding under reported speech (*\mention{She said that damn}),
and coordination restrictions, even if those predictions are less rich than the
pragmatic ones. A thin syntactic node is still a projectible syntactic
node. The framework requires reliable inference, and
\intj{syn} does.

Section~\ref{sec:projectibility} asked what categorizing a novel form as
an interjection lets you predict about that form. At the category level,
the causal-network account should also explain patterns across the
interjectional region as a whole. As Section~\ref{sec:prototype} argued, what
the causal-network account adds to what prototype theory already accommodates
is network-order predictions with named defeat conditions. The probes
below address profile stability, boundary behaviour, and failure. They
aren't tests of corrective control.

The strongest probe is pathway residue. If interjections are recruited by
different causal pathways, residual properties should line up with
recruitment history. The defeat condition is random distribution:
retained complements, euphemistic deformation, atypical phonology, and
regional skew should show no relation to source pathways. The evidence
in Section~\ref{sec:residues} supports the residue prediction, and the
pre-registered morphology-gain study adds an exit-residue case:
\mention{wowed}, \mention{booed}, \mention{oohed}, and \mention{shooed}
gain syntactic integration while retaining source-interjection semantics.
The payoff is pragmatic and diachronic: boundary failures diagnose
recruitment routes and explain why later uses keep source-specific
residues.

A second probe asks whether conditioning recovers structure. Apparent
gradation at the interjection boundary should decompose when conditioned
on register, dialect, or community. The defeat condition is parity
between conditioned and pooled models. A proof-of-concept probe uses
GloWbE frequency data \citep{daviesfuchs2015}: for the 10 most frequent
interjection-tagged items plus regional and boundary items,
frequency-per-million was extracted by country (20 countries) by raw
string search, and the coefficient of variation (CV; standard deviation
divided by the mean) was computed across countries.

The results show three tiers of geographic conditioning. Regional
interjections (\mention{lah}, \mention{yaar}, \mention{haba}) are
near-categorical: \mention{lah} occurs at 9.35 per million in Malaysia
and 6.72 in Singapore but near zero elsewhere; \mention{yaar} at 2.88
in Pakistan and 2.33 in India but near zero elsewhere; \mention{haba}
at 8.78 in Nigeria but near zero elsewhere.

Core interjections show
intermediate but structured variation: \mention{ah} has the highest
CV among core items (0.71), with Singapore (60.42) and Jamaica (53.16)
at eight times the rate of Kenya (7.62). Even broadly distributed items
like \mention{yeah} (CV 0.55) show a 6:1 ratio between the United
States (103.78) and Tanzania (16.85). Only \mention{yes} and
\mention{no} approach uniformity (CVs of 0.21--0.22). Boundary items
continue the pattern: the fillers \mention{um} (CV 0.61) and
\mention{uh} (CV 0.76) and the routine formula \mention{bye} (CV 0.61)
all show structured geographic variation.

These coefficients are estimated from twenty countries, so they carry more
uncertainty than a bare figure suggests, and it's worth saying which
comparisons survive it. Bootstrapping over the twenty sections, the
uniformity of \mention{yes} and \mention{no} is the firm result: their
intervals sit below eleven of the thirteen other core items, overlapping only
\mention{hello} and \mention{ha}. The ordering within the rest of the core
band isn't a result at all. No two of those items have separable intervals,
so \mention{uh} at 0.76 isn't distinguishable from \mention{yeah} at 0.55,
and the figures should be read as a single undifferentiated band rather than
a ranking. For the regional items the coefficient is the wrong summary
altogether: with a form absent from eighteen of twenty sections the mean sits
near zero and the coefficient becomes unstable, so \mention{lah}'s interval runs from 0.89 to 4.27. Their concentration is
better read off the counts than off a dispersion statistic.

Conditioning on country recovers structure at every level of this
sample. Variety structures the apparent gradience between core
interjections, fillers, and boundary items.

A Bayesian multilevel model (negative binomial with crossed random
effects for item and country) supports the same point. Adding country as
a grouping factor improves prediction over an item-only baseline: the
country-level random-effect standard deviation is 0.44 (95\% credible
interval [CrI]: 0.28--0.65), and the conditioned model outperforms the
unconditional model by 18 expected log predictive density (ELPD) units
(standard error [SE] 13) on exact leave-one-out cross-validation.

The descriptive CV results are the primary evidence; the model shows
that the pattern survives a crossed random-effects structure. Full model
specification and results are in the
supplementary materials.\footnote{\ifblind Available at
  [link removed for blind review].\else Available at
  \url{https://github.com/BrettRey/interjections-hpc}.\fi}
Several methodological limitations remain. GloWbE's
part-of-speech tagger, trained on inner-circle English, fails to tag
regional interjections (\mention{yaar} and \mention{haba} receive no
interjection tag at all), which means the tagged frequencies
underestimate regional items. The \mention{ha} row needs a criterion
rather than a caveat, and the criterion has to be settled before the figure
rather than after it. Iterated \mention{HA! HA!} is a rendering of laughter,
closer to eye dialect than to a lexical item; \mention{ha} used for sarcasm or
scepticism is an interjectional use. The line is harder to draw in a written
corpus than a spoken one, since nothing was vocalized for the spelling to
represent, and iterated \mention{haha} in digital writing has arguably
conventionalized into a form in its own right. Nothing here rests on a claim
that imitative forms fall outside the category, which would contradict the
recruitment route described above.

Hand-coding the tagged concordance for all twenty sections shows how much
turns on that one decision, and the figures reported above count iterated
laughter rather than excluding it. Laughter is 51.5\% of tagged \mention{ha}
in the United States and 63.0\% in Great Britain, so the two readings are far
apart: across six defensible codings of the laughter and residual-token
questions, \mention{ha}'s coefficient of variation runs from 0.40 to 0.83.
A single figure would report one path through that set rather than the set
\citep[cf.][]{gelmanloken2014}.

Two results should be kept apart, because one survives the spread and the
other doesn't. The dispersion statistic doesn't: it isn't identified without
the criterion decision, and the 0.41 above corresponds to counting laughter.
The ranking does. Coding also shows what proportion of tagged tokens are
interjections at all, which runs from 34.5\% in Bangladesh to 84.0\% in Great
Britain, with \term{hectare} the contaminant in East Africa and South Asia and
personal names in Singapore, where only 6.1\% is \term{hectare}. Correcting
each section by its own rate moves Kenya from fifth place among the twenty to
fifteenth, and it does so under every one of the six codings. Pooling the
twenty rates to allow for their unequal precision, which runs from about 8,100
tagged tokens for the United States down to 420 for Ghana, moves no section by
more than 0.6 percentage points, because the variance between sections is much
larger than the sampling variance within them.

Country differences in GloWbE may also reflect corpus composition,
genre mix, internet ecology, and orthographic convention as much as
dialect or community structure. A spelling-variant check bears the last of
these out, in an unexpected direction. \mention{Ya} and \mention{yeh} look
like variants of \mention{yeah} that a single-string query would miss, but
both concentrate where they're homographs of substrate words:
\mention{ya} peaks in Tanzania (19.5 per million) and Malaysia (13.9),
\mention{yeh} in Singapore (5.6), Pakistan (4.9), and India (4.9), and no
\mention{yeh} token in the corpus carries the interjection tag at all.
Folding both strings into \mention{yeah} would drop its dispersion from a
CV of 0.55 to 0.46, but only by importing non-English tokens unevenly across
varieties, so the figures below keep the strings apart. Lengthened spellings
point the same way and put a number on it. Summing \mention{umm},
\mention{ummm}, and \mention{ummmm} into \mention{um} moves its dispersion
from a CV of 0.61 to 0.60 once Pakistan is set aside, so lengthening is
negligible in general; including Pakistan it rises to 0.72, because Pakistani
English returns nearly eight times more \mention{umm} than \mention{um}
where the median variety returns a third as much. A twenty-token sample
settles what that signature is: nineteen are the Arabic teknonym
\mention{Umm}, as in \mention{Umm Salama}, drawn from hadith and biographical
material, and the twentieth mentions the filler rather than using it. Raw
strings are weakest, then, exactly where a variety's substrate supplies a
homograph, which is a sharper and more testable caveat than orthographic
variation at large, and one that bears on the varieties this section
compares. The regional items carry the most exposure, since they're
substrate-derived to begin with. Hand-coding sixty-nine \mention{yaar}
tokens puts the English-matrix rate at roughly 60\% in Pakistan and 65\% in
India, the remainder being Tamil \mention{yaar} \enquote{who}, proper names,
and tokens sitting inside Roman-script Hindi or Urdu. The rates are estimated
from twenty-token samples and are too coarse to correct with, but they're
large enough that raw-string figures for these items should be read as upper
bounds. Raw strings are imperfect proxies too:
\mention{yes}, \mention{no}, \mention{ha}, \mention{ah},
\mention{oh}, \mention{well}, \mention{right}, and \mention{like} are
not automatically interjection tokens. Some are clausal, quoted,
discourse-marker uses, literary representations, or noise. The
methodological payoff is that conditioning by variety reveals structure
that pooled category labels hide.

A third, weaker probe concerns hub-property asymmetry. One property may
be more central to network order than others, with prosodic isolation
and non-referentiality as the main candidates. The defeat condition is
equal contribution by all properties. The present evidence leaves this
prediction open.

Onomatopoeic interjections like \mention{pop} can be
syntactically integrated into clause structure; when prosodic isolation
is neutralized, as when \mention{pop} in \mention{pop went the cork}
is an adverb rather than a standalone interjection
\citep[152]{meinard2015}, reclassification is immediate. Restoring
partial referentiality, by contrast, allows the form to hover between
noun and interjection uses (as with \mention{God}). This suggests
prosodic isolation may be the hub, but the evidence is indirect.

The probes differ in evidential weight. Pathway residue is the main
explanatory payoff, conditioning by variety is a proof of concept, and
hub-property asymmetry is an open conjecture. Prototype theory describes
the gradience but doesn't predict the trajectory of entry, the
pathway-specific structure of boundary cases, or the structure hidden in
apparent variation.

At the framework level, if a feature-based account
(e.g., [\textsc{+peripheral}, \textsc{-argument-taking},
\textsc{+expressive}]) generates the same predictions without causal
structure, the network account would be a relabelling. The
causal-network payoffs are pathway residues and the field-relative
decomposition; if both fail under further testing, the account collapses to a descriptive
property list.

\subsection{Boundary disputes as causal-link overlap}\label{sec:boundaries}

The boundary disputes that embarrassed classical category theory
(interjections vs.\ nouns, verbs, adverbs, fillers, routine formulae)
are field-relative predictions of the causal-network account. Each
boundary reflects partial causal-link overlap and differences in what a
field needs the category to predict.

Routine formulae (\mention{bye}, \mention{hello}, \mention{sorry}) share
prosodic isolation and pragmatic function with interjections but add
addressee-directedness and social predictability
\citep[109--110]{ameka1992}. For grammar, those shared properties place
them near interjections. For speech-act analysis, the additional
properties matter more, because they predict conventional response slots,
addressees, and felicity conditions. They're structured by additional
processes (social convention, politeness norms) that prototypical
interjections don't require.

Fillers (\mention{um}, \mention{uh}) share prosodic distinctiveness and
syntactic non-integration but differ in emphasis, pause distribution,
and citation-introduction ability \citep{oconnell2005}. They occupy a
neighbouring region of the property space, structured by partially
overlapping links (processing-pressure management rather than
emotive expression).

The Ameka-Wilkins debate can be reframed in these terms. Ameka's
separation is right for a field that needs to predict social routines and
speech-act felicity \citep[109]{ameka1992}. Wilkins's inclusion is right
for a field that needs to track pragmatic and semantic overlap with
interjections \citep[142]{wilkins1992}. A causal-network account accommodates both
observations because projectibility is indexed to explanatory purpose.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

English interjections are a category whose members support projectible
inferences about use. Assigning a form to it lets an analyst predict
prosodic packaging, supplement function, use-conditional stance,
sequential position, response function, and uptake, and the coupling that
licenses those predictions is what lets speakers and hearers anticipate the
same behaviour from partial cues. The category is thin as syntax but rich
as pragmatics.

The reason is that the familiar interjection profile is stable and
causally ordered. Diachronic pathways recruit forms into the
profile: grammaticalization via semantic bleaching, pragmaticalization
via syntactic deactivation, and nonce coinage via onomatopoeia.
Synchronic links then structure the profile: prosodic-syntactic
coupling, the non-referentiality ratchet, interactional routine
entrenchment, and possibly regional/indexical conditioning. These links
explain why partial observations support further inferences.

The field-relative decomposition shows why the traditional label
is useful but not uniform. Syntax, semantics, and interactional
pragmatics track overlapping nodes with different predictive payoffs.
Boundary cases such as \mention{damn these mosquitoes!}, prenominal
\mention{damn}, fillers, routine formulae, and obsolete \mention{fie}
aren't embarrassing exceptions; they show which links are active and
which have weakened.

The broader pragmatic payoff is path dependence. Interjections don't
merely express emotion; they enter utterances with histories that shape
stance, sequence, and uptake. \mention{Gee}, \mention{damn},
\mention{bruh}, \mention{wowed}, and \mention{fie} matter because they
show projectible profiles, boundary behaviour, exit residues, and
failure conditions. They show how a category with no classical essence can support pragmatic
inference.

The account isn't specific to interjections. Wherever properties cluster
without a definition holding them, the same questions arise: which links are
active, what recruited the members, and what happens when a link fails.

The evidence supports a bounded declaration, a stable profile, and network
order, and it motivates candidate grounds without settling them. It doesn't
establish corrective control. That limit matters: pragmatic categories can be
stable and inductively useful without being maintained by a
perturbation-sensitive control system. English interjections are one
such category.

\section[Supplementary materials]{Supplementary materials: GloWbE conditioning analysis}
\label{app:conditioning}

\subsection{Data}

Frequency counts for 18 items were extracted from the GloWbE corpus
\citep{daviesfuchs2015}: 1.9 billion words across 20 countries. Items
include the 10 most frequent interjection-tagged forms (\mention{yes},
\mention{no}, \mention{oh}, \mention{yeah}, \mention{hi}, \mention{hey},
\mention{wow}, \mention{hello}, \mention{ah}, \mention{ha}), three
regional interjections (\mention{lah}, \mention{yaar}, \mention{haba}),
the semi-regional \mention{eh}, the boundary items \mention{cheers} and
\mention{bye}, and the fillers \mention{um} and \mention{uh}.

Figure~\ref{fig:country-variation} displays raw frequency per million
words by country for each item.

These counts are a model-dependent measurement rather than a transparent one:
a tagger stands between the text and the number, so the figures carry the
tagger's error as well as the corpus's composition. A model-dependent outcome
has to be evaluated by variety and not only on average, since an error rate
that holds overall but shifts across the varieties being compared is the
measurement analogue of a trend that isn't parallel
\citep{reynolds2026didcorpus}. The audit reported below is that evaluation.

One limit on it should be stated plainly. Hand-coding a tagged concordance
measures precision, the share of tagged tokens that are genuine, and says
nothing about recall, the share of genuine tokens the tagger found.
\mention{Haba} shows recall can be zero: 373 Nigerian tokens, none tagged. So
the corrected figures below are corrected for false positives only, and a
section whose genuine tokens the tagger largely missed would look clean on
this check while still being wrong.

Two features of the corpus constrain what country conditioning can mean here.
Country is a property of the page rather than of a speaker, assigned through
Google's region function: for a site with a top-level country domain the
assignment is direct, but for a \texttt{.com} or \texttt{.org} site
\textcite[4]{daviesfuchs2015} report that Google \enquote{relies on several
heuristics, including 1) the IP address for the web server, 2) who links to
that website, and 3) who visits the website}, and they raise the question
themselves of \enquote{how well Google has correctly identified web sites by
country}. The page list was also seeded by running \enquote{hundreds of very
high frequency 3-grams (three word strings)} from the Corpus of Contemporary
American English against Google \citep[4]{daviesfuchs2015}, so the sampling
frame is anchored to American frequency data.

The counts aren't all of one kind either, and the reason is worth stating
because it constrains what the comparison can show. English-Corpora.org tags
automatically, and the tagger handles regionalisms poorly. Counts for the core
items are restricted to the interjection tag, but restriction isn't the same as
exclusion. Hand-coding twenty tagged \mention{ha} tokens from Kenya and twenty
from Tanzania leaves six and eight respectively as interjections; the rest are
almost all the abbreviation for \term{hectare}, in agricultural and development
prose. On that basis \mention{ha} in Kenya falls from 24.9 tokens per million
to roughly 7.5, and from fifth place among the twenty countries to nineteenth.
So the tag narrows a count without cleaning it, and it fails worst where a
homograph belongs to a text type a region produces more of.

Counts for \mention{haba} and \mention{yaar} can't be tag-restricted at all,
because
neither string is ever assigned that tag: all 373 Nigerian tokens of
\mention{haba} are tagged as common or proper nouns, 65\% of them on a reading
that includes the proper noun. The regional figures are raw strings
where the core figures are tag-restricted, which inflates the regional items
relative to the items they're set beside, though by less than it would if the
tag were reliable. The size of that inflation is indicated by
\textcite{unuabonahdaniel2020}, who work on \mention{haba} in the same
Nigerian component and report 248 tokens against the 373 a raw query returns.
They reach the smaller figure by inspecting each hit: their data were
\enquote{manually searched in order to eliminate cases in which the BIs were
found in utterances made in Nigerian Pidgin}, in an indigenous Nigerian
language, or in posts repeated by copying \citep[69]{unuabonahdaniel2020}.
Two of those three exclusions are invisible to a frequency query, and the
third, duplication, is a known property of the corpus. That's a limit imposed by the tagger rather than a choice: a
form has to be known to the tagger before it can be tagged, and a borrowed
interjection in a postcolonial variety is exactly what an automatic tagger
trained elsewhere will miss.

\subsection{Model specification}

Four Bayesian negative binomial models were fitted using
\texttt{brms}~2.23 in R, with 4 chains of 2{,}000 iterations (1{,}000
warmup) each. The confirmatory models (m0--m2) use exact reloo for
leave-one-out cross-validation; the exploratory model (m3) is assessed
by Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS) diagnostics only.

\begin{quote}
\small
\noindent\texttt{m0} (unconditional):\\
\texttt{count \textasciitilde{} 1 + offset(log(words\_m)) + (1~|~item)}

\noindent\texttt{m1} (conditioned):\\
\texttt{count \textasciitilde{} 1 + offset(log(words\_m)) +}\\
\texttt{(1~|~item) + (1~|~country)}

\noindent\texttt{m2} (item type):\\
\texttt{count \textasciitilde{} 1 + item\_type + offset(log(words\_m)) +}\\
\texttt{(1~|~item) + (1~|~country)}

\noindent\texttt{m3} (exploratory):\\
\texttt{count \textasciitilde{} 1 + item\_type + offset(log(words\_m)) +}\\
\texttt{(1~|~item) + (1~+~item\_type~||~country)}
\end{quote}

Model m3 estimates country-level varying slopes by item type. Because
several item-type cells are sparse (semi-regional: 1 item, boundary: 2,
filler: 2, regional: 3), these slopes are thinly supported and m3 is
treated as exploratory.

\subsection{Results}

Exact leave-one-out cross-validation (confirmatory models):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
  \hline
  Model & ELPD diff & SE diff \\
  \hline
  m1 (conditioned) & 0.0 & 0.0 \\
  m2 (item type) & $-$9.3 & 7.9 \\
  m0 (unconditional) & $-$17.9 & 13.3 \\
  \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The conditioned model (m1) is preferred. Adding item-type fixed effects
(m2) doesn't improve on m1 because the item random effect already
captures between-item variation. The exploratory model (m3) is excluded
from formal ranking because 9 observations retain Pareto $k > 0.7$
after moment matching, indicating unstable PSIS leave-one-out estimates.

Random effects from the conditioned model (m1):
\begin{itemize}
  \item Country standard deviation (SD) = 0.44 (95\% CrI: 0.28--0.65)
  \item Item SD = 2.07 (95\% CrI: 1.49--2.96)
  \item Ratio: 4.7:1 (item identity explains more variation, while
    country adds structure)
\end{itemize}

The evidence is directionally consistent: conditioning on country
improves prediction ($\Delta$ELPD = 17.9, SE 13.3), and the country
random-effect SD is positive across the interval. The SE is large
relative to the point estimate, reflecting the small sample (18 items $\times$ 20
countries = 360 observations). The descriptive CV results in the main
text provide the primary evidence for the conditioning probe; the model
suggests the pattern survives the crossed random-effects structure.

   236	  recur without revealing any direction among its parts, and a directed
   237	  dependence can hold without recurring.
   238	\item \term{Grounds} explain why the profile obtains or
   239	  persists\ifblind\else\ \citep{reynolds2026notEveryStableCluster}\fi.
   240	  \term{Stabilizers} are grounds that hold the combination together as forms
   241	  enter and leave. \term{Corrective control} is the strongest, a stabilizer
   242	  that restores the combination when something disturbs it. Only that would
   243	  license calling the cluster \term{homeostatic}, Boyd's term
   244	  \citep{boyd1999}, and the evidence here doesn't establish
   245	  it.
   246	\end{itemize}

  1011	\subsection{Causal networks vs.\ prototype theory}\label{sec:prototype}
  1012	
  1013	Prototype theory \citep{rosch1975,taylor2003} was developed for exactly
  1014	the kind of data Section~\ref{sec:cluster} documents, and it handles
  1015	that data well: graded membership, better-and-worse exemplars, and
  1016	fuzzy boundaries via overlapping similarity spaces. It also correctly
  1017	predicts that boundary disputes (interjections vs.\ fillers, vs.\
  1018	routine formulae) should recur instead of being resolvable by
  1019	definition. These are genuine explanatory achievements, and the
  1020	causal-network account doesn't replace them.
  1021	
  1022	The accounts differ in what they buy that data with. Prototype theory buys it
  1023	with degrees of membership. The causal-network account doesn't need them,
  1024	because each field-relative cut is determinate and the disputes concern which
  1025	cut an inquiry should be using (Section~\ref{sec:boundaries}). That's the
  1026	source of a prediction prototype theory doesn't make: apparent gradience
  1027	should decompose under conditioning rather than persist as irreducible
  1028	fuzziness.
  1029	
  1030	A causal-network account adds a different kind of question. Prototype
  1031	theory explains why borderline cases should be expected: categories are
  1032	organized around better and worse exemplars. It doesn't explain why
  1033	the properties cluster in this particular pattern or what should happen
  1034	when one of their links is disrupted.
  1035	
  1036	The difference matters because the causal-network account generates
  1037	predictions about change, conditioning, and residues. It predicts that
  1038	forms recruited by different pathways should retain different traces of
  1039	their source constructions, that apparent gradience should decompose
  1040	under variety conditioning, and that some properties may be more central
  1041	to network order than others (hub-property asymmetry). These
  1042	predictions are probed for interjections in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
  1043	
  1044	Pathway residues provide a further discriminator. If the proposed causal
  1045	links are real, messy boundary cases shouldn't be messy in arbitrary
  1046	ways. Retained complements should point to verbal sources; euphemistic
  1047	phonological deformation should point to taboo-managed invocative
  1048	sources; regional concentration should point to regional or indexical
  1049	recruitment. Prototype theory offers no analogous retrodictive test.
  1050	
  1051	\subsection{What causal structure adds}
  1052	
  1053	Several analytical stances generate different predictions for
  1054	interjections. The weakest is a \term{property list}: interjections share
  1055	prosodic isolation, non-inflection, non-referentiality, and emotive
  1056	force. That's descriptively useful, but it makes no prediction about
  1057	what happens when one property changes.
  1058	
  1059	Feature specifications are stronger alternatives, for example
  1060	[\textsc{+peripheral}, \textsc{-argument-taking},
  1061	\textsc{+expressive}]. This generates some of the same distributional
  1062	consequences: no inflection follows from the absence of phi-features,
  1063	and no argument-taking follows from the relevant syntactic feature
  1064	bundle. But feature specifications treat those consequences as
  1065	entailments of the feature bundle, not as effects of identifiable
  1066	processes.
  1067	
  1068	A constructional account is a stronger competitor. It can represent
  1069	many of the same pairings of form, meaning, and use
  1070	\citep{goldberg1995constructions,goldberg2006,croft2001}. The
  1071	causal-network claim is compatible with that representational move. It
  1072	asks a different question: which positions in a constructional inventory
  1073	are projectible, why do their properties cohere, and which changes
  1074	should make them come apart? Construction grammar models the inventory;
  1075	the projectibility question asks where the inventory supports
  1076	cross-constructional and cross-field inference. The two main pressure
  1077	points are pathway residues and field-relative decomposition. If those
  1078	payoffs fail under further testing, the causal-network account collapses
  1079	to a descriptive inventory rather than an explanatory category analysis.
  1080	
  1081	The causal-network account adds five claims that property lists, feature
  1082	bundles, and constructional representations don't settle by themselves:
  1083	
  1084	\begin{enumerate}
  1085	\item Co-movement. Prosodic isolation, supplement function,
  1086	  non-referentiality, and pragmatic force recur in a patterned way. The
  1087	  account asks which links explain why observing one property licenses
  1088	  expectations about another.
  1089	\item Asymmetry. Some properties are upstream or more diagnostic than
  1090	  others. Prosodic packaging can make syntactic integration less
  1091	  available, while the reverse relation has a different status. A flat
  1092	  feature bundle can't represent that ordering.
  1093	\item Persistence. The category profile can remain available while
  1094	  individual forms enter and leave, which is a claim about stability rather
  1095	  than about what maintains it.
  1110	pressure point is whether the probes in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
  1111	support those claims.

  1113	\subsection{Three field-relative nodes}\label{sec:fieldrelative-nodes}
  1114	
  1115	The causal-network account developed by
  1116	\textcite{khalidi2013,khalidi2018nodes} also explains why
  1117	projectibility is field-relative. If kinds are nodes in causal
  1118	structures, different fields may track different nodes because they ask
  1119	different explanatory and predictive questions. The resulting categories
  1120	remain realist: each inquiry tracks the causal relations that matter for
  1121	its explanations and predictions.
  1122	
  1123	The familiar linguistic parallel is the interrogative/question
  1124	distinction. Syntacticians track \term{interrogative}: a clause type
  1125	with properties such as subject--auxiliary inversion, fronting of
  1126	interrogative words, and polar-question syntax. Pragmatists track \term{question}:
  1127	a speech act that requests information, makes an answer
  1128	relevant, and organizes uptake. The two often coincide, and they also come apart:
  1129	\mention{Can you pass the salt?} is syntactically interrogative and
  1130	pragmatically a request, while \mention{I wonder where she went} can
  1131	raise an information issue without having interrogative clause syntax.
  1132	
  1133	Interjections create a similar problem with less terminological help.
  1134	Syntax, semantics, and interactional pragmatics all use the
  1135	word \enquote{interjection}, but each field tracks a slightly different
  1136	node: different properties, different causal links, different
  1137	predictions. I distinguish these with subscripts:%
  1138	\footnote{The interrogative/question contrast uses different words for
  1139	  the syntactic and pragmatic nodes because both terms are established
  1140	  in their respective traditions. For interjections,
  1141	  every field uses the same word, so subscripts are the honest notation.
  1142	  An alternative would have been to adopt distinct terms from the
  1143	  relevant literatures: \term{expressive} from \textcite{potts2007}
  1144	  for the semantic node, and response token from
  1145	  \textcite{stivers2019} or \term{liminal sign} from
  1146	  \textcite{dingemanse2020} for the interactional-pragmatic node.
  1147	
  1148	  The Latin
  1149	  \mention{ejaculatio}, a throwing out, mirroring \mention{interjicere},
  1150	  \enquote{to throw between} \citep{libert2019},
  1151	  would have been etymologically perfect
  1152	  for the interactional-pragmatic category, but modern English has
  1153	  rendered the term unavailable.}
  1154	
  1155	\begin{itemize}
  1156	\item \intj{syn}, the syntactician's
  1157	  category (\textit{CGEL}, Quirk et al.). Distributional cluster: supplement
  1158	  function, non-inflecting, non-argument-taking. Linked by
  1159	  prosodic-syntactic coupling (Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}).
  1160	\item \intj{sem}, the semanticist's
  1161	  category, overlapping with \textcite{potts2007}'s \term{expressives}.
  1162	  Use-conditional meaning cluster: independent of at-issue content,
  1163	  nondisplaceable, perspective-dependent, descriptively ineffable
  1164	  \citep[166]{potts2007}. Often recruited through semantic bleaching.
  1165	  Different extension: prenominal \mention{damn} in
  1166	  \mention{the damn car} is \intj{sem} but not
  1167	  \intj{syn}.
  1168	\item \intj{prag}, the interactional-pragmatic
  1169	  category, overlapping with \textcite{stivers2019}'s
  1170	  response tokens and \textcite{dingemanse2020}'s liminal signs.
  1171	  Sequential/stance cluster: turn-initial, backchannel, stance display.
  1172	  Linked by routine entrenchment and possibly by regional/indexical
  1173	  conditioning, both from Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}. Different
  1174	  extension: \mention{mm-hm} is
  1175	  \intj{prag} but debatably
  1176	  \intj{syn}.
  1177	\end{itemize}

  1294	\subsection{Recruitment pathways}\label{sec:recruitment}
  1295	
  1296	Section~\ref{sec:cluster} already pointed to three recruitment
  1297	pathways: semantic bleaching from nouns, syntactic deactivation from
  1298	verbs, and nonce coinage via onomatopoeia. The first is historically
  1299	demonstrated; the second and third are proposed pathways with clear
  1300	examples but without the extended diachronic documentation that Path~1
  1301	has. The first two are analysed in detail here; the third is taken up
  1302	in Section~\ref{sec:turnover}.
  1303	
  1304	The pragmatic issue is how changes in communicative use become
  1305	categorially consequential: grammaticalization and pragmaticalization
  1306	matter here because they alter a form's communicative job as well as its
  1307	position in a syntactic tree
  1308	\citep[cf.][]{TraugottDasher2002, heinekuteva2002lexicon}.
  1309	
  1310	\subsection{Path 1: religious noun to interjection}\label{sec:path-religious}
  1311	
  1312	\textcite{gehweiler2008} traces the development of \mention{gee} from
  1313	\mention{Jesus} through three positions:
  1314	
  1315	\ea\label{ex:bleaching}
  1316	  \ea Proper name used as invocation (\mention{Jesus!} addressed to the
  1317	    deity)
  1318	  \ex Secondary interjection: exclamatory use, no longer addressing the
  1319	    deity (\mention{Jesus!} as an expression of surprise)
  1320	  \ex Primary interjection: phonological modification yields
  1321	    \mention{Jeez} and \mention{Gee}, and \mention{gee!} detaches from
  1322	    the source noun
  1323	  \z
  1324	\z
  1325	The two transitions don't work the same way, and Gehweiler is emphatic
  1510	inferencing and syntactic position \citep[88]{gehweiler2008}.
  1511	
  1512	The same coupling is visible synchronically. Standalone
  1513	\mention{Damn!} has both properties: it occupies its own intonation unit
  1514	and has no role in clause structure. In \mention{I think that, damn,
  1515	we're going to be late}, \mention{damn} remains possible only as a
  1516	parenthetical supplement, set off from the \mention{that}-clause. In
  1517	\mention{the damn car}, the form loses both properties at once: it's
  1518	inside the noun phrase and receives the prosody of the phrase.
  1519	
  1520	The prediction is concrete. If a novel form is reliably
  1521	packaged as its own intonation unit, it should resist complementation,
  1522	modification by ordinary phrase-internal material, and coordination with
  1523	integrated phrases. If it's pulled into a host phrase, prosodic isolation should
  1524	weaken together with \intj{syn} status.
  1525	
  1526	\subsubsection{The non-referentiality ratchet}
  1527	
  1528	Bleaching removes referential content, which weakens the semantic
  1529	motivation for argument structure. A form with no entity or event to
  1530	construe is less available as an argument, complement, or modifier. Once
  1531	that syntactic role is lost, prosodic isolation becomes easier to keep
  1532	in place. The result is a one-directional cascade. Once argument
  1533	structure is lost, the form occupies a different functional niche, so
  1534	ordinary pressures toward referential syntax are reduced.
  1535	
  1536	This ratchet supports a different set of predictions from the
  1537	prosodic-syntactic link. A non-referential stance form should resist
  1538	definiteness, quantification, and argument position:
  1539	*\mention{the damn!}, *\mention{every ouch}, *\mention{Damn surprised
  1540	me}. It should also resist embedding as propositional content
  1541	(\mention{*She said that damn}) unless it's quoted as a form. If
  1542	referential content becomes active again, the form should move toward
  1543	noun, verb, or routine-formula regions of the network. The ratchet
  1544	keeps the form near the interjection node by blocking return
  1545	to the source category, while prosodic-syntactic coupling links the
  1546	central structural properties.
  1547	
  1548	\subsubsection{Interactional routine entrenchment}
  1549	
  1550	The interactional-pragmatic node needs a candidate stabilizer of its own.
  1551	Conversation repeatedly places interjectional forms in recognizable
  1552	sequential slots: continuers, repair initiators, change-of-state tokens,
  1553	stance displays, and response tokens \citep{stivers2019,dingemanse2020}.
  1554	\textcite{dingemanse2024} argues that these interactional uses are
  1555	central rather than peripheral for high-frequency interjections.
  1556	
  1557	Repeated deployment in these slots favours a particular package of
  1558	properties. Two levels are in play here, and the argument needs both. On a
  1559	given occasion, a continuer such as \mention{mm-hm} works because it can be
  1560	recognized without taking over the turn or adding propositional content.
  1561	A change-of-state token such as \mention{oh} works because it can mark
  1562	an information update before the speaker builds further at-issue
  1563	content. A stance token such as \mention{bruh} works because it can
  1564	display evaluation while leaving the surrounding proposition untouched.
  1565	Across many occasions, that occasion-level fitness is supposed to accumulate,
  1566	so that the interactional routine favours short, standalone, prosodically
  1567	independent, non-referential material in the inventory at large. The
  1568	accumulation is an assumption here rather than a finding. The probes in
  1569	Section~\ref{sec:discussion} bear on the inventory-level pattern, not on the
  1570	step from one level to the other.
  1571	
  1572	This candidate stabilizer supports the projectibility of
  1573	\intj{prag}. If a novel form is recruited as a
  1574	minimal stance, response, repair, or backchannel resource, one should
  1575	expect turn-initial or standalone availability, independent prosodic
  1576	packaging, resistance to embedding under reported speech, and
  1577	availability for uptake as an interactional move. The same candidate
  1578	stabilizer also explains why the function can persist when category membership
  1579	weakens: a full clause such as \mention{I agree} can do agreement work,
  1580	but it lacks the reduced, non-propositional packaging that makes
  1581	\mention{mm-hm} a central \intj{prag} case.
  1582	
  1583	\subsubsection{Regional/indexical conditioning}
  1584	
  1585	Some interjections are sharply conditioned by region, register, or
  1586	community, and some may also do identity work. \mention{Lah} in
  1587	Singapore and Malaysia, \mention{yaar} in India and Pakistan,
  1588	\mention{haba} in Nigeria, and \mention{bruh} in African American
  1589	English \citep{louf2023} are plausible cases.
  1590	
  1591	They are not, however, the same kind of case, and the difference matters for
  1592	which node the conditioning operates on. \mention{Haba} is free-standing:
  1593	\textcite{unuabonahdaniel2020} avoid the terms utterance-initial and
  1594	utterance-final for it and its Nigerian congeners \enquote{because they are
  1595	free standing and are far more detached from the utterances than PMs}
  1990	belong to the same word family. They needn't share a lexeme.

  1992	\subsection{Partial-observation inference}\label{sec:proj-partial}

  1995	interjection, it has interjection properties. That's trivially true of
  1996	any category. Causal-network projectibility is different. Observing some
  1997	cluster properties in a novel form licenses inference to other
  1998	properties you haven't yet observed.

  2050	Consider the standalone interjectional use of \mention{bruh}, a
  2051	recently conventionalized form. A learner or hearer encountering that
  2052	use for the first time observes a partial package: standalone position,
  2053	prosodic isolation, morphological bareness, and stance meaning. From
  2054	those observations, the three field-relative nodes license a battery of
  2055	further expectations:
  2056	
  2057	\ea\label{ex:bruh}
  2058	  \ea \intj{syn}: the interjectional use
  2059	    won't inflect within that use (*\mention{bruhs}, *\mention{bruhed}),
  2060	    will resist syntactic integration (*\mention{I think that bruh,
  2061	    we're late}), and won't take a determiner or function as an argument
  2062	    (*\mention{the bruh}, *\mention{Bruh surprised me}). This leaves
  2063	    room for separate nominal or verbal lexemes in the same word family.
  2064	  \ex \intj{prag}: the form should be available for
  2065	    turn-initial or standalone interactional use.
  2066	  \ex \intj{sem}: its expressive meaning is
  2067	    expected to be use-conditional and speaker-oriented.
  2068	  \z
  2069	\z
  2070	These expectations vary in strength, and the asymmetry matters:
  2071	
  2072	\begin{itemize}
  2073	\item Near-entailments (a): the
  2074	  \intj{syn} predictions mostly extrapolate
  2075	  within the same domain from observed distributional facts. Any
  2076	  framework recognizing the relevant morphosyntactic properties
  2077	  generates many of the same predictions. These are real but not specific
  2078	  to the causal-network account.
  2079	\item Cross-domain inferences (b, c): the
  2080	  \intj{prag} prediction (interactional-pragmatic
  2081	  distribution from phonological form) and the
  2082	  \intj{sem} prediction (use-conditional meaning
  2083	  from stance expression) involve genuine leaps across domains. These are specific to the causal-network account: they rely on
  2084	  the cluster's internal links, not on any single property.
  2085	\end{itemize}
  2086	All three are licensed because identifiable links order the cluster's
  2087	properties. The non-referentiality ratchet
  2088	(Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}) links loss of referential content with
  2089	syntactic non-integration. Prosodic-syntactic coupling then links
  2090	syntactic non-integration with prosodic isolation. The inferences are
  2091	grounded in network order, not in definitions.
  2092	
  2093	Who does the projecting, though? A hearer meeting \mention{Bummer!} or
  2094	\mention{groovy} for the first time doesn't run a taxonomy and then read
  2095	off consequences. So it's worth separating two levels that the argument so
  2096	far has run together.
  2097	
  2098	\term{Category-driven projection} is the analyst's. Given that a form
  2099	belongs to the interjection cluster, expect the linked properties. That's
  2100	projectibility in Goodman's sense, claimed throughout the preceding
  2101	sections. \term{Cue-driven inference} is the participant's. The
  2102	\mention{bruh} case above describes it without naming it: what the hearer
  2103	has is standalone position, prosodic isolation, morphological bareness, and
  2104	stance meaning, from which the stance reading follows. No category
  2105	label need be represented for the reading to go through.
  2106	
  2107	The two aren't rivals. Cue-driven inference is reliable \emph{because} the cluster is
  2108	coupled: the links in Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling} are what make a
  2109	partial package informative about the rest. Analyst and participant are two
  2110	consumers of one causal structure, drawing on it for different purposes and
  2111	with different explicitness. So \mention{Bummer!} and \mention{groovy} tell
  2112	against an account on which the category label does the inferential work,
  2113	and for one on which the coupling does. The label is the analyst's
  2114	bookkeeping device for a structure participants exploit without naming.
  2115	
  2116	Fields differ in which links they track
  2117	(Section~\ref{sec:proj-fieldrelative}); analyst and participant differ in
  2118	whether the category figures in the inference at all. The two indices are
  2119	independent. Nothing here says that speakers tacitly represent
  2120	interjection-hood, which the account doesn't require.

  2122	\subsection{What each node projects}\label{sec:proj-fieldrelative}
  2123	
  2124	The three interjection nodes established in
  2125	Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes} do their work as different packages of
  2126	inference, not as rival definitions.
  2127	
  2128	\intj{syn} projects syntactic behaviour:
  2129	supplement function, resistance to \mention{that}-complementation,
  2130	resistance to coordination with non-interjections, and no ordinary
  2131	argument structure. These predictions are grounded in the
  2132	prosodic-syntactic coupling link from
  2133	Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}. \intj{sem}
  2134	projects meaning type, and here the projection has to reach past the
  2135	properties that admitted the form. Independence, nondisplaceability,
  2136	perspective dependence, and descriptive ineffability are what
  2137	Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes} uses to assign the node, so
  2138	predicting them would only restate membership.
  2139	\textcite{potts2007} lists two further properties of expressive content on
  2140	the following page, and neither does admission work here. One is immediacy,
  2141	where expressives \enquote{achieve their intended act simply by being
  2142	uttered}. The other is repeatability, where repeated use has an effect
  2143	\enquote{of strengthening the emotive content, rather than one of
  2144	redundancy} \citep[167]{potts2007}. Both are testable and both can fail.
  2145	\mention{Damn, damn, damn} intensifies where \mention{The car broke, the car
  2146	broke, the car broke} doesn't, and a form that flattened into redundancy
  2147	under repetition would defeat the prediction. \intj{prag}
  2148	projects interactional-pragmatic behaviour, and the same discipline applies.
  2149	Turn-initial or standalone positioning and stance display are admission
  2150	criteria. What they let us predict is uptake: that the form will be treated
  2151	as a complete move, so that a recipient's next turn responds to it rather
  2152	than waiting for its continuation, and that it will be usable as a
  2153	backchannel without taking the floor. Both are observable in sequence, and a
  2154	form routinely treated as an incomplete turn would defeat them.
  2155	
  2156	The nodes overlap because some links, especially prosodic isolation
  2157	and non-referentiality, contribute to more than one node. They come
  2158	apart because other links are field-specific. A novel form categorized
  2159	as \intj{syn} (prosodically isolated,
  2160	non-inflecting, syntactically non-integrated) should tend also to be
  2161	\intj{sem} (use-conditional, speaker-oriented)
  2162	and \intj{prag} (turn-initial, available for
  2163	backchanneling). Central cases such as \mention{wow}, \mention{ouch},
  2164	and \mention{hey} fit that default. Boundary cases locate the decoupling, \mention{mm-hm} among them
  2165	\citep{oconnell2005}.
  2166	
  2167	The three constructions of \mention{damn} show the same point inside a
  2168	single word family.
  2169	
  2170	For standalone
  2171	\mention{damn!}, all three nodes agree: the form is prosodically
  2172	isolated, syntactically non-integrated, use-conditional, and available
  2173	as a stance marker. For \mention{damn these mosquitoes!}, the picture
  2174	is mixed: \intj{syn} holds (it heads an IntjP)
  2175	but with limited complement-taking;
  2176	\intj{sem} holds (the meaning is expressive);
  2177	\intj{prag} is weakened (it's not a backchannel or
  2178	turn-management device).
  2179	
  2180	For prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}, the nodes
  2181	diverge further: \intj{syn} fails entirely (the
  2182	form is syntactically integrated inside a noun phrase), but
  2183	\intj{sem} holds (use-conditional, perspective-
  2184	dependent). Neither \textit{CGEL}'s syntax nor Potts's semantics alone
  2185	predicts this gradient. The three-node analysis does, because it tracks
  2185	predicts this gradient. The three-node analysis does, because it tracks
  2186	which links are active in each construction.

  2188	\subsection{Category vs.\ function}
  2189	
  2190	\textit{CGEL}'s distinction between category and function sharpens the
  2191	projectibility point: what does the \intj{syn} category label add
  2192	beyond the supplement function label? Supplements of any category
  2193	(noun phrases [NPs], adjective phrases [AdjPs], relative clauses) share
  2194	prosodic isolation and syntactic
  2195	non-integration. But \intj{syn} adds a package of
  2196	predictions that no other category-in-supplement-function supports:
  2197	
  2198	\begin{itemize}
  2199	\item Non-referentiality. Supplements can be referential
  2200	  (\mention{a university lecturer, Dr Brown, \ldots}); interjections
  2201	  characteristically can't.
  2202	\item Emotive/interpersonal force. Not all supplements express
  2203	  stance (\mention{Dr Brown} expresses none); interjections
  2204	  characteristically do (\mention{ugh}, \mention{yay}).
  2205	\item Free coinage. No other category in supplement function
  2206	  can be freely coined via onomatopoeia. Comics gave English
  2207	  \mention{bam} and \mention{pow}; nothing comparable mints new
  2208	  relative clauses.
  2209	\item Non-inflection. NP supplements inflect freely
  2210	  (\mention{a lecturer}, \mention{two lecturers}); interjections don't
  2211	  (*\mention{ouches}).
  2212	\end{itemize}
  2213	
  2214	The function label tells you about syntactic integration. The category
  2215	label tells you about inference: it licenses a package of predictions
  2216	that the function label alone can't provide. The category/function distinction matters
  2217	beyond the \textit{CGEL} tradition: any framework that distinguishes what kind of
  2218	thing an expression is from what job it's doing in a particular clause
  2219	will face the same question. The causal-network answer is that the
  2220	category earns its keep by licensing inferences that the function
  2221	doesn't.
  2222	
  2223	\subsection{What sets the domain of projection}\label{sec:proj-domain}
  2224	
  2225	Categories don't all project in the same domain, and what fixes the domain
  2226	is where the causal links run. Nouns project morphosyntactically:
  2227	\mention{lecturer} takes a determiner, inflects for number, and fills an
  2228	argument slot. Categorizing something as a noun tells you almost nothing
  2229	about its pragmatics, since nouns aren't a pragmatic category. Interjections
  2230	come out the other way, and the reason is the same reason.
  2231	
  2232	For interjections, the situation is reversed. The three nodes overlap,
  2233	and the richest projectibility belongs to
  2234	\intj{prag}, the interactional-pragmatic
  2235	cluster.
  2236	\textcite{dingemanse2024} reaches the same conclusion from interactional
  2237	data: the most frequent and functionally central interjections are
  2238	interactional tools (continuers, repair initiators, change-of-state
  2239	tokens) rather than emotive exclamations, and their primary
  2240	projectibility is about sequential position and conversational
  2241	management. For forms at the intersection of all three nodes, the strongest inferences
  2242	are about use, not denotation. \mention{Oh} shows the
  2243	asymmetry. From \intj{syn} you get what any
  2244	supplement gives you: prosodic isolation, no inflection, no argument structure.
  2245	From \intj{prag} you get a turn position,
  2246	availability as a response token, and the display of evolving knowledge
  2247	described in Section~\ref{sec:pragmatics}. Same form, and the two nodes differ
  2248	in how much they buy.
  2249	
  2250	The contrast with nouns and verbs concerns where the density lies, not how
  2251	much structure each node carries.
  2252	\intj{syn} is thin next to those nodes, lacking
  2253	the network of agreement, argument realization, derivation, and inflectional
  2254	behaviour that makes them so stable. What the three nodes divide between them
  2255	is the kind of prediction each supports, set out in
  2256	Section~\ref{sec:proj-fieldrelative}.
  2257	
  2258	Why pragmatic in this case? Because causal links generate predictions
  2259	within the domains they operate in. The prosodic-syntactic coupling from
  2260	Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}
  2261	predicts prosodic and syntactic behaviour, with no corresponding
  2262	prediction about morphological paradigms or truth-conditional content.
  2263	Regional/indexical conditioning predicts uneven persistence across
  2264	varieties, which is why \mention{lah} and \mention{yaar} are entrenched in some
  2265	Englishes and absent from others, with no corresponding prediction about
  2266	inflectional regularity. The strongest established links among the three
  2267	interjection nodes are prosodic and interactional; the regional or
  2268	indexical link remains a maintenance hypothesis. These links and
  2269	hypotheses underwrite predictions in their respective domains.
  2270	
  2271	The same point holds outside interjections. Noun projectibility is
  2272	morphosyntactic because the underlying processes (agreement, structural
  2273	analogy, discourse frequency) operate in morphosyntactic domains.
  2274	
  2275	This connects to the content/procedural distinction. The central
  2276	projectibility of interjections is procedural and use-conditional rather
  2277	than denotational \citep{TraugottDasher2002}. The
  2278	\intj{sem} (expressive) cluster is the semantic
  2279	reflex: use-conditional meaning is the semantic signature of procedural
  2280	content. The same pattern shows up elsewhere. \term{Animacy} projects grammatically (argument realization,
  2281	reference tracking); \term{colour} projects ecologically (edibility,
  2282	toxicity) but usually doesn't project grammatically in the way animacy
  2283	can \citep[cf.][]{seifart2010}. In each case, the domain of
  2284	projectibility follows from the underlying processes.
  2285	
  2286	The richest projectibility is pragmatic, but
  2287	\intj{syn} still belongs in the grammar
  2288	because it's projectible and has its own causal links. Neutralize
  2289	prosodic isolation and the form is reclassified, which is what happens to
  2290	prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}. The syntactic category
  2291	licenses distributional inference: it predicts supplement function,
  2292	resistance to embedding under reported speech (*\mention{She said that damn}),
  2293	and coordination restrictions, even if those predictions are less rich than the
  2294	pragmatic ones. A thin syntactic node is still a projectible syntactic
  2295	node. The framework requires reliable inference, and
  2296	\intj{syn} does.

  2300	\subsection{Empirical probes and boundary conditions}
  2301	
  2302	Section~\ref{sec:projectibility} asked what categorizing a novel form as
  2303	an interjection lets you predict about that form. At the category level,
  2304	the causal-network account should also explain patterns across the
  2305	interjectional region as a whole. As Section~\ref{sec:prototype} argued, what
  2305	interjectional region as a whole. As Section~\ref{sec:prototype} argued, what
  2306	the causal-network account adds to what prototype theory already accommodates
  2307	is network-order predictions with named defeat conditions. The probes
  2308	below address profile stability, boundary behaviour, and failure. They
  2309	aren't tests of corrective control.
  2310	
  2311	The strongest probe is pathway residue. If interjections are recruited by
  2312	different causal pathways, residual properties should line up with
  2313	recruitment history. The defeat condition is random distribution:
  2314	retained complements, euphemistic deformation, atypical phonology, and
  2315	regional skew should show no relation to source pathways. The evidence
  2316	in Section~\ref{sec:residues} supports the residue prediction, and the
  2317	pre-registered morphology-gain study adds an exit-residue case:
  2318	\mention{wowed}, \mention{booed}, \mention{oohed}, and \mention{shooed}
  2319	gain syntactic integration while retaining source-interjection semantics.
  2320	The payoff is pragmatic and diachronic: boundary failures diagnose
  2321	recruitment routes and explain why later uses keep source-specific
  2322	residues.
  2323	
  2324	A second probe asks whether conditioning recovers structure. Apparent
  2325	gradation at the interjection boundary should decompose when conditioned
  2326	on register, dialect, or community. The defeat condition is parity
  2327	between conditioned and pooled models. A proof-of-concept probe uses
  2328	GloWbE frequency data \citep{daviesfuchs2015}: for the 10 most frequent
  2329	interjection-tagged items plus regional and boundary items,
  2330	frequency-per-million was extracted by country (20 countries) by raw
  2331	string search, and the coefficient of variation (CV; standard deviation
  2332	divided by the mean) was computed across countries.
  2333	
  2334	The results show three tiers of geographic conditioning. Regional
  2335	interjections (\mention{lah}, \mention{yaar}, \mention{haba}) are
  2336	near-categorical: \mention{lah} occurs at 9.35 per million in Malaysia
  2337	and 6.72 in Singapore but near zero elsewhere; \mention{yaar} at 2.88
  2338	in Pakistan and 2.33 in India but near zero elsewhere; \mention{haba}
  2339	at 8.78 in Nigeria but near zero elsewhere.
  2340	
  2341	Core interjections show
  2342	intermediate but structured variation: \mention{ah} has the highest
  2343	CV among core items (0.71), with Singapore (60.42) and Jamaica (53.16)
  2344	at eight times the rate of Kenya (7.62). Even broadly distributed items
  2345	like \mention{yeah} (CV 0.55) show a 6:1 ratio between the United
  2346	States (103.78) and Tanzania (16.85). Only \mention{yes} and
  2347	\mention{no} approach uniformity (CVs of 0.21--0.22). Boundary items
  2348	continue the pattern: the fillers \mention{um} (CV 0.61) and
  2349	\mention{uh} (CV 0.76) and the routine formula \mention{bye} (CV 0.61)
  2350	all show structured geographic variation.
  2351	
  2352	These coefficients are estimated from twenty countries, so they carry more
  2353	uncertainty than a bare figure suggests, and it's worth saying which
  2354	comparisons survive it. Bootstrapping over the twenty sections, the
  2355	uniformity of \mention{yes} and \mention{no} is the firm result: their
  2356	intervals sit below eleven of the thirteen other core items, overlapping only
  2357	\mention{hello} and \mention{ha}. The ordering within the rest of the core
  2358	band isn't a result at all. No two of those items have separable intervals,
  2359	so \mention{uh} at 0.76 isn't distinguishable from \mention{yeah} at 0.55,
  2360	and the figures should be read as a single undifferentiated band rather than
  2361	a ranking. For the regional items the coefficient is the wrong summary
  2362	altogether: with a form absent from eighteen of twenty sections the mean sits
  2363	near zero and the coefficient becomes unstable, so \mention{lah}'s interval runs from 0.89 to 4.27. Their concentration is
  2364	better read off the counts than off a dispersion statistic.
  2365	
  2366	Conditioning on country recovers structure at every level of this
  2367	sample. Variety structures the apparent gradience between core
  2368	interjections, fillers, and boundary items.
  2369	
  2370	A Bayesian multilevel model (negative binomial with crossed random
  2371	effects for item and country) supports the same point. Adding country as
  2372	a grouping factor improves prediction over an item-only baseline: the
  2373	country-level random-effect standard deviation is 0.44 (95\% credible
  2374	interval [CrI]: 0.28--0.65), and the conditioned model outperforms the
  2375	unconditional model by 18 expected log predictive density (ELPD) units
  2376	(standard error [SE] 13) on exact leave-one-out cross-validation.
  2377	
  2378	The descriptive CV results are the primary evidence; the model shows
  2379	that the pattern survives a crossed random-effects structure. Full model
  2380	specification and results are in the
  2381	supplementary materials.\footnote{\ifblind Available at
  2382	  [link removed for blind review].\else Available at
  2383	  \url{https://github.com/BrettRey/interjections-hpc}.\fi}
  2384	Several methodological limitations remain. GloWbE's
  2385	part-of-speech tagger, trained on inner-circle English, fails to tag
  2386	regional interjections (\mention{yaar} and \mention{haba} receive no
  2387	interjection tag at all), which means the tagged frequencies
  2388	underestimate regional items. The \mention{ha} row needs a criterion
  2389	rather than a caveat, and the criterion has to be settled before the figure
  2390	rather than after it. Iterated \mention{HA! HA!} is a rendering of laughter,
  2391	closer to eye dialect than to a lexical item; \mention{ha} used for sarcasm or
  2392	scepticism is an interjectional use. The line is harder to draw in a written
  2393	corpus than a spoken one, since nothing was vocalized for the spelling to
  2394	represent, and iterated \mention{haha} in digital writing has arguably
  2395	conventionalized into a form in its own right. Nothing here rests on a claim
  2396	that imitative forms fall outside the category, which would contradict the
  2397	recruitment route described above.
  2398	
  2399	Hand-coding the tagged concordance for all twenty sections shows how much
  2400	turns on that one decision, and the figures reported above count iterated
  2401	laughter rather than excluding it. Laughter is 51.5\% of tagged \mention{ha}
  2402	in the United States and 63.0\% in Great Britain, so the two readings are far
  2403	apart: across six defensible codings of the laughter and residual-token
  2404	questions, \mention{ha}'s coefficient of variation runs from 0.40 to 0.83.
  2405	A single figure would report one path through that set rather than the set
  2406	\citep[cf.][]{gelmanloken2014}.
  2407	
  2408	Two results should be kept apart, because one survives the spread and the
  2409	other doesn't. The dispersion statistic doesn't: it isn't identified without
  2410	the criterion decision, and the 0.41 above corresponds to counting laughter.
  2411	The ranking does. Coding also shows what proportion of tagged tokens are
  2412	interjections at all, which runs from 34.5\% in Bangladesh to 84.0\% in Great
  2413	Britain, with \term{hectare} the contaminant in East Africa and South Asia and
  2414	personal names in Singapore, where only 6.1\% is \term{hectare}. Correcting
  2415	each section by its own rate moves Kenya from fifth place among the twenty to
  2416	fifteenth, and it does so under every one of the six codings. Pooling the
  2417	twenty rates to allow for their unequal precision, which runs from about 8,100
  2418	tagged tokens for the United States down to 420 for Ghana, moves no section by
  2419	more than 0.6 percentage points, because the variance between sections is much
  2420	larger than the sampling variance within them.
  2421	
  2422	Country differences in GloWbE may also reflect corpus composition,
  2423	genre mix, internet ecology, and orthographic convention as much as
  2424	dialect or community structure. A spelling-variant check bears the last of
  2425	these out, in an unexpected direction. \mention{Ya} and \mention{yeh} look
  2426	like variants of \mention{yeah} that a single-string query would miss, but
  2427	both concentrate where they're homographs of substrate words:
  2428	\mention{ya} peaks in Tanzania (19.5 per million) and Malaysia (13.9),
  2429	\mention{yeh} in Singapore (5.6), Pakistan (4.9), and India (4.9), and no
  2430	\mention{yeh} token in the corpus carries the interjection tag at all.
  2431	Folding both strings into \mention{yeah} would drop its dispersion from a
  2432	CV of 0.55 to 0.46, but only by importing non-English tokens unevenly across
  2433	varieties, so the figures below keep the strings apart. Lengthened spellings
  2434	point the same way and put a number on it. Summing \mention{umm},
  2435	\mention{ummm}, and \mention{ummmm} into \mention{um} moves its dispersion
  2436	from a CV of 0.61 to 0.60 once Pakistan is set aside, so lengthening is
  2437	negligible in general; including Pakistan it rises to 0.72, because Pakistani
  2438	English returns nearly eight times more \mention{umm} than \mention{um}
  2439	where the median variety returns a third as much. A twenty-token sample
  2440	settles what that signature is: nineteen are the Arabic teknonym
  2441	\mention{Umm}, as in \mention{Umm Salama}, drawn from hadith and biographical
  2442	material, and the twentieth mentions the filler rather than using it. Raw
  2443	strings are weakest, then, exactly where a variety's substrate supplies a
  2444	homograph, which is a sharper and more testable caveat than orthographic
  2445	variation at large, and one that bears on the varieties this section
  2446	compares. The regional items carry the most exposure, since they're
  2447	substrate-derived to begin with. Hand-coding sixty-nine \mention{yaar}
  2448	tokens puts the English-matrix rate at roughly 60\% in Pakistan and 65\% in
  2449	India, the remainder being Tamil \mention{yaar} \enquote{who}, proper names,
  2450	and tokens sitting inside Roman-script Hindi or Urdu. The rates are estimated
  2451	from twenty-token samples and are too coarse to correct with, but they're
  2452	large enough that raw-string figures for these items should be read as upper
  2453	bounds. Raw strings are imperfect proxies too:
  2454	\mention{yes}, \mention{no}, \mention{ha}, \mention{ah},
  2455	\mention{oh}, \mention{well}, \mention{right}, and \mention{like} are
  2456	not automatically interjection tokens. Some are clausal, quoted,
  2457	discourse-marker uses, literary representations, or noise. The
  2458	methodological payoff is that conditioning by variety reveals structure
  2459	that pooled category labels hide.
  2460	
  2461	A third, weaker probe concerns hub-property asymmetry. One property may
  2462	be more central to network order than others, with prosodic isolation
  2463	and non-referentiality as the main candidates. The defeat condition is
  2464	equal contribution by all properties. The present evidence leaves this
  2465	prediction open.
  2466	
  2467	Onomatopoeic interjections like \mention{pop} can be
  2468	syntactically integrated into clause structure; when prosodic isolation
  2469	is neutralized, as when \mention{pop} in \mention{pop went the cork}
  2470	is an adverb rather than a standalone interjection
  2471	\citep[152]{meinard2015}, reclassification is immediate. Restoring
  2472	partial referentiality, by contrast, allows the form to hover between
  2473	noun and interjection uses (as with \mention{God}). This suggests
  2474	prosodic isolation may be the hub, but the evidence is indirect.
  2475	
  2476	The probes differ in evidential weight. Pathway residue is the main
  2477	explanatory payoff, conditioning by variety is a proof of concept, and
  2478	hub-property asymmetry is an open conjecture. Prototype theory describes
  2479	the gradience but doesn't predict the trajectory of entry, the
  2480	pathway-specific structure of boundary cases, or the structure hidden in
  2481	apparent variation.
  2482	
  2483	At the framework level, if a feature-based account
  2484	(e.g., [\textsc{+peripheral}, \textsc{-argument-taking},
  2485	\textsc{+expressive}]) generates the same predictions without causal
  2486	structure, the network account would be a relabelling. The
  2487	causal-network payoffs are pathway residues and the field-relative
  2488	decomposition; if both fail under further testing, the account collapses to a descriptive
  2489	property list.
  2490	
  2491	\subsection{Boundary disputes as causal-link overlap}\label{sec:boundaries}
  2492	
  2493	The boundary disputes that embarrassed classical category theory
  2494	(interjections vs.\ nouns, verbs, adverbs, fillers, routine formulae)
  2495	are field-relative predictions of the causal-network account. Each
  2496	boundary reflects partial causal-link overlap and differences in what a
  2497	field needs the category to predict.
  2498	
  2499	Routine formulae (\mention{bye}, \mention{hello}, \mention{sorry}) share
  2500	prosodic isolation and pragmatic function with interjections but add
  2501	addressee-directedness and social predictability
  2502	\citep[109--110]{ameka1992}. For grammar, those shared properties place
  2503	them near interjections. For speech-act analysis, the additional
  2504	properties matter more, because they predict conventional response slots,
  2505	addressees, and felicity conditions. They're structured by additional
  2506	processes (social convention, politeness norms) that prototypical
  2507	interjections don't require.
  2508	
  2509	Fillers (\mention{um}, \mention{uh}) share prosodic distinctiveness and
  2510	syntactic non-integration but differ in emphasis, pause distribution,
  2511	and citation-introduction ability \citep{oconnell2005}. They occupy a
  2512	neighbouring region of the property space, structured by partially
  2513	overlapping links (processing-pressure management rather than
  2514	emotive expression).
  2515	
  2516	The Ameka-Wilkins debate can be reframed in these terms. Ameka's
  2517	separation is right for a field that needs to predict social routines and
  2518	speech-act felicity \citep[109]{ameka1992}. Wilkins's inclusion is right
  2519	for a field that needs to track pragmatic and semantic overlap with
  2520	interjections \citep[142]{wilkins1992}. A causal-network account accommodates both
  2521	observations because projectibility is indexed to explanatory purpose.
  2522	
  2523	\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
  2524	
  2525	English interjections are a category whose members support projectible
  2526	inferences about use. Assigning a form to it lets an analyst predict
  2527	prosodic packaging, supplement function, use-conditional stance,
  2528	sequential position, response function, and uptake, and the coupling that
  2529	licenses those predictions is what lets speakers and hearers anticipate the
  2530	same behaviour from partial cues. The category is thin as syntax but rich
  2531	as pragmatics.
  2532	
  2533	The reason is that the familiar interjection profile is stable and
  2534	causally ordered. Diachronic pathways recruit forms into the
  2535	profile: grammaticalization via semantic bleaching, pragmaticalization
  2536	via syntactic deactivation, and nonce coinage via onomatopoeia.
  2537	Synchronic links then structure the profile: prosodic-syntactic
  2538	coupling, the non-referentiality ratchet, interactional routine
  2539	entrenchment, and possibly regional/indexical conditioning. These links
  2540	explain why partial observations support further inferences.
  2541	
  2542	The field-relative decomposition shows why the traditional label
  2543	is useful but not uniform. Syntax, semantics, and interactional
  2544	pragmatics track overlapping nodes with different predictive payoffs.
  2545	Boundary cases such as \mention{damn these mosquitoes!}, prenominal
  2546	\mention{damn}, fillers, routine formulae, and obsolete \mention{fie}
  2547	aren't embarrassing exceptions; they show which links are active and
  2548	which have weakened.
  2549	
  2550	The broader pragmatic payoff is path dependence. Interjections don't
  2551	merely express emotion; they enter utterances with histories that shape
  2552	stance, sequence, and uptake. \mention{Gee}, \mention{damn},
  2553	\mention{bruh}, \mention{wowed}, and \mention{fie} matter because they
  2554	show projectible profiles, boundary behaviour, exit residues, and
  2555	failure conditions. They show how a category with no classical essence can support pragmatic
  2556	inference.
  2557	
  2558	The account isn't specific to interjections. Wherever properties cluster
  2559	without a definition holding them, the same questions arise: which links are
  2560	active, what recruited the members, and what happens when a link fails.
  2561	
  2562	The evidence supports a bounded declaration, a stable profile, and network
  2563	order, and it motivates candidate grounds without settling them. It doesn't
  2564	establish corrective control. That limit matters: pragmatic categories can be
  2565	stable and inductively useful without being maintained by a
  2566	perturbation-sensitive control system. English interjections are one
  2567	such category.

  2611	These counts are a model-dependent measurement rather than a transparent one:
  2612	a tagger stands between the text and the number, so the figures carry the
  2613	tagger's error as well as the corpus's composition. A model-dependent outcome
  2614	has to be evaluated by variety and not only on average, since an error rate
  2615	that holds overall but shifts across the varieties being compared is the
  2616	measurement analogue of a trend that isn't parallel
  2617	\citep{reynolds2026didcorpus}. The audit reported below is that evaluation.
  2618	
  2619	One limit on it should be stated plainly. Hand-coding a tagged concordance
  2620	measures precision, the share of tagged tokens that are genuine, and says
  2621	nothing about recall, the share of genuine tokens the tagger found.
  2622	\mention{Haba} shows recall can be zero: 373 Nigerian tokens, none tagged. So
  2623	the corrected figures below are corrected for false positives only, and a
  2624	section whose genuine tokens the tagger largely missed would look clean on
  2625	this check while still being wrong.
  2626	
  2627	Two features of the corpus constrain what country conditioning can mean here.
  2628	Country is a property of the page rather than of a speaker, assigned through
  2629	Google's region function: for a site with a top-level country domain the
  2630	assignment is direct, but for a \texttt{.com} or \texttt{.org} site
  2631	\textcite[4]{daviesfuchs2015} report that Google \enquote{relies on several
  2632	heuristics, including 1) the IP address for the web server, 2) who links to
  2633	that website, and 3) who visits the website}, and they raise the question
  2634	themselves of \enquote{how well Google has correctly identified web sites by
  2635	country}. The page list was also seeded by running \enquote{hundreds of very
  2636	high frequency 3-grams (three word strings)} from the Corpus of Contemporary
  2637	American English against Google \citep[4]{daviesfuchs2015}, so the sampling
  2638	frame is anchored to American frequency data.
  2639	
  2640	The counts aren't all of one kind either, and the reason is worth stating
  2641	because it constrains what the comparison can show. English-Corpora.org tags
  2642	automatically, and the tagger handles regionalisms poorly. Counts for the core
  2643	items are restricted to the interjection tag, but restriction isn't the same as
  2644	exclusion. Hand-coding twenty tagged \mention{ha} tokens from Kenya and twenty
  2645	from Tanzania leaves six and eight respectively as interjections; the rest are
  2646	almost all the abbreviation for \term{hectare}, in agricultural and development
  2647	prose. On that basis \mention{ha} in Kenya falls from 24.9 tokens per million
  2648	to roughly 7.5, and from fifth place among the twenty countries to nineteenth.
  2649	So the tag narrows a count without cleaning it, and it fails worst where a
  2650	homograph belongs to a text type a region produces more of.
  2651	
  2652	Counts for \mention{haba} and \mention{yaar} can't be tag-restricted at all,
  2653	because
  2654	neither string is ever assigned that tag: all 373 Nigerian tokens of
  2655	\mention{haba} are tagged as common or proper nouns, 65\% of them on a reading
  2656	that includes the proper noun. The regional figures are raw strings
  2657	where the core figures are tag-restricted, which inflates the regional items
  2658	relative to the items they're set beside, though by less than it would if the
  2659	tag were reliable. The size of that inflation is indicated by
  2660	\textcite{unuabonahdaniel2020}, who work on \mention{haba} in the same

  2662	They reach the smaller figure by inspecting each hit: their data were
  2663	\enquote{manually searched in order to eliminate cases in which the BIs were

  2669	interjection in a postcolonial variety is exactly what an automatic tagger
  2670	trained elsewhere will miss.

  1081	The causal-network account adds five claims that property lists, feature
  1082	bundles, and constructional representations don't settle by themselves:
  1083	
  1084	\begin{enumerate}
  1085	\item Co-movement. Prosodic isolation, supplement function,
  1086	  non-referentiality, and pragmatic force recur in a patterned way. The
  1087	  account asks which links explain why observing one property licenses
  1088	  expectations about another.
  1089	\item Asymmetry. Some properties are upstream or more diagnostic than
  1090	  others. Prosodic packaging can make syntactic integration less
  1091	  available, while the reverse relation has a different status. A flat
  1092	  feature bundle can't represent that ordering.
  1093	\item Persistence. The category profile can remain available while
  1094	  individual forms enter and leave, which is a claim about stability rather
  1095	  than about what maintains it.
  1096	\item Boundary behaviour. Different recruitment pathways should leave
  1097	  different residues at the category boundary. Feature theory can list
  1098	  residual properties but doesn't explain why they align with
  1099	  particular histories.
  1100	\item Failure. When a prediction fails, the pattern of failure should
  1101	  identify which link or declared inference has broken. If \mention{fie}
  1102	  loses all three nodes together under community-wide attrition, the
  1103	  result is a boundary condition on ordered erosion rather than evidence of corrective
  1104	  control.
  1105	\end{enumerate}
  1106	
  1107	If a feature-based account can replicate co-movement, asymmetry,
  1108	persistence, boundary behaviour, and failure without network order,
  1109	causal structure is doing no additional explanatory work. The empirical
  1110	pressure point is whether the probes in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
  1111	support those claims.

  1113	\subsection{Three field-relative nodes}\label{sec:fieldrelative-nodes}
  1114	
  1115	The causal-network account developed by
  1116	\textcite{khalidi2013,khalidi2018nodes} also explains why
  1117	projectibility is field-relative. If kinds are nodes in causal
  1118	structures, different fields may track different nodes because they ask
  1119	different explanatory and predictive questions. The resulting categories
  1120	remain realist: each inquiry tracks the causal relations that matter for
  1121	its explanations and predictions.
  1122	
  1123	The familiar linguistic parallel is the interrogative/question
  1124	distinction. Syntacticians track \term{interrogative}: a clause type
  1125	with properties such as subject--auxiliary inversion, fronting of
  1126	interrogative words, and polar-question syntax. Pragmatists track \term{question}:
  1127	a speech act that requests information, makes an answer
  1128	relevant, and organizes uptake. The two often coincide, and they also come apart:
  1129	\mention{Can you pass the salt?} is syntactically interrogative and
  1130	pragmatically a request, while \mention{I wonder where she went} can
  1131	raise an information issue without having interrogative clause syntax.
  1132	
  1133	Interjections create a similar problem with less terminological help.
  1134	Syntax, semantics, and interactional pragmatics all use the
  1135	word \enquote{interjection}, but each field tracks a slightly different
  1136	node: different properties, different causal links, different
  1137	predictions. I distinguish these with subscripts:%
  1138	\footnote{The interrogative/question contrast uses different words for
  1139	  the syntactic and pragmatic nodes because both terms are established
  1140	  in their respective traditions. For interjections,
  1141	  every field uses the same word, so subscripts are the honest notation.
  1142	  An alternative would have been to adopt distinct terms from the
  1143	  relevant literatures: \term{expressive} from \textcite{potts2007}
  1144	  for the semantic node, and response token from
  1145	  \textcite{stivers2019} or \term{liminal sign} from
  1146	  \textcite{dingemanse2020} for the interactional-pragmatic node.
  1147	
  1148	  The Latin
  1149	  \mention{ejaculatio}, a throwing out, mirroring \mention{interjicere},
  1150	  \enquote{to throw between} \citep{libert2019},
  1151	  would have been etymologically perfect
  1152	  for the interactional-pragmatic category, but modern English has
  1153	  rendered the term unavailable.}
  1154	
  1155	\begin{itemize}
  1156	\item \intj{syn}, the syntactician's
  1157	  category (\textit{CGEL}, Quirk et al.). Distributional cluster: supplement
  1158	  function, non-inflecting, non-argument-taking. Linked by
  1159	  prosodic-syntactic coupling (Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}).
  1160	\item \intj{sem}, the semanticist's
  1161	  category, overlapping with \textcite{potts2007}'s \term{expressives}.
  1162	  Use-conditional meaning cluster: independent of at-issue content,
  1163	  nondisplaceable, perspective-dependent, descriptively ineffable
  1164	  \citep[166]{potts2007}. Often recruited through semantic bleaching.
  1165	  Different extension: prenominal \mention{damn} in
  1166	  \mention{the damn car} is \intj{sem} but not
  1167	  \intj{syn}.
  1168	\item \intj{prag}, the interactional-pragmatic
  1169	  category, overlapping with \textcite{stivers2019}'s
  1170	  response tokens and \textcite{dingemanse2020}'s liminal signs.
  1171	  Sequential/stance cluster: turn-initial, backchannel, stance display.
  1172	  Linked by routine entrenchment and possibly by regional/indexical
  1173	  conditioning, both from Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}. Different

  1294	\subsection{Recruitment pathways}\label{sec:recruitment}
  1295	
  1296	Section~\ref{sec:cluster} already pointed to three recruitment
  1297	pathways: semantic bleaching from nouns, syntactic deactivation from
  1298	verbs, and nonce coinage via onomatopoeia. The first is historically
  1299	demonstrated; the second and third are proposed pathways with clear
  1300	examples but without the extended diachronic documentation that Path~1
  1301	has. The first two are analysed in detail here; the third is taken up
  1302	in Section~\ref{sec:turnover}.
  1303	
  1304	The pragmatic issue is how changes in communicative use become
  1305	categorially consequential: grammaticalization and pragmaticalization
  1306	matter here because they alter a form's communicative job as well as its
  1307	position in a syntactic tree
  1308	\citep[cf.][]{TraugottDasher2002, heinekuteva2002lexicon}.
  1309	
  1310	\subsection{Path 1: religious noun to interjection}\label{sec:path-religious}
  1311	
  1312	\textcite{gehweiler2008} traces the development of \mention{gee} from
  1313	\mention{Jesus} through three positions:
  1314	
  1315	\ea\label{ex:bleaching}
  1316	  \ea Proper name used as invocation (\mention{Jesus!} addressed to the
  1317	    deity)
  1318	  \ex Secondary interjection: exclamatory use, no longer addressing the
  1319	    deity (\mention{Jesus!} as an expression of surprise)
  1320	  \ex Primary interjection: phonological modification yields
  1321	    \mention{Jeez} and \mention{Gee}, and \mention{gee!} detaches from
  1322	    the source noun
  1323	  \z
  1324	\z
  1325	The two transitions don't work the same way, and Gehweiler is emphatic
  1510	inferencing and syntactic position \citep[88]{gehweiler2008}.
  1511	
  1512	The same coupling is visible synchronically. Standalone
  1513	\mention{Damn!} has both properties: it occupies its own intonation unit
  1514	and has no role in clause structure. In \mention{I think that, damn,
  1515	we're going to be late}, \mention{damn} remains possible only as a
  1516	parenthetical supplement, set off from the \mention{that}-clause. In
  1517	\mention{the damn car}, the form loses both properties at once: it's
  1518	inside the noun phrase and receives the prosody of the phrase.
  1519	
  1520	The prediction is concrete. If a novel form is reliably
  1521	packaged as its own intonation unit, it should resist complementation,
  1522	modification by ordinary phrase-internal material, and coordination with
  1523	integrated phrases. If it's pulled into a host phrase, prosodic isolation should
  1524	weaken together with \intj{syn} status.
  1525	
  1526	\subsubsection{The non-referentiality ratchet}
  1527	
  1528	Bleaching removes referential content, which weakens the semantic
  1529	motivation for argument structure. A form with no entity or event to
  1530	construe is less available as an argument, complement, or modifier. Once
  1531	that syntactic role is lost, prosodic isolation becomes easier to keep
  1532	in place. The result is a one-directional cascade. Once argument
  1533	structure is lost, the form occupies a different functional niche, so
  1534	ordinary pressures toward referential syntax are reduced.
  1535	
  1536	This ratchet supports a different set of predictions from the
  1537	prosodic-syntactic link. A non-referential stance form should resist
  1538	definiteness, quantification, and argument position:
  1539	*\mention{the damn!}, *\mention{every ouch}, *\mention{Damn surprised
  1540	me}. It should also resist embedding as propositional content
  1541	(\mention{*She said that damn}) unless it's quoted as a form. If
  1542	referential content becomes active again, the form should move toward
  1543	noun, verb, or routine-formula regions of the network. The ratchet
  1544	keeps the form near the interjection node by blocking return
  1545	to the source category, while prosodic-syntactic coupling links the
  1546	central structural properties.
  1547	
  1548	\subsubsection{Interactional routine entrenchment}
  1549	
  1550	The interactional-pragmatic node needs a candidate stabilizer of its own.
  1551	Conversation repeatedly places interjectional forms in recognizable
  1552	sequential slots: continuers, repair initiators, change-of-state tokens,
  1553	stance displays, and response tokens \citep{stivers2019,dingemanse2020}.
  1554	\textcite{dingemanse2024} argues that these interactional uses are
  1555	central rather than peripheral for high-frequency interjections.
  1556	
  1557	Repeated deployment in these slots favours a particular package of
  1558	properties. Two levels are in play here, and the argument needs both. On a
  1559	given occasion, a continuer such as \mention{mm-hm} works because it can be
  1560	recognized without taking over the turn or adding propositional content.
  1561	A change-of-state token such as \mention{oh} works because it can mark
  1562	an information update before the speaker builds further at-issue
  1563	content. A stance token such as \mention{bruh} works because it can
  1564	display evaluation while leaving the surrounding proposition untouched.
  1565	Across many occasions, that occasion-level fitness is supposed to accumulate,
  1566	so that the interactional routine favours short, standalone, prosodically
  1567	independent, non-referential material in the inventory at large. The
  1568	accumulation is an assumption here rather than a finding. The probes in
  1569	Section~\ref{sec:discussion} bear on the inventory-level pattern, not on the
  1570	step from one level to the other.
  1571	
  1572	This candidate stabilizer supports the projectibility of
  1573	\intj{prag}. If a novel form is recruited as a
  1574	minimal stance, response, repair, or backchannel resource, one should
  1575	expect turn-initial or standalone availability, independent prosodic
  1576	packaging, resistance to embedding under reported speech, and
  1577	availability for uptake as an interactional move. The same candidate
  1578	stabilizer also explains why the function can persist when category membership
  1579	weakens: a full clause such as \mention{I agree} can do agreement work,
  1580	but it lacks the reduced, non-propositional packaging that makes
  1581	\mention{mm-hm} a central \intj{prag} case.

  2103	has is standalone position, prosodic isolation, morphological bareness, and
  2104	stance meaning, from which the stance reading follows. No category

  2122	\subsection{What each node projects}\label{sec:proj-fieldrelative}
  2123	
  2124	The three interjection nodes established in
  2125	Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes} do their work as different packages of
  2126	inference, not as rival definitions.
  2127	
  2128	\intj{syn} projects syntactic behaviour:
  2129	supplement function, resistance to \mention{that}-complementation,
  2130	resistance to coordination with non-interjections, and no ordinary
  2131	argument structure. These predictions are grounded in the
  2132	prosodic-syntactic coupling link from
  2133	Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}. \intj{sem}
  2134	projects meaning type, and here the projection has to reach past the
  2135	properties that admitted the form. Independence, nondisplaceability,
  2136	perspective dependence, and descriptive ineffability are what
  2137	Section~\ref{sec:fieldrelative-nodes} uses to assign the node, so
  2138	predicting them would only restate membership.
  2139	\textcite{potts2007} lists two further properties of expressive content on
  2140	the following page, and neither does admission work here. One is immediacy,
  2141	where expressives \enquote{achieve their intended act simply by being
  2142	uttered}. The other is repeatability, where repeated use has an effect
  2143	\enquote{of strengthening the emotive content, rather than one of
  2144	redundancy} \citep[167]{potts2007}. Both are testable and both can fail.
  2145	\mention{Damn, damn, damn} intensifies where \mention{The car broke, the car
  2146	broke, the car broke} doesn't, and a form that flattened into redundancy
  2147	under repetition would defeat the prediction. \intj{prag}
  2148	projects interactional-pragmatic behaviour, and the same discipline applies.
  2149	Turn-initial or standalone positioning and stance display are admission
  2150	criteria. What they let us predict is uptake: that the form will be treated
  2151	as a complete move, so that a recipient's next turn responds to it rather
  2152	than waiting for its continuation, and that it will be usable as a
  2153	backchannel without taking the floor. Both are observable in sequence, and a
  2154	form routinely treated as an incomplete turn would defeat them.
  2155	
  2156	The nodes overlap because some links, especially prosodic isolation
  2157	and non-referentiality, contribute to more than one node. They come
  2158	apart because other links are field-specific. A novel form categorized
  2159	as \intj{syn} (prosodically isolated,
  2160	non-inflecting, syntactically non-integrated) should tend also to be
  2161	\intj{sem} (use-conditional, speaker-oriented)
  2162	and \intj{prag} (turn-initial, available for
  2163	backchanneling). Central cases such as \mention{wow}, \mention{ouch},
  2164	and \mention{hey} fit that default. Boundary cases locate the decoupling, \mention{mm-hm} among them
  2165	\citep{oconnell2005}.
  2166	
  2167	The three constructions of \mention{damn} show the same point inside a
  2168	single word family.
  2169	
  2170	For standalone
  2171	\mention{damn!}, all three nodes agree: the form is prosodically
  2172	isolated, syntactically non-integrated, use-conditional, and available
  2173	as a stance marker. For \mention{damn these mosquitoes!}, the picture
  2174	is mixed: \intj{syn} holds (it heads an IntjP)
  2175	but with limited complement-taking;
  2176	\intj{sem} holds (the meaning is expressive);
  2177	\intj{prag} is weakened (it's not a backchannel or
  2178	turn-management device).
  2179	
  2180	For prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}, the nodes
  2181	diverge further: \intj{syn} fails entirely (the
  2182	form is syntactically integrated inside a noun phrase), but
  2183	\intj{sem} holds (use-conditional, perspective-
  2184	dependent). Neither \textit{CGEL}'s syntax nor Potts's semantics alone
  2185	predicts this gradient. The three-node analysis does, because it tracks
  2185	predicts this gradient. The three-node analysis does, because it tracks
  2186	which links are active in each construction.
  2187	
  2188	\subsection{Category vs.\ function}
  2189	
  2190	\textit{CGEL}'s distinction between category and function sharpens the
  2191	projectibility point: what does the \intj{syn} category label add
  2192	beyond the supplement function label? Supplements of any category
  2193	(noun phrases [NPs], adjective phrases [AdjPs], relative clauses) share
  2194	prosodic isolation and syntactic
  2195	non-integration. But \intj{syn} adds a package of
  2196	predictions that no other category-in-supplement-function supports:
  2197	
  2198	\begin{itemize}
  2199	\item Non-referentiality. Supplements can be referential
  2200	  (\mention{a university lecturer, Dr Brown, \ldots}); interjections
  2201	  characteristically can't.
  2202	\item Emotive/interpersonal force. Not all supplements express
  2203	  stance (\mention{Dr Brown} expresses none); interjections
  2204	  characteristically do (\mention{ugh}, \mention{yay}).
  2205	\item Free coinage. No other category in supplement function
  2206	  can be freely coined via onomatopoeia. Comics gave English
  2207	  \mention{bam} and \mention{pow}; nothing comparable mints new
  2208	  relative clauses.
  2209	\item Non-inflection. NP supplements inflect freely
  2210	  (\mention{a lecturer}, \mention{two lecturers}); interjections don't
  2211	  (*\mention{ouches}).
  2212	\end{itemize}
  2213	
  2214	The function label tells you about syntactic integration. The category
  2215	label tells you about inference: it licenses a package of predictions
  2216	that the function label alone can't provide. The category/function distinction matters
  2217	beyond the \textit{CGEL} tradition: any framework that distinguishes what kind of
  2218	thing an expression is from what job it's doing in a particular clause
  2219	will face the same question. The causal-network answer is that the
  2220	category earns its keep by licensing inferences that the function
  2221	doesn't.
  2222	
  2223	\subsection{What sets the domain of projection}\label{sec:proj-domain}
  2224	
  2225	Categories don't all project in the same domain, and what fixes the domain
  2226	is where the causal links run. Nouns project morphosyntactically:
  2227	\mention{lecturer} takes a determiner, inflects for number, and fills an
  2228	argument slot. Categorizing something as a noun tells you almost nothing
  2229	about its pragmatics, since nouns aren't a pragmatic category. Interjections
  2230	come out the other way, and the reason is the same reason.
  2231	
  2232	For interjections, the situation is reversed. The three nodes overlap,
  2233	and the richest projectibility belongs to
  2234	\intj{prag}, the interactional-pragmatic
  2235	cluster.
  2236	\textcite{dingemanse2024} reaches the same conclusion from interactional
  2237	data: the most frequent and functionally central interjections are
  2238	interactional tools (continuers, repair initiators, change-of-state
  2239	tokens) rather than emotive exclamations, and their primary
  2240	projectibility is about sequential position and conversational
  2241	management. For forms at the intersection of all three nodes, the strongest inferences
  2242	are about use, not denotation. \mention{Oh} shows the
  2243	asymmetry. From \intj{syn} you get what any
  2244	supplement gives you: prosodic isolation, no inflection, no argument structure.
  2245	From \intj{prag} you get a turn position,
  2246	availability as a response token, and the display of evolving knowledge
  2247	described in Section~\ref{sec:pragmatics}. Same form, and the two nodes differ
  2248	in how much they buy.
  2249	
  2250	The contrast with nouns and verbs concerns where the density lies, not how
  2251	much structure each node carries.
  2252	\intj{syn} is thin next to those nodes, lacking
  2253	the network of agreement, argument realization, derivation, and inflectional
  2254	behaviour that makes them so stable. What the three nodes divide between them
  2255	is the kind of prediction each supports, set out in
  2256	Section~\ref{sec:proj-fieldrelative}.
  2257	
  2258	Why pragmatic in this case? Because causal links generate predictions
  2259	within the domains they operate in. The prosodic-syntactic coupling from
  2260	Section~\ref{sec:synchronic-coupling}
  2261	predicts prosodic and syntactic behaviour, with no corresponding
  2262	prediction about morphological paradigms or truth-conditional content.
  2263	Regional/indexical conditioning predicts uneven persistence across
  2264	varieties, which is why \mention{lah} and \mention{yaar} are entrenched in some
  2265	Englishes and absent from others, with no corresponding prediction about
  2266	inflectional regularity. The strongest established links among the three
  2267	interjection nodes are prosodic and interactional; the regional or
  2268	indexical link remains a maintenance hypothesis. These links and
  2269	hypotheses underwrite predictions in their respective domains.
  2270	
  2271	The same point holds outside interjections. Noun projectibility is
  2272	morphosyntactic because the underlying processes (agreement, structural
  2273	analogy, discourse frequency) operate in morphosyntactic domains.
  2274	
  2275	This connects to the content/procedural distinction. The central
  2276	projectibility of interjections is procedural and use-conditional rather
  2277	than denotational \citep{TraugottDasher2002}. The
  2278	\intj{sem} (expressive) cluster is the semantic
  2279	reflex: use-conditional meaning is the semantic signature of procedural
  2280	content. The same pattern shows up elsewhere. \term{Animacy} projects grammatically (argument realization,
  2281	reference tracking); \term{colour} projects ecologically (edibility,
  2282	toxicity) but usually doesn't project grammatically in the way animacy
  2283	can \citep[cf.][]{seifart2010}. In each case, the domain of
  2284	projectibility follows from the underlying processes.
  2285	
  2286	The richest projectibility is pragmatic, but
  2287	\intj{syn} still belongs in the grammar
  2288	because it's projectible and has its own causal links. Neutralize
  2289	prosodic isolation and the form is reclassified, which is what happens to
  2290	prenominal \mention{damn} in \mention{the damn car}. The syntactic category
  2291	licenses distributional inference: it predicts supplement function,
  2292	resistance to embedding under reported speech (*\mention{She said that damn}),
  2293	and coordination restrictions, even if those predictions are less rich than the
  2294	pragmatic ones. A thin syntactic node is still a projectible syntactic
  2295	node. The framework requires reliable inference, and
  2296	\intj{syn} does.

  2300	\subsection{Empirical probes and boundary conditions}
  2301	
  2302	Section~\ref{sec:projectibility} asked what categorizing a novel form as
  2303	an interjection lets you predict about that form. At the category level,
  2304	the causal-network account should also explain patterns across the
  2305	interjectional region as a whole. As Section~\ref{sec:prototype} argued, what
  2306	the causal-network account adds to what prototype theory already accommodates
  2307	is network-order predictions with named defeat conditions. The probes
  2308	below address profile stability, boundary behaviour, and failure. They
  2309	aren't tests of corrective control.
  2310	
  2311	The strongest probe is pathway residue. If interjections are recruited by
  2312	different causal pathways, residual properties should line up with
  2313	recruitment history. The defeat condition is random distribution:
  2314	retained complements, euphemistic deformation, atypical phonology, and
  2315	regional skew should show no relation to source pathways. The evidence
  2316	in Section~\ref{sec:residues} supports the residue prediction, and the
  2317	pre-registered morphology-gain study adds an exit-residue case:
  2318	\mention{wowed}, \mention{booed}, \mention{oohed}, and \mention{shooed}
  2319	gain syntactic integration while retaining source-interjection semantics.
  2320	The payoff is pragmatic and diachronic: boundary failures diagnose
  2321	recruitment routes and explain why later uses keep source-specific
  2322	residues.
  2323	
  2324	A second probe asks whether conditioning recovers structure. Apparent
  2325	gradation at the interjection boundary should decompose when conditioned
  2326	on register, dialect, or community. The defeat condition is parity
  2327	between conditioned and pooled models. A proof-of-concept probe uses
  2328	GloWbE frequency data \citep{daviesfuchs2015}: for the 10 most frequent
  2329	interjection-tagged items plus regional and boundary items,
  2330	frequency-per-million was extracted by country (20 countries) by raw
  2331	string search, and the coefficient of variation (CV; standard deviation
  2332	divided by the mean) was computed across countries.
  2333	
  2334	The results show three tiers of geographic conditioning. Regional
  2335	interjections (\mention{lah}, \mention{yaar}, \mention{haba}) are
  2336	near-categorical: \mention{lah} occurs at 9.35 per million in Malaysia
  2337	and 6.72 in Singapore but near zero elsewhere; \mention{yaar} at 2.88
  2338	in Pakistan and 2.33 in India but near zero elsewhere; \mention{haba}
  2339	at 8.78 in Nigeria but near zero elsewhere.

  2342	intermediate but structured variation: \mention{ah} has the highest
  2343	CV among core items (0.71), with Singapore (60.42) and Jamaica (53.16)
  2344	at eight times the rate of Kenya (7.62). Even broadly distributed items
  2345	like \mention{yeah} (CV 0.55) show a 6:1 ratio between the United
  2346	States (103.78) and Tanzania (16.85). Only \mention{yes} and
  2347	\mention{no} approach uniformity (CVs of 0.21--0.22). Boundary items
  2348	continue the pattern: the fillers \mention{um} (CV 0.61) and
  2349	\mention{uh} (CV 0.76) and the routine formula \mention{bye} (CV 0.61)

  2353	uncertainty than a bare figure suggests, and it's worth saying which
  2354	comparisons survive it. Bootstrapping over the twenty sections, the

  2362	altogether: with a form absent from eighteen of twenty sections the mean sits
  2363	near zero and the coefficient becomes unstable, so \mention{lah}'s interval runs from 0.89 to 4.27. Their concentration is
  2364	better read off the counts than off a dispersion statistic.
  2365	
  2366	Conditioning on country recovers structure at every level of this
  2367	sample. Variety structures the apparent gradience between core
  2368	interjections, fillers, and boundary items.

  2371	effects for item and country) supports the same point. Adding country as
  2372	a grouping factor improves prediction over an item-only baseline: the

  2461	A third, weaker probe concerns hub-property asymmetry. One property may
  2462	be more central to network order than others, with prosodic isolation
  2463	and non-referentiality as the main candidates. The defeat condition is
  2464	equal contribution by all properties. The present evidence leaves this
  2465	prediction open.
  2466

  2471	\citep[152]{meinard2015}, reclassification is immediate. Restoring
  2472	partial referentiality, by contrast, allows the form to hover between
  2473	noun and interjection uses (as with \mention{God}). This suggests
  2474	prosodic isolation may be the hub, but the evidence is indirect.
  2475	
  2476	The probes differ in evidential weight. Pathway residue is the main
  2477	explanatory payoff, conditioning by variety is a proof of concept, and

  2479	the gradience but doesn't predict the trajectory of entry, the
  2480	pathway-specific structure of boundary cases, or the structure hidden in
  2481	apparent variation.

  2488	decomposition; if both fail under further testing, the account collapses to a descriptive
