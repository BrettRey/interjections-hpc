## 2. Form properties shared by two or more expressive uses

Biber gives no form property in these chapters that unites the expressive uses against both the interjections and the controls. The grammar splits them on purpose. Damn, hell, God, gee and heavens as exclamations get the insert positional profile (p. 1088). Bloody and fucking as modifiers are put outside inserts because they "can combine with other words in the normal clausal syntax of English" (p. 1089). You bastard is put outside expletives because insults "refer to people" (p. 1089). Candidate by candidate:

- **Integrated modifier position (P03 Y).** Holds of DAMN_PRENOM, BLOODY_PRENOM, FUCKING_PRENOM, BLOODY_INTENS and DAMN_DEGREE; THE_HELL_WH has examples only. It doesn't discriminate: very, main, black and quickly all have it. For interjections the statements are hedged ("in general", "rarely medially").
- **Amplifier class, immediately before an adjective.** BLOODY_INTENS and DAMN_DEGREE share it with VERY_INTENS (p. 551; Table 7.12, p. 561). The only difference recorded is register (not in academic prose, p. 560), and that's shared with really, real, incredibly and terribly. It's a register property, not a form property.
- **Occurring inside a standalone exclamatory phrase.** Holds of BLOODY_PRENOM ("The bloody key!", p. 1097), BLOODY_INTENS ("Bloody brilliant!", p. 518) and YOU_BASTARD (p. 1105); DAMN_REACT is itself a standalone utterance. VERY_INTENS has the same pattern ("Very special.", p. 1096; "Very pretty.", p. 1078), and adjectives and adverbs stand alone generally (pp. 518, 547). Fails as a discriminator.
- **Clause-edge position (standalone or initial, occasionally final).** Stated only for the expletive insert class, so among the expressive rows only DAMN_REACT (plus GEE and HEAVENS, which are interjection boundary rows). Biber doesn't extend it to the modifiers. It also isn't distinctive: inserts generally (p. 1077) and OKAY (p. 1048) have the same profile.
- **Formulaic combination with expletive nouns** (*bloody hell*, *fucking hell's bells*). Holds of bloody and fucking, but as a collocation exception to the class split, not as a general distributional property. The grammar says nothing about controls or interjections here.
- **After wh-words.** Examples only (the hell pp. 1047 and 1073; the heck p. 1059; on earth p. 1103), with no statement of restriction. A non-expressive item occurs in the same place: "else is used as a postmodifier of indefinite and wh-pronouns" (p. 559).
- **Insertion inside words or phrases, stress, intonation.** Neither chapter says anything about these for any expressive form. The only medial expletive is a clause-level insertion: "This is, God, a bloody afternoon wasted!" (p. 1089).

## 3. Cautions against using a property as a diagnostic

1. **Prosody.** There is no audio. "Given that we do not have prosodic information for the LSWE Corpus, we have to rely on punctuation in the transcriptions" (p. 1071). Pause marking "is impressionistic" (p. 1048). Any P12S or P12I value taken from Biber rests on transcription.
2. **Frequencies are per word, not per use.** "Because the same word is sometimes used in a number of overlapping functions, we do not attempt to distinguish the multiple functions of a single word in the frequency counts." (p. 1090). The matrix rows are uses, so the tables can't be mapped onto rows directly.
3. **Internal inconsistency.** The text says "the nasalized variants mm, mhm are more common in BrE than in AmE" (p. 1090), but Table 14.10 shows mhm AmE 25 ⬛ against BrE 4 (p. 1092). Table 14.9 has mm the other way round (AmE 2, BrE 8, p. 1091). Take care citing the backchannel figures.
4. **A category claim at odds with the matrix.** The note to Table 14.10 treats *What the hell* as a noun use (p. 1092), while items-v4 justifies THE_HELL_WH as a CGEL emotive modifier. I'm flagging this, not settling it.
5. **The edge-position criteria are tendencies, and the book contradicts them.** Medial counterexamples: oh (p. 1070), hey (p. 1083), aha (p. 1079), God (p. 1089). Hesitators occur at medial boundaries (p. 1048). Attributive and predicative restrictions are "usually not absolute" (p. 506). Classifiers are "generally non-gradable" (p. 519).
6. **Standalone use isn't distinctive.** Adverbs (p. 547), adjectives (p. 518) and phrases with very (p. 1096) all stand alone.
7. **Constructions change Biber's category.** Formulae with complements are "not ... inserts" (p. 1088). *Damn you* is a formula, not an imperative (p. 1097). Criterion (5), not being a homonym of another word class (p. 1077), excludes sorry, God, okay and yes/no from the core. So "insert" in Biber isn't a stable property of a use.
8. **Bloody and -er/-est (p. 520).** The use isn't stated, so this can't support or rule out grading for the expressive uses.
9. **Deliberate versus dysfluent repetition** is hard to tell apart (p. 1050: "there is sometimes a difficulty in deciding whether a given instance is a case of dysfluency or not"). That limits the P10 codings from hey and very.
10. **Look-alikes that aren't the matrix uses:** "poor little bastards" (p. 507, noun bastard); "a hell of a lot" (p. 545); "Must be some narky bastards in the rugby club!" (p. 1099, plural noun); "Exactly. Exactly." (p. 548).
