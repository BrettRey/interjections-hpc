## The question

Does clause-final *lah* form its own intonation unit (the interjection
profile), or is it prosodically integrated? Brett's judgement: "not its own
intonational unit but rather the fall of the larger unit."

## Answer: that is Gupta's position, and *lah* is the best case for it

**Lim (2007: 447)** lays out the state of the debate:

> "whether the particles have an independent lexical tone. While Platt (1987)
> queries this against the possibility of their being tone unit nuclei, but
> does not come to a conclusion, **Gupta holds that particles function as the
> carrier of the intonation contour of the utterance (1992; 2006)**. Platt and
> Ho (1989: 221) propose that 'for some speakers they may function as in
> Chinese, while, for others, they may be mainly the carriers of the pitch
> movement'. Lim (2004) suggests the coexistence of both systems."

So there are three positions: independent lexical tone (Lim), carrier of the
utterance contour (Gupta), and speaker-dependent variation (Platt & Ho).
Brett's is Gupta's, and it is a published position rather than an intuition.

**And *lah* is where the lexical-tone rival is weakest.** Lim (2007: 463):

> "all the SE particles, **with the exception of lah, what and perhaps ah**,
> can be seen to be the exact match of particles in Cantonese, including the
> maintenance of lexical tone (noted in Lim, 2004)."

> "Bazaar Malay sees similar particles in just two, lah and ah. If one
> considers the tone of the particles in the substrates, however, it must then
> be acknowledged and accepted that **the Mandarin and Hokkien particles have
> neutral tone and weak stress**, which would make explaining the emergence of
> prominent suprasegmental features of some of the SE particles difficult to
> account for, in particular the mid and high level pitch of SE's *lor*, *leh*
> and *meh*."

The lexical-tone analysis is built on the Cantonese-derived particles (*lor*,
*leh*, *meh*, mid and high level pitch). *Lah* comes from Bazaar Malay and
Hokkien, whose particles have neutral tone and weak stress.

**Lim (2004: 48)** poses the same question and notes the interaction:

> "how they exhibit a maintenance of their tone as in their language of origin
> (interacting with intonation in the rest of the utterance (cf. Loke & Low
> 1988)) … A question which has been posed (see Platt 1987) is whether the
> particles have an independent lexical tone and/or whether they are the nuclei
> of their respective tone units."

**Chong (2012: 59)**, modelling SgE intonational phonology in AM terms, leaves
the particles out as unresolved: they "have been argued to have
lexically-specified tone (see Lim 2004 for a summary). How these would fit into
the present intonational model is an open question."

## What the paper can now say

The two rival analyses **agree on the point the paper needs**. On Gupta's
account *lah* carries the utterance's intonation contour; on the lexical-tone
account it is a weakly-stressed, neutral-tone enclitic inherited from Hokkien
and Bazaar Malay. Neither makes it a prosodically independent unit. So *lah*
fails the prosodic-isolation criterion for \intj{syn} on either analysis, and
the paper does not have to pick a side to say so.

That, with the KWIC finding that *lah* is clause-final and orthographically
attached in 20 of 20 tokens, supports treating *lah* as \intj{prag} without
\intj{syn}: a third decoupling case alongside prenominal *damn* and *mm-hm*.
