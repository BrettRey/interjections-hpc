Bounded source check: positive grammatical interjection core
Date: 2026-11-03
Scope: CGEL 2002, p. 1361 and its immediately preceding examples; Ameka 1992, primary forms/particles/conatives; Wilkins 1992, formal definition and single-word utterances. Local texts and corresponding PDF text only. No outward search or manuscript edits.

1. Supported grammatical core

CGEL explicitly lists ah, hey, oh, oops, ouch, sh, ugh, wow, and the now dated alas as central English interjections. In their sole or primary meanings, these are used independently or as supplements anchored to clauses; they do not combine with other words in ordinary integrated syntactic constructions. This directly supports a shared grammatical profile for a core, rather than treating the grammatical category as a label with no expectations. Source: literature/huddlestonpullum2002.md:41525-41530; printed pp. 1360-1361 (examples [31] on 1360; definition/list/distribution on 1361).

Ameka defines primary interjections by their independent utterance capacity and their normally not entering constructions with other word classes; examples include Ouch!, Wow!, Gee!, Oho!, and Oops!. He illustrates co-occurrence with Gee, you look like you had it! and contrasts independent Oh! with elliptical And? Source: literature/ameka1992-interjections.md:197-231; printed p. 105. His morphological generalisation is qualified: interjections normally take neither inflection nor derivation; apparent verbal inflections can be frozen rather than participating in agreement. Source: lines 253-263, p. 106.

Wilkins gives a deliberately formal definition: a conventional lexical form that conventionally constitutes an utterance, typically does not combine with other word classes, is usually monomorphemic, and generally does not host inflectional or derivational morphology. The hedges admit conventionalised extensions and uncertain boundaries; the unhedged version identifies his clearest core. Sources: literature/wilkins1992.md:33-53; printed pp. 124-125. His point is explicitly to investigate semantic/pragmatic commonalities after formal identification, rather than place them in the formal criterion (same passage).

Safe synthesis: a core of conventional lexical utterances with a characteristic distribution outside ordinary clause integration, normally morphologically invariant, capable of independent use and (in CGEL's analysis) clausal supplementation. These are grammatical expectations. An expressive, evaluative, affective, or interactional grouping requires its own evidence. CGEL itself couples its grammatical definition with expressive/nonpropositional meaning; that does not make the formal descriptions unavailable for a separately stated grammatical argument.

2. Standalone/supplement transfer: warranted, but bounded

CGEL p. 1361 directly attributes both independent and clausal-supplement use to its listed central members. It therefore warrants expecting these two environments for a member of that grammatical core. Ameka p. 105 supplies independent/co-utterance examples; p. 108 contrasts interjections' syntactic independence and separate intonation units with integrated particles (lines 336-350). His particle/interjection distinction is at lines 298-326, printed p. 107; discourse marker is a functional grouping that can include either.

This is a qualitative distributional expectation, not a measured probability, held-out prediction, or claim that every member freely occurs in every position with every clause. Standalone occurrence alone is not enough to identify the category. Nor do these sources establish that an expressive semantic classification independently predicts this distribution.

There is also genuine analytic disagreement. Wilkins treats the oh in Oh, don't go on like an old fool as a particle use rather than an independent interjectional utterance; his note acknowledges Ameka's alternative analysis as paratactic co-utterance and grounds his own choice partly in intonation. Sources: literature/wilkins1992.md:57-83 and 906; printed pp. 126-127, note 5. Do not cite Wilkins as unqualified agreement with CGEL's supplement analysis. Ameka's strong pause/intonation statement is a source claim, not independently verified prosodic evidence here.

CGEL also expressly allows exceptional interjection phrases with NP complements, tentatively analysing Damn these mosquitoes! and Fuck you! that way. Source: literature/huddlestonpullum2002.md:41548; printed p. 1361, note 70. Thus no-combination is a central pattern, not an exceptionless boundary for every interjectional extension. Supplement is a function realised by other categories too; independent or supplementary status is not by itself a lexical-category diagnostic (the adjacent CGEL discussion, lines 41480-41523, shows clauses, AdjPs, PPs and AdvPs as supplements).

3. Why ordinary Stop! and nominal answer fragments do not thereby qualify

The relevant criterion is a lexeme's conventional grammatical distribution, not the number of orthographic words in one utterance. Wilkins's direct counterexample is the adjective Black. answering What colour was her car?: it does not thereby become an interjection. Source: literature/wilkins1992.md:53, printed pp. 124-125; his ellipsis explanation at line 97, p. 129. Applying that distinction to a nominal answer is an inference from his criterion, not his own quoted example. A noun used as an answer retains its ordinary nominal combinatorics; isolated answer use does not demonstrate an interjectional lexical use. Do not require every fragment analysis to be syntactic ellipsis: his narrower practical contrast concerns the linguistic context supplying the answer's interpretation.

Likewise, ordinary Stop! is a one-word imperative with stop retaining its verbal use and ordinary verbal combinatorics (e.g. Stop the car! and Don't stop!). Those examples are illustrative analysis, not examples in the bounded source passages. Wilkins explicitly treats reduced imperatives separately: their predictable addressee argument can be supplied extralinguistically; that contextual similarity does not make them interjections. Source: literature/wilkins1992.md:97-101, printed p. 129. CGEL's reanalysis claim for damn etc. specifically invokes loss of verbal meaning in the interjectional use (line 41530, p. 1361). That extra analysis is absent from an ordinary directive Stop!.

Qualification: Ameka does count conventionally used alarm/attention calls such as Help!, Fire!, and Careful! as secondary interjections (lines 488-510, p. 111). It would therefore overstate agreement to claim that all nominal or imperative-looking standalone calls are excluded. What is excluded is automatic reclassification solely because a token is a complete one-word utterance.

4. hey/sh and semantic diversity

Ameka explicitly identifies sh! as conative, glossed as wanting silence, and characterises conatives as seeking attention or a listener's action/response. Source: literature/ameka1992-interjections.md:601-607, printed p. 113. He separately identifies hey as a conative interjection, while allowing a vocative-particle use with names: lines 660-665, p. 115. CGEL includes both in its grammatical core (p. 1361).

Ameka distinguishes expressive interjections (emotive or cognitive) from conative and phatic groupings: lines 582-607, p. 113; examples include emotional/sensory Wow!, Ouch!, Ugh! and cognitive Aha!. He also allows overlapping functions and classifies by the predominant one: lines 622-630, p. 114. Thus the evidence supports saying that a shared grammatical profile need not entail a single affective/evaluative profile. It does not support saying that hey/sh have no expressive, evaluative, or affective uses, or that the functional groupings are disjoint. Broad 'stance' can be stretched to include wants, but these passages do not establish it as a uniform nontrivial prediction across the grammatical core.

Conclusion for revision: a positive grammatical core is supported. Its useful expectations concern independent lexical utterance capacity, the absence of ordinary integrated combination/productive inflection, and CGEL-style supplementation, with the qualifications above. Keep this claim separate from a stronger claim that grammatical membership predicts a uniform meaning, sequential action, or quantitative generalisation. Stop reached: assigned passages checked; no broader investigation undertaken.
