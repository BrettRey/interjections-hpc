# A grammatical core with consequences

Revise the separate working manuscript, `inquiry-relative-categories.tex`, around a positive grammatical case. WTWSS supplies the starting point: a grouping's grammatical standing depends on the grammatical expectations it licenses; overlapping semantic groupings have their own standing. The paper will apply that account rather than reopen a competition between grammatical and semantic definitions of one all-purpose category.

## Proposed analysis

The grammatical category groups conventional lexical uses with a recurrent distribution: they can constitute independent, non-elliptical utterances and occur as supplements, while lacking the ordinary inflection and integrated constituent patterns of nouns, verbs, and adjectives in those uses. The profile combines positive possibilities with restrictions. Standalone occurrence or invariance alone doesn't identify the category.

Use **ah, hey, oh, oops, ouch, sh, ugh, and wow** as a working core, following *CGEL*'s central examples (p. 1361). Its additional example *alas* shows that datedness needn't make grammatical membership insecure. “Core” means a set of well-supported assignments, not an intersection of grammatical, semantic, and interactional classifications, and not a claim that these are the most frequent forms. The inventory concerns the identified conventional uses, not every occurrence of these spellings.

The supporting sources already present in the paper are *CGEL* (p. 1361), Ameka (1992, especially the primary-interjection discussion), and Wilkins (1992, the formal definition and distinction between conventional utterances and contextual fragments). Their analyses supply the descriptive warrant. The revision won't present constructed examples as a newly conducted test.

## Proposed argumentative sequence

### 1. Establish what the grammatical grouping lets us expect

Open the new grammatical subsection with *Oops!* and *Oops, I've dropped it*, and *Ouch!* and *Ouch, that hurt*. In the longer examples the interjection supplements the clause; it doesn't fill an argument or modifier position inside it. Its independent use doesn't require an unspoken clause to supply its grammatical structure.

The inferential payoff is the connection between environments: given evidence identifying a member in one environment, the established category profile warrants expectations about its use in another, including the relation to a following clause. The observed use and projected environment must remain distinct. State once that the paper adopts this generalization from the grammatical descriptions and illustrates it with constructed examples; it hasn't estimated a new success rate.

Then introduce the core and the restrictions that distinguish it from nearby analyses. Retain the existing examples showing that relative clauses and noun phrases can also function as supplements.

### 2. Make the alternatives concrete

Compare *Ouch!* with an ordinary imperative *Stop!* and a nominal answer such as *Coffee* after *What would you like?* The imperative retains verbal structure and possibilities such as *Stop the car* and *Don't stop*. The nominal answer retains noun-phrase organization, visible in *Some coffee*. Their ability to constitute an utterance doesn't erase that grammar.

These comparisons support a lexical analysis of the core without deciding every isolated expression's category. They don't settle the grammatical analysis of depictive *Bang!* or conventional routine formulae. They also don't make lack of a particular inflection a necessary condition for every member.

### 3. Let meaning and interaction cross the grammatical core

Attention-getting *Hey!* and silencing *Sh!* belong in the grammatical comparison alongside *Ouch!* and *Wow!*; Ameka's conative grouping supplies the relevant contrast with affective uses. The core therefore doesn't require every member to convey the evaluative or affective stance used to identify the paper's narrower expressive grouping.

Keep *CGEL*'s use of “expressive” distinct from that narrower definition. A broad semantic characterization in a grammar doesn't by itself establish that all its members share the particular expressive properties being investigated here.

### 4. Use *damn* to separate the profiles

Move from the established core to standalone and supplementary *damn*, which *CGEL* analyzes as reanalysed interjectional uses. Then compare prenominal *damn* in *the damn car*: expressive commonality persists across a grammatical difference. Treat *damn + NP* separately because its internal complementation supports a real dispute between verbal and exceptional-interjection analyses.

This gives the section a completed progression: a supported grammatical grouping, differences of contribution among its members, and expressive commonality extending across its boundary. The expressive–depictive comparison and the genuine encoding rivalry follow naturally from that progression.
