                                                        Abstract
                Linguistic category disputes often seek one classification for expressions that support sev-
           eral kinds of inference. I develop an account of inquiry-relative categories through English in-
           terjections. Established grammatical descriptions support a core including ouch, oops, hey, and
           sh: their conventional uses connect independent utterance with clausal supplementation and
           restricted integration. Grammatical membership also supports expectations about character-
           istic meanings and conversational uses, separate intonational packaging, and possibilities for
           unusual phonology. Within syntax, interjections, noun phrases, and relative clauses can share
           supplement function, whose prosodic expectations cross category boundaries. Expressive con-
           tribution connects standalone Damn! with prenominal damn across a grammatical difference.
           Comparing stance with sound depiction in Bang! shows why even one semantic inquiry can
           require several distinctions. These cases show how crosscutting classifications can be mutually
           informative. A category’s basis of identification doesn’t confine the domain of its useful predic-
           tions, and a partial association doesn’t erase the differences between groupings. Several classific-
           ations can be correct without one superseding the others, while incompatible claims about the
           same use remain open to empirical adjudication.

     A grammatical answer begins with a category whose members support expectations about their
use in constructions. Ouch! can constitute an utterance and supplement a clause in Ouch, that hurt.
Attention-getting hey and silencing sh share the broad grammatical profile despite their different con-
versational jobs. Their grammatical membership also supports expectations about meaning and real-
ization: reactions, affective displays, and attempts to direct attention or action are characteristic, as is
separate intonational packaging. A grammatical classification can thus guide inquiry beyond gram-
mar.
     Syntax itself requires more than lexical classification. In Ah, so you were there after all!, ah is an
interjection functioning as a supplement. A noun phrase or relative clause can serve that function
too. Classifying by function connects expressions that category distinguishes, with expectations of
its own about their relation to an anchor and their prosodic packaging.
     A comparison with depictive Bang! exposes a further distinction. The selected damn use conveys
an evaluative stance; the selected Bang! depicts a sound. Both concern meaning, but they motivate
different groupings and different questions about interpretation. Grouping one under semantics
and the other under semiotics would do nothing to resolve those questions. Nor would calling both
interjections establish that they share the relevant semantic properties.
     This paper asks what warrants recognizing a category for an inquiry. An inquiry identifies a ques-
tion and a standard of success; evidence constrains which grouping answers it. A field can pursue
several inquiries, an inquiry can employ several categories, and a category can serve questions pur-
sued across fields. What establishes membership and what membership warrants expecting are dis-
tinct questions. English interjections make this many-to-many relation visible because grammatical,
semantic, interactional, phonological, and historical descriptions repeatedly organize overlapping ma-
terial.
     A grouping can make measurement comparable or description informative. The distinctions
among damn uses have descriptive value even before a new prediction is tested. Projectibility
concerns the warrant for expectations beyond the evidence used to identify a case (Goodman, 1955).
Neither descriptive usefulness nor a successful projection identifies its own causal explanation. These
achievements need separate assessment.
     Pluralism about useful and objectively grounded groupings has substantial precedents. Dupré
(1993, pp. 5–7, 17–18) relates the choice of classification to the goals of inquiry while rejecting one
uniquely privileged division of things. Khalidi (2013, pp. 63–64, 69–72) defends crosscutting kinds
answering different epistemic interests. Their wider metaphysical commitments needn’t all be adop-
ted to recognize that distinct questions can require overlapping groupings.
     Within linguistics, Haspelmath (2010, pp. 663, 666, 680) distinguishes language-particular de-
scriptive categories from concepts designed for cross-language comparison. The division of labour
here is different: several inquiries within English can organize the same expressions differently. The
assessment developed here starts with a positive grammatical case and follows its relation to other
groupings. The grammatical category supports a wider profile of expectations, including expressive
and conative contributions. An expressive grouping connects uses across its grammatical boundary.
Their partial alignment makes the classifications informative about one another while leaving each
with work to do. Specifying the bearer, grouping, and intended inference also shows when two ana-

2   Several classifications of signs and constructions
    Grammars and studies of meaning and interaction organize the traditional interjection domain differ-
    ently. Jespersen (1924, p. 90) treated interjection as a manner in which words of other categories are
    used. The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language (CGEL) recognizes a lexical category (Hud-
    dleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 22). The Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English places in-
    terjections within the broader class of inserts, while assigning damn, gosh, and gee to expletives (Biber
    et al., 1999, pp. 1082–1083, 1094–1095).
         The disputes concern a well-studied domain. Libert (2019) questions the description of interjec-
    tions as neglected; Cram (2008, p. 63) identifies their recurring role in controversies over the syntax–
    pragmatics and semantics–pragmatics boundaries. The following comparisons preserve what those
    traditions distinguish without treating each tradition as the source of exactly one category.
    2.1 A grammatical core and what it projects
    Someone drops a cup and says Oops!; someone catches a finger in a drawer and says Ouch!. Either re-
    action can also accompany a clause. The following constructed pairs illustrate the two environments.
        (1)   a. Oops!     Oops, I’ve dropped it!
              b. Ouch!     Ouch, that hurt!
        In the independent utterances, the lexical item supplies the expression without an unspoken
    clause. In the longer examples, it functions as a supplement to the clause. I’ve dropped it and that
    hurt are already complete: the reaction fills no argument or integrated modifier position within them.
    These are two characteristic environments for the grammatical category interjection.
        I take ah, hey, oh, oops, ouch, sh, ugh, and wow, in their conventional interjectional uses, as a work-
    ing core. CGEL identifies these as central members and attributes both independent and supplement-
    ary use to them (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 1361). Its additional example alas illustrates how

a dated form can have a secure grammatical assignment. Here, core concerns the support for those
assignments, rather than frequency or typicality.
     The shared profile combines these positive possibilities with restricted integration. The selected
uses are characteristically invariant and don’t participate in the ordinary argument, predicative, and
modifier patterns of nouns, verbs, and adjectives. Ameka (1992, pp. 105–106) describes primary in-
terjections through their independent utterance capacity, limited combination, and lack of product-
ive inflection. Wilkins (1992, pp. 124–125) likewise identifies a formal profile of conventional lexical
utterances, keeping their semantic and pragmatic properties available for separate investigation.
     This category licenses an inference between environments. An analyst who has established the
grammatical assignment of reaction ouch from independent uses can expect it also to supplement an
intact clause. From an identified supplementary oops, the analyst can expect an independent utterance
without recovering a missing predicate. The grammatical descriptions warrant these expectations
about the core; the pairs in (1) illustrate their application. Neither the examples nor the category
claim imply free combination with every clause or a measured rate of predictive success.
     An isolated word can have a different analysis. Ordinary Stop! is an imperative clause: the verb
retains the possibilities illustrated by Stop the car! and Don’t stop!. A reply Coffee to What would you
like? retains nominal organization, also visible in Some coffee. These constructed comparisons extend
the contrast drawn by Wilkins (1992, pp. 124–125) with adjectival Black as an answer to a question
about colour. Utterance status alone doesn’t reclassify the word.
     The core also supports expectations beyond distribution. Its members characteristically express
reactions, give voice to affective or cognitive states, or seek a listener’s attention or action. A speaker
can use Ouch! to react to pain, Hey! to attract attention, and Sh! to request silence. Ameka (1992,
pp. 113–115) distinguishes expressive, conative, and phatic functions while allowing overlap. The
grammatical grouping gives an initial expectation about this range of contributions; the narrower
groupings specify which one.
     The expectation extends to realization. Interjections characteristically form their own intonation
units, including when they accompany another utterance (Ameka, 1992, p. 108). The core also ad-
mits sound patterns outside ordinary English words: sh can lack a vowel (Ameka, 1992, pp. 105–
106), and ugh can contain a consonant absent from the usual standard British and American English
inventories (Quirk et al., 1985, pp. 74, 853). Grammatical membership thus gives reason to anticip-
ate separate prosodic packaging and to allow for unusual segmental realizations.
     These expectations are defeasible and differ in strength. Separate intonational packaging is char-
acteristic; unusual segmental phonology is a recurrent possibility, alongside ordinary sound patterns.
The descriptions support this qualitative profile without supplying numerical probabilities. Where
the grammatical assignment has been established independently of the target property, it can guide
expectations about that property. Section 4 develops the phonological and prosodic relations.
     CGEL’s broad characterization of interjection meaning as expressive (Huddleston & Pullum,
2002, p. 1361) fits this wider profile. It leaves room for the narrower expressive grouping developed
below, which concerns evaluative or affective stance. Conative sh can be a secure grammatical mem-
ber without requiring that contribution. The category’s grammatical standing and its wider inferen-
tial reach go together: distributional generalizations establish one contribution, while associations
with meaning, use, and realization extend its value.1

2.2 Category and syntactic function
A syntactician also asks what an expression does in a construction. CGEL distinguishes category, such
as noun phrase, from function, such as subject or object: expressions of the same category can have
different functions, and different categories can realize the same function (Huddleston & Pullum,
2002, p. 23). Interjections participate in this familiar crosscutting. Interjection identifies a lexical
category; supplement identifies a function.
     Compare the interjection with the relative clause and noun phrase in these examples, adapted
from CGEL (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, pp. 1356–1357, 1360).
    (2)   a. Ah, so you were there after all!
          b. We called in to see Sue’s parents, which made us rather late.
          c. A university lecturer, Dr Brown, was arrested for the crime.
     The highlighted expressions differ in category and internal structure, but each supplements an
anchor. The interjection and relative clause have clausal anchors; Dr Brown has the noun phrase a
university lecturer as its anchor. Functional classification groups them by that relation. Category
classification preserves differences relevant to their internal organization and distribution elsewhere.
     The functional grouping has inferential value. In the central cases, supplements are intonation-
ally separate from the surrounding sentence (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 1350). An independ-
ently established supplementary analysis gives reason to expect that packaging whether the expression
is an interjection, a noun phrase, or a relative clause. This expectation reaches beyond the interjection
inventory. It also explains why intonational separation alone doesn’t identify an interjection.
     The converse comparison keeps category constant. In the constructed Dr Brown was arrested
for the crime, the same noun phrase functions as subject. Its category doesn’t require the detached
packaging expected of the supplement in (2c). A functional assignment concerns the expression’s
relation in a construction; the noun phrase retains its category across those different relations.
     Part of the interjection’s wider profile is shared through its characteristic functions. Knowing its
lexical category supports an expectation that it can supplement a clause; knowing that it functions
as a supplement supports an expectation about that occurrence’s packaging. These are connected
inferences. Their usefulness doesn’t require either classification to predict something unavailable
from every other description.
     An independent Ah! supplies a contrasting environment for the same lexical category. Inde-
pendent utterance is a constructional status here, not an additional syntactic function. The category
records possibilities across environments; the functional analysis identifies a relation within one. A
syntactic inquiry can need both classifications, each preserving generalizations the other leaves open.

the link to the core; their conveyed stance supplies a different link to expressive uses elsewhere in the

     Prenominal damn, in the damn car, occurs inside the noun phrase while contributing an atti-
tude toward its referent. Potts (2007, pp. 171, 182) distinguishes that expressive contribution from
descriptive content. Grammatical integration leaves a semantic relation to standalone Damn! avail-
able for analysis. The grammatical analysis separates these uses; the expressive analysis groups them
together. Each preserves a relation needed for its own question.
     Calling prenominal damn a modifier specifies its function within the noun phrase. It leaves the
lexical-category analysis open here. The contrast with supplementary damn thus includes a func-
tional difference as well as the question of category. Its expressive contribution remains available
across both constructions.
     Complement-taking Damn these mosquitoes! presents a further grammatical question. The
phrase’s external detachment is compatible with complementation inside it. CGEL tentatively treats
the head as an exceptional interjection taking a noun-phrase complement (Huddleston & Pullum,
2002, 1361 n. 70). A verbal analysis remains a substantive alternative. This disagreement concerns
the head’s grammar; recognizing expressive commonality with the other damn uses leaves it intact.
     The semantic grouping here is expressive signs: conventional uses whose deployment conveys
an evaluative or affective stance, rather than merely describing that stance. This intended contribu-
tion is narrower than all non-descriptive or use-conditional meaning. A conventional instruction for
managing interpretation or interaction needn’t express such a stance. Potts’s account supplies expect-
ations about perspective dependence, immediacy, and intensifying repetition (Potts, 2007, pp. 166–
167). For damn, these ask whose attitude is conveyed, whether the utterance displays that attitude
on the occasion of speaking, and whether repeating the form strengthens it. These semantic expect-
ations remain available across the grammatical difference. Their extension to other expressive signs
depends on the evidence for those signs and properties.
     Expressive commonality extends across the grammatical boundary. Prenominal damn qualifies
because of its conveyed stance, while the grammatical core includes conative uses for which that stance
isn’t required. A fuller account of expressives can include integrated signs with no traditional interjec-
tion label. Selecting examples from interjection disputes lets us compare the two groupings without
adding a semantic membership condition based on that traditional label.
2.4 Expressive and depictive signs
Compare the selected expressive use of Damn! with a sound depiction Bang! in a narrative. The
bearer in each case is the conventional use, not every occurrence of the spelling. The question is
what contribution the use makes to interpretation. The strongest simple alternative groups both as
expressive interjections on the basis of their vividness and standalone packaging. That alternative still
has to explain the difference between conveying an attitude and presenting a sound.
     A depictive sign conventionally presents a sound, event, or sensory scene through a resemb-
lance between aspects of its performance and what it depicts. Dingemanse’s account of ideophones
supplies the wider comparison: conventional lexical items can depict sensory imagery, and their gram-
matical organization varies across languages (Dingemanse, 2018, pp. 2–3). The term expressive is
itself used for ideophones in some traditions; that usage doesn’t identify them with Potts’s express-
ives.
     For the selected Bang!, the relevant contribution is a portrayal of an impact sound. For Damn!,
it’s the conveyed stance toward a situation. The distinction changes the interpretation of a possible
modification. In a constructed narrative, bang, bang, bang can multiply portrayed impacts; repeated

damn can strengthen the conveyed stance. Repetition can thus transform the contribution in dif-
ferent ways. These examples articulate the contrast to be explained; they aren’t results from a new
elicitation study.
     The literature motivates those different targets. Potts treats repetition as potentially strengthen-
ing expressive contribution (Potts, 2007, pp. 181–182). Dingemanse connects depictive perform-
ance with iconic mappings between form and scene (Dingemanse, 2018, pp. 10–11). An investig-
ation can ask whether repetition changes portrayed event structure, conveyed stance, or both. It
needn’t assume one answer for every form traditionally called an interjection.
     The descriptive comparison has a definite result. Separate expressive and depictive groupings
preserve a difference in contribution that the undifferentiated alternative omits. A richer alternative
could keep one superordinate class and record the difference as subtypes or dimensions. For this
descriptive task, that’s an acceptable equivalent if it retains the two relations. The result requires the
distinction; it doesn’t require a particular taxonomic rank.
     Existing accounts can also warrant expectations about how a contribution will change. Potts’s
repetition generalization gives reason to expect intensification for his expressive cases; the depictive
comparison asks how repeated performance maps onto the portrayed scene. The projected beha-
vior needs to remain distinct from the assignment evidence. Identifying a use as depictive from its
response to repetition and then predicting that response would recycle the same observation.
     The groupings also needn’t be disjoint. A depiction can convey an attitude, and an expressive per-
formance can exploit resemblance. What matters is whether the relevant contribution is conventional
in the selected use and what follows from it. Semantics can investigate both relations, or several more,
without assigning each to a separate department or requiring one category to represent the semantic
perspective as a whole.
2.5 Contribution, encoding, and competing analyses
Consider the constructed utterance The damn car won’t start, said after several failed attempts at igni-
tion. The speaker reports a starting problem and conveys a negative attitude. Calling damn expressive
identifies that second contribution. Asking how the wording helps a hearer recover the attitude raises
a different question about its encoding.
     Wharton (2016) develops a procedural account of expressive meaning, connecting guidance for
interpretation with the showing of emotion. Applied to the car example, such an account would treat
damn as a cue for recognizing the attitude being shown. The use could then be expressive in contri-
bution and procedural in encoding. Those descriptions would answer different questions about the
same utterance.
     Now imagine a narrator representing the car’s backfire with Bang!. The sign depicts a noise,
and its performance can resemble what it depicts. That iconic presentation differs from damn’s con-
veyed stance. Identifying the depiction still leaves open how the sign guides interpretation: resemb-
lance alone doesn’t decide whether it supplies conceptual content, an interpretive procedure, or some
combination.
     Two accounts would compete if they made exclusive claims about the same damn use. Suppose
one assigned it only conceptual content, such as a negative evaluation, while the other assigned it only
a procedure for recognizing an attitude. These are hypothetical rivals, not summaries of Potts and
Wharton. The first needn’t make annoyance part of what the sentence asserts. Their incompatible
claims about encoding would require adjudication.

    Finer groupings also become visible in ordinary situations. Someone touches a hot pan and says
Ouch!; someone finds missing keys under a newspaper and says Aha!. The situations are constructed;
Ameka (1992, p. 113) gives the forms as examples of emotive and cognitive interjections. The first
gives voice to a sensation, the second displays a change in knowledge or recognition.
    Ameka’s mental-state grouping is broader than the stance grouping used here. Finding the keys
can be displayed with Aha! without conventionally evaluating them. That reading motivates a cog-
nitive subtype; it doesn’t prevent another occurrence from also conveying affect. Different contri-
butions, crosscutting accounts of encoding, and rival encoding hypotheses thus have different con-
sequences even within this small set of expressions.
2.6 Interactional resource classes
Consider two constructed exchanges. A teller says I tried the front door, a listener responds Mm-hm,
and the teller continues but it was locked. The listener supports continuation of the story. In a second
exchange, someone says We meet at five; a recipient who missed the time responds Huh?, and the first
speaker repeats At five. Here the response prompts repair.
     The difference concerns what happens next: the story proceeds after the continuer, while the
meeting time is repeated after the repair initiator. Closing bye has another job, participating in the end
of an encounter. Accounts of interjections in conversation make these sequential differences central
(Dingemanse, 2024, pp. 257–259, 263–265). Grammatical interjectionhood can orient expectations
toward reactive or interaction-managing uses; these finer categories identify the particular sequential
relation.
     For comparison, these can be collected as conventional action formats: non-elliptical resources
that can perform their action without a lexically specified host. The working class collects resources
whose particular sequential consequences need separate characterization. Predictions about whether
another turn continues a story or repairs an utterance belong to particular resource classes and envir-
onments. A broader grouping can remain informative at a coarser level without deciding between
them.
     The criterion has consequences at the boundary. Response okay and closing or thanking thanks
are candidates insofar as their selected uses conventionally perform the relevant action. A formulaic
greeting how are you? can qualify despite having several words. An ordinary imperative stop! doesn’t
qualify merely because clause structure permits it to stand alone; a claim that it’s also a conventional
routine needs evidence beyond that structure.
     Host-dependent please in please sit down belongs to a different comparison. A standalone appeal
Please! would have to be assessed as that selected use. Thus the admission rule concerns conventional
action formats, rather than brevity, wordhood, or every expression capable of being uttered alone.
These constructed comparisons specify the proposal; they don’t supply an annotated inventory.
     The grammatical treatment of mm-hm and huh? likewise needs more than standalone occur-
rence. Ameka (1992, p. 114) includes the former among phatic interjections; Wilkins (1992, p. 152)
treats repair huh? as a conventional utterance. For the selected uses, I adopt the non-elliptical ana-
lysis: neither needs a recovered nominal head or clause to supply its interpretation. The grammatical
assignments below are defeasible analyses supported by these descriptions, rather than deductions
from punctuation.

    2.7 Bearers, relations, and distinct questions
    A sign-type is an identifiable expression in a conventional use. Its profile is the combination
    of grammatical, semantic, interactional, and other properties attributed to it. A use can retain its
    identity while a proposed property turns out to be absent. Otherwise every surprising observation
    would identify a different object and leave the original proposal immune to correction.
        The recurrent contrast between standalone and prenominal damn supports distinguishing their
    conventional uses. Table 1 separates category from function or constructional status. The category
    column concerns the listed expression, whether a lexical item, phrase, or clause. In damn + NP, it
    concerns the head damn; the whole phrase’s distribution remains a distinct observation. The entries
    are analytical judgments, not annotation results.
        A function belongs to an expression in a constructional relation. The sign-type’s profile records
    which relations its realizations can enter. Thus Dr Brown can retain its noun-phrase category across
    subject and supplement function, while ah can retain its interjection category across supplementary
    and independent use. The table’s status descriptions keep independent utterance distinct from syn-
    tactic function.
        The table records crossings within syntax as well as across inquiries. Supplement function con-
    nects interjections, a noun phrase, and a relative clause. The grammatical interjection core groups
    reactions with conatives. Expressive classification connects standalone and prenominal damn, while
    stance and depiction distinguish contributions within meaning. A single ordering from less to more
    interjectional can’t substitute for these inquiry-specific distinctions without losing relevant informa-
    tion. An ordering may still be useful for a specified task.
        Bang! makes the unresolved grammatical question concrete. English also has nominal bang, as
    in the constructed a loud bang and two bangs. A standalone occurrence needs assessment against
    a nominal-fragment analysis. Its depictive interpretation can be recognized while that question re-
    mains open. Section 4 completes the related assessment of what phonological evidence can decide.

3   What warrants a class, a category, or an explanation?
    The grammatical core supports expectations about distribution, characteristic contributions, and
    forms of realization. Supplement function supports expectations across lexical and phrasal categor-
    ies. Expressive classification extends some of those expectations across a grammatical boundary; the
    expressive–depictive comparison distinguishes further relations within meaning. Each grouping can
    supply information relevant to another inquiry. Assessing the particular inferences explains both
    their usefulness and their limits: the groupings overlap without becoming interchangeable, and reli-
    able associations still leave their causal explanation open.

     The bearer is the object grouped. Two recordings of The damn car won’t start contain two
realizations of the selected prenominal damn use. Counting the recordings gives two occurrences,
not two sign-types. A phonologist can classify the individual productions; a lexical inquiry can ask

it and the relations among its constituents. A historian may instead classify a change connecting an
earlier sign with a later one.
     Functional classification identifies an expression together with its constructional relation. The
noun phrase Dr Brown is a supplement to an anchor in (2c) and a subject in Dr Brown was arrested
for the crime. These assignments concern different relations of an expression with the same category.
Functions can also group complex expressions, such as the relative clause in (2b), whose whole word-
ing needn’t be conventionalized.
     The grouping basis supplies the assignment evidence. Prenominal damn enters the expressive
grouping because of its conveyed stance, while its position inside the damn car supplies grammatical
evidence. Grouping it with standalone Damn! for the first reason doesn’t erase their difference in the
second respect. Other proposals can use ancestry or participation in a social repertoire; each needs to
identify the relation its membership records.
     The grouping basis doesn’t set a disciplinary boundary around the contribution. Grammatical
evidence can identify an interjection whose prosodic realization is still unknown. The category’s
established profile then supports an expectation about its packaging. Conversely, expressive contri-
bution can motivate a comparison between grammatically different damn uses. The source of an
assignment and the target of an inference needn’t belong to the same domain. Nor does remaining
within syntax select between lexical-category, phrasal-category, and functional classifications.
     The contribution identifies what the grouping accomplishes. A constructed class can fix a
comparison set. A descriptive class can organize a recurrent relation across independently identified
cases. A category claim requires evidence that co-occurring properties support induction beyond the
application conditions. A causal claim requires evidence about the process producing an outcome.
These achievements can accumulate, but evidence for an earlier one doesn’t establish a later one auto-
matically.
     Descriptive success can be assessed without requiring a prediction about a differently named
property. The expressive grouping preserves a semantic relation across standalone and integrated con-
structions; the expressive–depictive contrast records different contributions concealed by the broad
cover term. Those descriptive achievements establish which similarities an adequate account needs
to preserve. Further projections can build on them, with their warrant assessed against the evidence
for the particular expectation.
     An alternative can be a broader class, narrower subtypes, a continuous dimension, or an
item-by-item account. In the worked semantic comparison, separate classes and a superordinate class
with explicit subtypes both preserve the decisive distinction. For an inquiry into recipient responses,
action-specific groupings may be more informative than their union. The number and rank of cat-
egories are outcomes of comparison, rather than requirements supplied by a field or inquiry.

3.2 From proposal to category claim
An admission rule fixes which cases enter a class. A category claim makes a further commitment:
membership supports informative expectations beyond the evidence used to assign it. Assembling a
grouping and warranting those expectations can succeed or fail separately. Here, category standing
names the latter achievement; it isn’t a verdict on whether a descriptive class is scientifically useful.
     Three constructed comparisons distinguish the reach of an expectation. After identifying the
expressive contribution in The damn car won’t start, an investigator could examine another speaker’s
utterance of that sentence, the question Has the damn car broken down?, or The bloody car won’t start.
These comparisons change the realization, the environment, and the expression respectively.
     The question supplies a concrete target: does the negative stance remain conveyed while the car’s
breakdown is put in question? Evidence from declaratives needn’t settle that. The investigator can
test the familiar property in a new environment without inventing a differently named property. Gen-
eralizing to independently identified bloody makes a further commitment across sign-types. Neither
move is licensed simply by defining the class to contain the desired cases.
     An additional shared property isn’t sufficient merely because it’s additional. If all members and
nearly every sign outside a proposed grouping exhibit the target repetition behavior, membership
supplies little additional guidance about it. An imperfect probabilistic association can be informative
when it changes a warranted expectation at the stated scope. The alternatives in the preceding section
help assess that contribution.
     This comparison concerns what membership licenses an investigator to expect. Whether a cat-
egory representation improves a particular model is a separate question, taken up below. Projective
warrant doesn’t require that representation to outperform every account of the same evidence. Both
claims remain limited to the profile and scope supported: support for a subtype doesn’t automatically
extend to its superordinate class.
     For the grammatical core in Section 2.1, the reach includes both use across constructions and
associations with meaning and realization. The grammatical descriptions warrant the distributional
connection; descriptions of meaning and prosody support the wider profile. A lexical category earns

grammatical standing through grammatical generalizations, but the information available from mem-
bership can extend well beyond them. Functional groupings have comparable reach: supplementary
status supports an expectation about prosodic detachment across expression categories.
     The source evidence matters for each inference. An assignment established from distribution can
support an expectation about an unexamined intonation boundary. If that boundary helped identify
the case, predicting it adds nothing; characteristic packaging can instead be assessed in another real-
ization. This distinction concerns what was observed and what’s projected, rather than which field
owns either property.
     I also adopt the action-specific categories for the sequential relations documented in the cited
descriptions. The same evidential policy applies across inquiries: established descriptions can warrant
scoped semantic and interactional projections without a new experiment in this paper.
     The semantic comparisons trace expressive commonality across grammatical distinctions and dis-
tinguish stance from depiction. Potts’s account also supplies expectations about the behavior of ex-
pressives, including their intensification under repetition. Those expectations remain available for
integrated uses such as prenominal damn. The grammatical and expressive classifications each reach
beyond a description of their membership, while differing in which uses and properties they connect.
3.3 Projection and identity
Revising the analysis of a conventional use can improve subsequent predictions without turning an
earlier failure into a success. To keep those judgments separate, a projective claim states what’s been
observed, what’s expected, and over which bearers and conditions. Its warrant is the evidence sup-
porting that expectation (Goodman, 1955). Claim content, evidential status, and proposed causal
ground can then be assessed separately (Reynolds, 2026, pp. 11–12).
     Suppose source contexts identify an expressive use and the target is its interpretation under re-
petition. Repetition can’t also supply the case-level evidence used to admit that use. Similarly, if
resistance to determiner combination is the target, an unexpected determiner can’t by itself justify
excluding the case as conversion. A change to the identification rule creates a revised claim requiring
assessment.
     Imagine an investigator who’s identified expressive damn only in standalone reactions such as
Damn! We’ve missed the train. Their provisional identification uses the reaction meaning; it doesn’t
require standalone position. They predict that these uses resist grammatical integration. Encoun-
tering The damn car won’t start then presents an adverse case: the expression conveys stance while
functioning as an integrated modifier inside the noun phrase.
     This is a hypothetical research sequence using familiar constructions. The investigator should
check the structural analysis, but the confirmed prenominal occurrence counts against the restriction
under the original identification rule. Its inconvenient distribution isn’t by itself a reason to remove it
from that assessment. Later recognizing a distinct prenominal use can improve the analysis without
making the original prediction successful.
     Evaluate the original projection under the identification rule available when it was made. Later
evidence may warrant distinguishing a recurrent conventional use by its contribution or distribution.
That revision can improve subsequent predictions, but it doesn’t remove earlier counterexamples
from the original assessment. Observations that motivate a new distinction don’t also supply inde-
pendent confirmation of its predictive success.

         A quoted token or a productive noun denoting an utterance remains another possible analysis,
    but it needs evidence for quotation or nominal reference. A correction to a mistaken structural ana-
    lysis should be recorded as such. It differs from changing the identification rule so that a confirmed
    counterexample no longer falls within its scope.
         An informative restriction also needs relevant opportunities. Failure to observe inflection says
    little if the sampled environments wouldn’t elicit it under a rival analysis. Testing distribution re-

    vironments. Either prediction can start from evidence of membership in a category established for
    another inquiry. The common bearer connects those claims to the same use while preserving the

    3.4 Representation and causal explanation
    Suppose an inventory records that a use occurs standalone and conveys stance. Adding the label
    “standalone stance use” abbreviates those two observations; it adds no information to a fully observed
    record. The combined grouping could still be useful when investigating something not yet recorded,
    such as interpretation under repetition. Pooling observations from its members might help estimate
    that pattern from sparse records. Whether it does is a comparative question.
         The grouping selects an intersection: stance Damn! qualifies, while prenominal damn in the
    damn car fails the standalone condition. A model can represent that intersection as one label or keep
    its two component assignments. Their relative value depends on the information available and the
    target being predicted. A benefit for estimating repetition behavior wouldn’t establish a unique cause
    of the shared properties or priority for every inquiry.
         Explanation asks another question. Once the present form of gee has been recorded, knowing
    its religious source adds nothing to that already observed spelling. Its history can still explain how
    the form arose. More generally, conditioning a prediction on consequences of a historical process
    can remove the association through which that process is recognized. Section 5 distinguishes this
    predictive comparison from assessment of the proposed history itself.
         Hill (1965, pp. 295–299) offered viewpoints for comparing causal interpretations with altern-
    atives, including temporal order and consistency with independently supported knowledge. These
    concern specified causes and outcomes. In the history of gee, for example, the sequence of semantic
    change and clipping helps distinguish the processes that contributed to the present form (Section 5).

4   Phonological organization and situated realization
    Grammatical interjectionhood is informative about sound as well as distribution. Its characteristic
    profile includes separate intonational packaging and an openness to sounds or combinations unusual
    elsewhere in a variety. A phonological inquiry can pursue those associations and also group signs
    across grammatical categories. Even where the grammatical analysis of depictive Bang! remains un-
    settled, its phonological realization is available for comparison.
         These observations motivate different classifications. The first can group sign-types by their
    phonological possibilities. The second groups realizations by how speakers produce them in con-
    text. Their relation connects the resources available for a conventional sign with the ways speakers
    realize it on particular occasions.

4.1 A phonological stratum of signs
English conversational clicks make the first possibility concrete. The click represented by tut-tut dif-
fers from an ordinary spelling pronunciation (Wilkins, 1992, p. 147). Forms such as ugh permit
sounds outside the usual phoneme inventory of standard British and American English (Quirk et al.,
1985, pp. 74, 853). A phonological inquiry can ask which conventional signs admit such resources,
how their variants are organized, and how speakers acquire or adapt them.
     These possibilities contribute to the interjection profile. Having identified a grammatical inter-
jection, an investigator has reason to consider a wider range of conventional sound patterns than
the ordinary word phonology would allow (Ameka, 1992, pp. 105–106). Some members use that
range and others have ordinary phonology. Membership guides expectations without predicting an
anomalous segment in every item.
     Ameka (1992, pp. 112–113) diagnoses a different inference: unusual phonology shared with in-
terjections has been used to include onomatopoeic words in the grammatical interjection category.
His alternative recognizes a phonological grouping whose members can belong to different grammat-
ical categories. The forward association and this objection are compatible: grammatical membership
can inform phonological expectations without unusual phonology being sufficient for grammatical
membership.
     The grammatical claim concerns membership in a lexical category. The evidence offered concerns
sounds or sound patterns unusual elsewhere in the language. That evidence supports a comparison
of phonological possibilities across independently identified signs. By itself, it doesn’t settle whether
a particular use has nominal, verbal, or interjectional grammar. Nor does sound depiction itself entail
phonological anomaly: resemblance and distribution within the sound system are different relations.
     The reverse inference can also have value as defeasible evidence. Its strength depends on how the
observed phonological property is distributed among competing grammatical analyses. Evidence that
interjections permit unusual sounds doesn’t by itself settle how diagnostic an unusual sound is of in-
terjectionhood. That question concerns the association and its alternatives, rather than a prohibition
on inference across domains.
     For English Bang!, the competing grammatical analysis is a standalone nominal fragment, sup-
ported as a possibility by the independently available noun in a loud bang. A depictive interpretation
leaves that analysis open. Even if an occurrence had unusual phonation, the additional observation
would concern its realization. Choosing between nominal and interjectional analyses needs distribu-
tional and constructional evidence about the selected conventional use, or an independently warran-
ted association connecting the observed phonology to that grammar.
     The strongest alternative retains one broad descriptive class and records phonology and gram-
mar as separate attributes. For organizing known cases, that can preserve the same information. It
becomes inadequate only if membership in the broad class is used to infer a grammatical assignment
without the relevant evidence. The assessment rejects that sufficient test while retaining both the
wider interjection profile and the cross-grammatical phonological comparison. Establishing one pro-
jectible phonological stratum would require its own family of supported expectations.
     An account of the stratum would gain projective reach if membership organized acquisition, ad-
aptation, or alternation beyond the diagnostic used to identify it. Consider the contrast between
producing a click and pronouncing tut-tut as spelled words. An investigation could first group signs
by independently recorded articulations, then ask which permit a spelling pronunciation and in what
4   Phonological organization and situated realization                                                16


settings. That prospective comparison would test a relation beyond the articulation used for group-
ing; the reported alternatives don’t yet establish it.
     Regular phonology matters too. In a ten-language study of open repair interjections, Dinge-
manse et al. (2013) find broad phonetic similarities alongside language-specific vowel targets and in-
tonation. The repair interjections in Icelandic and Cha’palaa have falling contours, consistent with
local interrogative prosody. The study concerns comparable repair environments. It supplies no
reason to assign every occurrence resembling huh to one phonologically exceptional class.
     Phonological classification thus has work to do within ordinary linguistic organization as well
as at its margins. An inquiry into repair intonation could group forms with interrogative resources;
another into unusual segments could group conversational clicks with other marked forms. Both
could be useful. A single label such as phonological interjection would obscure the difference
unless the inquiry and grouping basis were specified.
4.2 Prosodic classes of realizations
A prosodic boundary is a property of a realization, and a sign-type’s characteristic packaging sup-
ports expectations about its realizations. For an independently identified interjection, separate inton-
ational phrasing is a reasonable default (Ameka, 1992, p. 108), with variation across environments.
Realizations of grammatically different signs can share a boundary pattern. Supplements provide a
familiar example: interjections, noun phrases, and relative clauses can all be prosodically detached
(Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, pp. 66, 1350).
    Here functional classification connects category with realization (Section 2.2). An interjection’s
distribution includes supplementary use; supplementary status in turn supports a prosodic expecta-
tion shared with other expression categories. Once the function is known, lexical category needn’t
add a further prediction about that same boundary to remain useful for other properties.
    Take a recording of Damn, we’re going to be late!. The context can identify the reaction use while
the acoustic record supplies evidence about a boundary, such as a pause or pitch movement. A second
recording can realize the same use with different timing. The category supplies an expectation be-
fore each boundary is measured; the recordings show whether and how it’s realized. Estimating the
strength of that tendency requires productions across relevant environments.
    Interaction supplies some of those environments. Ogden (2013) describes English clicks and per-
cussives in stance-taking, sequence management, word searches, and turn timing, with visual beha-
viour contributing to their interpretation. A classification of productions by timing and articulation
can intersect an analysis of sequential practices. The range of jobs also cautions against assigning one
conventional action or expressive meaning to every click.
    The comma in a transcript of Damn, we’re going to be late! is a different kind of observation. It
may record a transcriber’s analysis of detachment, but it isn’t itself a pause or a grammatical relation.
Entering that comma once as a prosodic boundary and again as syntactic independence would manu-
facture apparent agreement between the measures. An acoustic analysis and a constructional analysis
can converge without relying on that single transcription decision.
    The two phonological inquiries extend the family in different ways. Phonological strata can share
their sign-type bearers and enter membership intersections. Prosodic classes of occurrences connect
to them through realization and through distributions over contexts. A sign-type table can record a
propensity for separate intonational packaging. Keeping that propensity distinct from each realized
boundary lets the table support a prediction whose outcome can be checked.

5   Historical ancestry and classes of change
    The history of gee explains a feature of its present form that a synchronic interjection label leaves
    unexplained. Signs with different current distributions can descend from related sources, while signs
    with similar current profiles can arrive by different routes.
        A historical inquiry can classify present signs by independently established ancestry. It can also
    classify changes, such as clipping or reanalysis. The first has sign-types as bearers; the second has
    changes linking sources and outcomes.
    5.1 The documented route to gee
    Gehweiler (2008) traces gee through an invocation of Jesus, an exclamatory use of the religious name,
    and a clipped form. In the invocation, the speaker addresses the deity. The exclamation can express a
    reaction without that address. Clipping then produces a form whose relation to the name can become
    opaque. The sequence separates semantic change from the later change of form.
         The transitions also have different proposed explanations. Gehweiler relates the first to gradual
    invited inference, aided by the syntactically peripheral invocation. She analyzes the clipping as an ab-
    rupt, taboo-motivated change involving hearer reanalysis (Gehweiler, 2008, pp. 84–88). The source
    invocation already permits peripheral packaging.
         The history explains why the shape of gee bears a relation to religious invocation even for a speaker
    who doesn’t recognize that relation. Its present opacity alone wouldn’t identify its ancestry. The
    documented sequence supplies the additional evidence for a restricted instance of path dependence:
    a particular route helps explain a feature of its outcome, without making every other outcome of that
    route predictable.
         The explanation concerns a particular outcome. It doesn’t yet establish that a class of signs with re-
    ligious ancestry outperforms alternatives or supports a common projection. That comparison would
    require independently documented histories and outcomes across more than this one route.
         The distinction also corrects an ambiguity in primary and secondary interjections. Ameka’s
    distinction concerns whether a form has other, non-interjectional uses in the language (Ameka, 1992,
    pp. 105–106). It isn’t a partition into historically underived and historically derived signs. Geh-
    weiler’s primary gee has a documented derived history. Conversely, an unknown etymology supplies
    a gap in knowledge, not a common origin shared by all forms whose sources remain unknown.
    5.2 Source, mechanism, transmission, and trajectory
    A useful historical description separates four relations. The source identifies an earlier sign or con-
    struction. The mechanism specifies a change, such as clipping or reanalysis. The transmission
    setting concerns how the innovation spreads, including borrowing where supported. The sub-
    sequent trajectory concerns further changes in use or form. Signs can share one relation without
    sharing the other three.
         For gee, a religious invocation supplies the source and tabooistic clipping supplies one mechanism.
    Those facts don’t establish the transmission history of every later community’s use. Nor does a shared
    source force a common trajectory: an exclamatory religious name can remain phonologically close
    to its source without later undergoing clipping. Historical classes can be organized at several levels,
    according to which relation the inquiry needs.
         The proposed verbal route to complement-taking damn illustrates unequal support among his-
    tories. God damn him has the shape of an optative, a construction independently recognized in Eng-
    6   Broader classes and further inquiries                                                            18


    lish (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 944). Omission of the subject and increasing conventional
    expressive force offer a plausible route to damn him. The surviving complement fits that reconstruc-
    tion. These synchronic comparisons don’t establish the sequence as independently as Gehweiler’s
    historical record does.
         The source optative already uses plain-form damn, so the reconstruction doesn’t require loss of
    tense marking at that transition. And the present complement remains compatible with competing
    analyses of the head as verbal or interjectional. A classification by independently established verbal
    ancestry could cross that present grammatical boundary. Inferring the ancestry solely from the com-
    plement and then invoking the ancestry to predict that same complement would be circular.
         Classes of changes require a different representation. An instance of clipping relates an earlier
    form to an outcome; an instance of semantic reanalysis relates earlier and later interpretations.
    Neither is itself a sign-type alongside gee and damn. A process classification can support compar-
    isons among developments, while explicit source–result relations connect those developments to
    classifications of their inputs and outputs.
    5.3 What historical evidence can establish
    Historical explanation and forward projection need different comparisons. A documented route may
    explain a present feature through the changes that produced it. A forward projection asks what in-
    formation about the route warrants expecting in another case. For a verbal-source class, that target
    could concern surviving restrictions on dependents, provided those restrictions weren’t used to as-
    sign the source. The present examples motivate that inquiry without establishing a category-wide
    result.
         Evidence for replication also needs to distinguish innovations from their descendants. Suppose
    one community coins a clipped exclamation and three others borrow it. Four community records
    then document one origin, not four independent instances of clipping. They can support an account
    of transmission or subsequent divergence. Testing whether the same source repeatedly gives rise to
    the same outcome would require independently established innovations. This hypothetical count
    makes the sampling unit depend on the historical claim.
         An ancestry classification can still explain a present contrast even when ancestry adds no predict-
    ive information after all its consequences are observed. Conditioning on those consequences changes
    the question to whether ancestry contributes anything further. That can be a useful test of predictive
    economy; it isn’t a general test of whether the documented process occurred or helped produce the
    contrast.
         Historical classes can have two kinds of payoff. They can organize independently supported
    source relations among present signs, and they can compare processes across developments. These are
    legitimate descriptive and explanatory contributions. An empirical ancestry category, in the stronger
    usage adopted here, also needs support for the inductions it licenses. The documented history of one
    sign can’t supply that support for an entire source class.

6   Broader classes and further inquiries
    The traditional interjection domain also intersects classes whose most informative members lie else-
    where. Social evaluation can group forms by their appropriateness for audiences and settings. Pro-
    cessing inquiries can group familiar expressions by how they’re produced or understood. An inter-
    jection’s membership in either class needn’t make the class interjection-specific. Three questions

remain distinct: whether the broader class is supported, how it intersects the traditional domain, and
whether an interjection label helps describe that intersection.
6.1 Social evaluation and recognized repertoires
Religious exclamations connect the historical case to a further classification. In Gehweiler’s account,
taboo pressure contributes to clipping Jesus into gee. A present-day inquiry can group uses by their
evaluated appropriateness for an audience or setting. It asks how speakers assess the available forms
in their own community.
     Jay and Janschewitz (2008) examine ratings of hypothetical swearing scenarios, finding that judg-
ments depend on the word, speaker–listener relations, and social and physical context. These ratings
support contextual differentiation in evaluations of taboo language.
     Consider a proposed comparison of Gee! and Jesus! as reactions to surprising news among close
friends and during a formal interview. Ratings would ask how appropriate each choice seems in each
setting. A difference in those ratings would distinguish social appropriateness without by itself chan-
ging the grammatical classification of the reaction.
     Social indexing poses a related but distinct inquiry. Agha’s account of enregisterment concerns
the social recognition of speech forms as indexing attributes of speakers, with a repertoire’s associ-
ations and social reach subject to change (Agha, 2005, pp. 38–40). A classification by participation
in a recognized repertoire could cross grammatical, semantic, and sequential divisions. The relevant
evidence concerns recognition and use of those associations, beyond a frequency difference between
geographical collections.
     For example, if listeners regularly associated Gee! with an old-fashioned speaker persona, their
judgments would support a social association. They wouldn’t merely be rating whether it was ac-
ceptable in the interview. Both investigations could concern the same surprise reaction, while asking
different questions about its social meaning. Such associations could also unite forms used for several
actions. Any claimed convention would need a specified community and evidence of shared recogni-
tion.
6.2 Formulaicity and processing
Compare a familiar greeting, How are you?, a pause filler, um, and the idiom kick the bucket. These
illustrative forms differ in grammar and conversational job. A processing inquiry could ask whether
speakers produce them as familiar routines. Its grouping would cross the earlier grammatical and
semantic divisions; formulaic expressions might name it more clearly than another kind of in-
terjection.
     Van Lancker Sidtis et al. (2015) report differences in formulaic language production and com-
prehension across clinical groups and healthy participants. Their production analysis combines six
kinds of formulaic material, including conversational formulae, pause fillers, sentence stems, and
idioms. The results give empirical consequences to a grouping that crosses grammatical categories:
pause fillers can be studied alongside phrases and idioms despite their grammatical differences.
     For the greeting, one question is whether How are you? is retrieved fluently because repeated use
has helped establish a routine. A comparison at the same measured frequency asks how fluency differs
among expressions with comparable recorded use. That adjustment doesn’t by itself identify how
much repetition contributed to the greeting’s fluency. A causal interpretation still needs assumptions
about exposure, retrieval, and how the frequency measure relates to speakers’ experience.

8   The general consequence
    Grammatical interjectionhood gives access to a wider profile: characteristic meanings and uses, a tend-
    ency toward separate intonational packaging, and possibilities for unusual phonology. These expect-
    ations extend the category’s value beyond the grammatical generalizations that establish its standing.
    A classification can be informative across several inquiries even when its members vary in the associ-
    ated properties.
         Within syntax, category and function preserve different generalizations. Supplement function
    connects an interjection with a noun phrase and a relative clause, supporting expectations about their
    packaging. Noun-phrase category connects supplementary Dr Brown with its subject use. The same
    syntactician needs both analyses. Some of the interjection’s wider expectations are shared through
    its characteristic functions, and recognizing that connection clarifies what each classification contrib-
    utes.
         That variation leaves other groupings useful. Conative sh shares the grammatical profile of ouch
    without requiring its affective contribution. Prenominal damn shares expressive properties with stan-
    dalone Damn! without becoming an intermediate grammatical interjection. The relevant relations
    are partly aligned: grammatical membership guides expectations about meaning, while expressive
    classification connects uses across its boundary. Their divergence preserves information that either
    grouping alone would lose.
         Depictive Bang! introduces a further semantic relation, and continuers and repair initiators or-
    ganize different sequential environments. These distinctions prevent pluralism from becoming a
    fixed arrangement with one category for grammar, another for semantics, and a third for interaction.
    The number and extension of the groupings depend on the questions and the relations supported by
    the evidence.
    8.1 Many-to-many relations and local failure
    Dupré’s pluralism and Khalidi’s crosscutting kinds make room for multiple defensible groupings.
    The linguistic cases add a practical requirement: specify which relation a proposed grouping pre-
    serves and which objects participate in it. Haspelmath’s division between descriptive categories and
    comparative concepts supplies a related lesson about analytical purpose. Here, the same care is needed
    within a language and within an inquiry.
        In The damn car won’t start, expressive contribution concerns the attitude conveyed; procedural
    encoding concerns how the wording guides its recognition. Both descriptions could hold. The con-
    trast with depictive Bang! concerns a different contribution, while the exclusive encoding hypotheses
    9   Conclusion                                                                                        22


    in Section 2.5 disagree about the same use. Assigning the hypotheses to different fields wouldn’t make
    their claims compatible.
         Failure has correspondingly local consequences. Suppose a repetition test found that an inde-
    pendently identified expressive use didn’t strengthen stance under the specified conditions. The pre-
    diction of intensification would fail there; the observed distinction between conveying stance and
    depicting a sound wouldn’t disappear with it. Restricting the prediction to another subtype would
    create a new claim. Its support couldn’t be inherited from the failed generalization.
         The classifications are mutually informative without being interchangeable. Knowing that a use
    is a grammatical interjection gives reason to expect parts of the wider profile. Knowing that it’s ex-
    pressive supports more specific expectations about stance, including in integrated uses outside the
    grammatical category. A phonological grouping can connect realizations that those classifications
    separate. Their value lies both in the associations that let one guide another and in the differences
    that keep one from replacing another.
    8.2 Overlap, mechanisms, and category standing
    Selecting standalone stance uses includes expressive Damn! and excludes prenominal damn in the
    damn car. That intersection is well-defined and can fix a useful comparison set. Its further value
    depends on the question: does restricting attention to those uses help with the proposed inference?
    Neither the overlap itself nor the availability of its component assignments answers that.
         Distinct categories needn’t be statistically independent, and they can share causal processes. A
    partial association can make membership in one informative about properties studied through an-
    other. Such associations are part of what makes the classifications useful. Strong correlation still
    leaves distinct questions and extensions; a shared history can help explain their alignment without
    establishing every present property.
         For Bang!, uncertainty between a nominal-fragment and an interjection analysis doesn’t itself
    establish graded membership. The analysis may simply be unresolved. A separate inquiry could ask
    how typical speakers judge it compared with Damn!; prototype accounts organize such typicality
    and similarity (Rosch, 1975; Taylor, 2003). Either a graded or a categorical representation can serve
    a task, but its form alone doesn’t establish what it licenses an investigator to infer.
         A field supplies no fixed quota of categories, and an inquiry needn’t end with just one. A group-
    ing may serve several questions across fields, but warrant only specified projections under specified
    conditions. The historical label interjection identifies a meeting point for these inquiries. The
    evidence, rather than the label or departmental boundary, determines which proposed categories de-
    serve recognition.

9   Conclusion
    English interjections support several classifications because the relevant properties recur in partially
    aligned patterns. Within syntax, lexical and phrasal categories crosscut functions: an interjection, a
    noun phrase, and a relative clause can all serve as supplements, with shared expectations about pros-
    odic detachment. A syntactician’s interest in those relations is as grammatical as an interest in lexical
    membership.
        A grammatical core including ouch, oops, hey, and sh supports expectations about independent
    utterance and clausal supplementation, and also about characteristic contributions and realization.
9   Conclusion                                                                                         23


Identifying a grammatical interjection gives reason to expect a reactive, expressive, or conative use, of-
ten with its own intonational packaging and with possibilities for unusual phonology. Grammatical
standing doesn’t confine the category’s inferential reach to grammar.
     Crosscutting classifications needn’t be rival approximations to a single complete classification.
They can be mutually informative while preserving different relations. Expressive classification con-
nects standalone and prenominal damn across their grammatical difference; conative sh shares the
grammatical core without requiring evaluative stance. The distinction between stance and depiction
adds another useful semantic comparison. An apparent anomaly can thus reveal the limits of an as-
sociation without undermining either category.
     The same approach distinguishes coexistence from competition. Expressive commonality leaves
the rival grammatical analyses of complement-taking damn unresolved; it doesn’t reconcile them.
The relevant question is what each assignment warrants expecting about the identified use, and where
another classification improves or changes that expectation. Inquiry-relative categories organize over-
lapping routes of inference through linguistic expressions, their constructional relations, and their
realizations. Their reach depends on the supported associations, rather than on the disciplinary ori-
gin of the classification.
