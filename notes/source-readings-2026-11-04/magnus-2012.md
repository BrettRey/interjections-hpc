**No responsibility notice is needed.** The paper doesn't present inquiry-relativity as its own discovery; it credits Dupré and Khalidi as precedents. But Magnus names this thesis "enquiry relativity" and defends it at length. With a title like "Inquiry-Relative Categories", leaving him out is a gap a philosophy-of-science reader will notice. It should be fixed.

**1. Magnus's account**

- **The definition** (ch. 2 §A): "A category k is a natural kind for domain d if (1) k is part of a taxonomy that allows the scientific enquiry into d to achieve inductive and explanatory success, and (2) any alternative taxonomy that excluded k would not do so."
  - He calls (1) the success clause and (2) the restriction clause.
  - The restriction clause stops irrelevant kinds being tacked onto a successful science. It also excludes domains where "success is too easy."
- **Degrees of naturalness** (ch. 2 §B): "A category k is natural for domain d insofar as k has to be recognized in order to achieve inductive and explanatory success providing an account of d." He also gives an all-or-nothing version for settled science (ch. 2 §D) and endorses both.
- **Relativity** (ch. 1 §B.11): "Say rather that natural kinds are natural only relative to a specified domain of enquiry." Also: "Naturalness is a two-place relation of the form 'k is a natural kind for d'." He quotes Boyd as the source of the idea: "that 'natural' kinds are relative to disciplines, inductive task or contexts of enquiry" (Boyd 1982, 642).
- **What a domain is** (ch. 1 §B.11):
  - "I have not given a precise analysis of what a domain of enquiry is." Existing disciplines serve as his examples.
  - He separates two senses. A domain can be the phenomena an enquiry addresses. Or it "could depend on the structure of a practicing scientific discipline."
  - He provisionally takes the first: "a range of phenomena, things, and features of things". He admits "I do not have a compelling argument for construing domains of enquiry one way rather than the other." He repeats this as his first difference from Boyd (ch. 4 §A.1).
  - "I do not put any constraint on what can count as a domain of enquiry" (ch. 5 §C.2). But "bloated super-domains could not be the domain of any actual scientific enquiry" (ch. 2 §B.2).
- **Induction:**
  - On its own, the inductive criterion suffers from "similarity fetishism". It also "allows naturalness to become a slippery, unmanageable matter of indefinitely many degrees" (ch. 1 §B.1).
  - His first remedy is that inductive inference always needs knowledge specific to a domain: "There is no general canon of inductive inference" (ch. 1 §B.11).
  - His second is that a domain limits which predicates count. So "the focus provided by a domain of enquiry might settle matters of degree which could not be settled by considerations of induction alone" (ch. 2 §C, Fig. 2.1).
- **Crosscutting kinds:**
  - He holds that the assumption that natural kinds nest in a hierarchy "turns out to be false" (ch. 1 §B.9). From one female jaguar we might generalize about "female mammals, predators, or any number of other kinds which do not neatly fit inside one another."
  - Replying to Machery on *life*: "there would not be a single natural kind life, but instead several related kinds which figure in the work of different disciplines" (ch. 2 §B.2).
  - "Different objects, different domains, different natural kinds" (ch. 2 §D.2).
- **Homeostatic property clusters (HPC)** (ch. 6):
  - Natural kinds aren't defined as HPCs; electrons are a natural kind but not an HPC. Still, "Many natural kinds are HPCs, and we can understand them better by recognizing this."
  - He loosens the cluster idea: it "will be whatever complex array of property regularities are sustained by the underlying causal process" (§A.3).
  - The kind's boundary is set causally: "We identify natural kinds by the scope of those mechanisms" (§A.2).
- **Kinds open to choice** (his "fungible kinds", ch. 2 §D.2): a kind fails the restriction clause when the people describing a domain are also shaping the things in it. An established convention studied from outside does pass. In his example of an island tradition for splitting cake, the halves "are natural kinds" for the anthropologist.
- **Animal signals** (ch. 5 §C.1): meerkat alarm calls "function both referentially and motivationally", and they count as natural kinds for meerkat ecology.

**2. Bearing on the paper**

Where he anticipates it:
- **Same expressions, different features.** His example is "psychology and physiology are both concerned with people, but their domains include different features of people". That is the paper's situation: lexical grammar, the semantics of stance, and interaction attend to different features of the same expressions. In his first sense of "domain", all three count as domains.
- **One label, several categories.** His reply on *life* parallels the paper's treatment of "interjection" as the meeting point of several inquiries.
- **Two categories in one field.** His discussion of distributed cognition ("recognizing one as a natural kind should not lock out the other", ch. 3 §C.2) parallels "The same syntactician needs both analyses."

Where he differs:
- **(a) How inquiries are picked out.** The paper says "An inquiry identifies a question and a standard of success". That is Magnus's second sense of "domain", the one he sets aside, and it is closer to Boyd's "disciplinary matrix". The nearest precedent for the wording is Boyd as Magnus quotes him; Magnus is the nearest fully developed account.
- **(b) What success requires.** Magnus requires both inductive and explanatory success, and lets explanation secure a kind where induction can't (the anglerfish, ch. 6 §A.3). The paper keeps descriptive, projective and causal success apart: "Neither descriptive usefulness nor a successful projection supplies its own causal explanation." Its "category standing" is a weaker claim than his "natural kind".
- **(c) Indispensability.** The paper's question "what alternative would serve the same inquiry?" has the same shape as his restriction clause, but it doesn't require the grouping to be indispensable. In the paper, separate classes and a superordinate class "both preserve the decisive distinction". On Magnus's clause, the subtypes would count as natural kinds and the superordinate class wouldn't.
- **(d) What gets assessed.** His kinds are features of the world: "If it is a natural kind now, I say, then it was all along" (ch. 4 §A.1). The paper assesses claims "under the identification rule in force when it was made". These are different levels, not a contradiction.

Recommendation: cite Magnus as the precedent for inquiry-relativity, and say plainly where the paper differs on (a) to (c). Log this in `DECISIONS.md` as "deliberately distinguished".

Two possible sentences (page numbers still to be checked):

- For the introduction, after Khalidi: "\textcite{magnus2012scientific} states the relativity explicitly: a category is a natural kind for a domain of enquiry when enquiry into that domain succeeds inductively and explanatorily with a taxonomy that includes it, and would fail without one."
- For §7.1: "The categories here are relative to inquiries picked out by their questions rather than to domains of phenomena, and their standing is assessed one projection at a time, so a grouping can be descriptively useful or projectively warranted without being indispensable in Magnus's sense."

**3. Against Brett's commitments**

- **Categories as what fallible diagnostics converge on: mostly compatible.** He rejects sharp boundaries (ch. 1 §B.6) and identifies a cluster by starting from observed properties. But he draws the kind's boundary by the reach of its causal mechanism, not by where diagnostics converge. That would let shared history pull prenominal *damn* into a grouping its diagnostics exclude. The paper's separation of history from category assignment answers this.
- **No single property decides: compatible.** See ch. 1 §B.8, and ch. 6 §A.3, where the cluster isn't "a single list of properties which are necessary and sufficient for membership".
- **Absences count: not addressed.** He discusses kinds with no current members (Darmstadtium), not negative diagnostics. Nothing in the book conflicts with it.
- **Each category anchored in one kind of property: the main tension.** In Fig. 2.1, only predicates describing phenomena inside the domain count toward a kind's naturalness. That would discount the very projections into prosody and meaning that the anchoring view puts in the lexical category's cluster. The paper's closing claim, that a category's reach "depends on the supported associations, rather than on the disciplinary origin of the classification", goes beyond Magnus. His requirement that neighbouring domains fit together (ch. 2 §B.2) is a consistency constraint, not projection across domains. The anchoring idea is Brett's own contribution.
- **"Homeostatic": looser than Brett's standard.** Magnus accepts it as a metaphor ("homeostasis has often been used metaphorically in this broader way", ch. 6 §A.3). That is looser than the portfolio's requirement of corrective-control evidence. The paper avoids HPC vocabulary, so this matters only if the vocabulary comes back.
- **Feedback from grammars: a likely reviewer question.** A reviewer could ask whether grammar books' own classifications feed back into usage, which by his test would make the categories a matter of choice. His cake example supports treating conventional sign-types as natural kinds. The paper's line "The bearers of that history are classifications in grammars, not signs" is the reply.
