## Brett's prompt

I understand that the question is underdetermined, but I want a list of the properties of the following items: 
the bastard │ supplementary epithet: He's taken my chair again, the bastard
you bastard │ exclamatory NP: You bastard!
bloody      │ degree modifier: It's bloody marvellous
damn        │ degree modifier: They drove damn fast
fucking     │ attributive-only expletive: a fucking investigation
-bloody-    │ infix (tmesis): abso-bloody-lutely
the hell    │ emotive modifier after an interrogative: What the hell is she trying to do?

The properties should be of interest to linguistics of a wide range of different subfields, though no property on its own need be of interest to multiple subfields or apply to multiple items.

You can ask for clarification, but the question is intentionally vague.

## Reply

I’m treating each item as a form in the specified use, rather than transferring properties from every use of its component words. The inventories are deliberately uneven. Uncited example contrasts below are my diagnostic judgments, not corpus findings; I’ve marked some properties whose values would need to be established empirically.

## 1. *The bastard*: supplementary epithet
*He’s taken my chair again, the bastard.*

- Nominal constituency. It’s a phrase containing the definite article and a singular count noun, not an internally unanalyzable exclamation.

- Internal expandability. The epithet permits modification: *the selfish bastard*, *the lucky bastard*, *the absolute bastard*. These aren’t necessarily equivalent evaluations.

- Number variation. The construction has a plural counterpart: *They’ve taken our chairs again, the bastards*. Its expressive function doesn’t eliminate ordinary nominal number morphology.

- External syntactic detachment. On the stipulated supplementary reading, it isn’t an argument or integrated modifier of *taken*. Removing it leaves the host clause grammatically complete.

- Anaphoric anchoring. In this example, its referent is the person already referred to by *he*. The NP doesn’t introduce a second participant in the chair-taking event.

- Given referent, additional evaluation. Its contribution needn’t help identify who took the chair: the reference can already be settled. It supplies a characterization of someone already under discussion.

- Person-directed evaluation. The epithet characterizes its referent, rather than merely marking dissatisfaction with the situation. This contrasts with expressive adjectives whose evaluative target can extend beyond their syntactic host. Lo Guercio and Orlando explicitly distinguish epithets from expressive adjectives on this dimension. [journals.linguisticsociety.org](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/SALT/article/view/32.003)

- Independence from the host’s polarity. Compare *He hasn’t taken my chair, the bastard*. Negating the chair-taking doesn’t negate the derogatory characterization. But this example alone doesn’t establish a lexical projection property of *bastard*: supplementary placement itself puts the epithet outside the host negation. [Semantics Archive](https://semanticsarchive.net/Archive/TJhZDgwZ/potts-expressives06.pdf)

- Perspective dependence. The unmarked interpretation attributes the evaluation to the current speaker. That shouldn’t become an exceptionless property of the expression: experimental and corpus work establishes systematic non-speaker-oriented interpretations of expressives and appositives in suitable contexts. [Springer Nature Link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10988-010-9070-5)

- Separation from the parentage sense. This use doesn’t entail anything about the marital status of the referent’s parents. The generalized abusive sense coexists with the historically associated parentage sense; their relationship is a lexical-semantic and historical property, not a truth-conditional commitment of this token. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bastard)

- Evaluative rather than merely identifying repetition. Although *he* and *the bastard* refer to the same person, the second expression isn’t redundant in its discourse contribution.

- Post-completion placement. The evaluation arrives after a grammatically complete clause. That makes it available as an evaluative continuation; whether a particular production is planned as one turn-constructional unit or produced as an increment requires sequential and acoustic evidence.

- Prosodic separability. Supplementary interpretation permits separate prosodic packaging. The comma doesn’t establish a silent pause, a particular boundary tone, low volume, or a muttered delivery.

- Distinct targets for disagreement. A hearer can reject the event report—*No, he hasn’t*—or resist the characterization—*Don’t call him that*. These are different responses, even though both can oppose the speaker.

- Computational reference requirement. An adequate representation of this example should link *the bastard* to *he* while retaining the additional evaluation. Treating the epithet as a newly introduced person would misrepresent the example.

## 2. *You bastard*: exclamatory NP
*You bastard!*

- Independent utterancehood. The NP can constitute an entire utterance without an accompanying clause.

- Absence of overt clausal predication. There’s no finite verb, copula, tense marking, or overt subject–predicate clause structure. Its communicative completeness doesn’t by itself establish syntactic ellipsis.

- Construction-specific internal combination. It combines second-person *you* with a singular count noun without an intervening article. Zwicky treats such exclamatory NPs separately from both ordinary referential *you*–nominal combinations and vocatives accompanying clauses. [Stanford University](https://web.stanford.edu/~zwicky/hey-whatsyourname.pdf)

- Addressee orientation. In the specified use, *you* identifies the target through their role in the speech situation. This differs from the anaphoric, third-person anchoring of the preceding example.

- Number marking on the noun. *You bastards!* marks plural targets overtly even though *you* itself doesn’t distinguish singular from plural.

- Internal productivity. The pattern permits *you selfish bastard*, *you lucky bastard*, and *you poor bastard*. These modifications can substantially change the stance conveyed.

- Exclamatory force without exclamative-clause syntax. It can perform an exclamation without having the clause structure of *What a bastard you are!* Treating those as equivalent in force wouldn’t make them structurally equivalent. [Stanford University](https://web.stanford.edu/~zwicky/hey-whatsyourname.pdf)

- A related vocative use. The same sequence can occur in *Come back, you bastard!* That establishes a constructional relationship, not that every standalone occurrence is syntactically an adjunct to an unspoken imperative. [Stanford University](https://web.stanford.edu/~zwicky/hey-whatsyourname.pdf)

- Capacity to perform an interpersonal act. In hostile use, producing it can itself constitute an insult; it isn’t merely a report that the speaker feels angry. Its conventional association with name-calling contributes to its potential offensiveness. [Lancaster University](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/projects/impoliteness/strategy.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

- Context-sensitive interpersonal valence. Abusive wording doesn’t guarantee a hostile interaction. Taboo address can participate in affiliation, affection, teasing, or competitive banter as well as antagonism. Those functions need to be identified from the interaction, not assigned from the swearword alone. [John Benjamins Publishing Catalog](https://benjamins.com/online/prag/articles/prag.19028.pal)

- Conventional but non-obligatory *you*. In Palacios Martínez’s London teenage data, 37 of 52 taboo-vocative occurrences of *bastard* were preceded by *you*. That’s evidence about the broader address construction, not an estimate of the frequency of standalone *You bastard!* [John Benjamins Publishing Catalog](https://benjamins.com/online/prag/articles/prag.19028.pal)

- Potential prosodic contrast. Prominence on *YOU* rather than *BASTARD* can foreground the target rather than the characterization. Pitch range, voice quality, duration, and accompanying laughter are additional dimensions to measure, not recoverable properties of the written example.

- Sequential flexibility. It could respond to a revelation, an injury, a joke, or an unexpected success. Those environments would support different action analyses; the written form alone doesn’t select one.

- No guaranteed uptake. Being recognizable as an insult doesn’t entail causing offence, and intending banter doesn’t guarantee affiliative uptake. Production, interpretation, and effect are separate properties.

## 3. *Bloody*: degree modifier
*It’s bloody marvellous.*

- Adjective-directed modification. In this example, *bloody* modifies *marvellous*, rather than the subject or the copular clause as a whole.

- Integration into an adjective phrase. *Bloody marvellous* occupies the position available to the predicative adjective phrase. *Bloody* isn’t a detached supplement here.

- Degree-related contribution. It strengthens the positive assessment. With an already strongly evaluative adjective such as *marvellous*, the contribution may involve emphatic endorsement as well as movement on a degree scale; the example doesn’t establish a fixed numerical or ordinal increment.

- Expressive contribution alongside intensification. Degree strengthening and displayed affect shouldn’t automatically be collapsed into one property. Analyses of expressive intensification distinguish the degree-related contribution from the accompanying expressive act. [De Gruyter Brill](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/tl-2025-2007/html)

- Compatibility with opposite evaluative polarities. Compare *bloody marvellous* and *bloody awful*. The modifier doesn’t make the host property negative; dictionaries also attest positive combinations such as *a bloody good time*. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bloody)

- No blood-related entailment. Nothing in the example entails blood, bleeding, injury, or violence. That distinguishes this use from the descriptive adjective in *a bloody bandage*. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bloody)

- Morphological invariance in this use. The degree modifier doesn’t agree with the subject or vary with tense. The existence of descriptive *bloodier* and *bloodiest* doesn’t establish corresponding comparative forms of the intensifier.

- Category–form mismatch. It performs adverbial modification without *-ly*, while sharing its form with an adjective. That’s relevant to lexical categorization, conversion, and morphological transparency.

- Lexical prosody. In a common pronunciation, /ˈblʌdi/, it’s disyllabic with initial stress. That supplies a stressed–unstressed sequence whose rhythmic contribution differs from monosyllabic *damn*. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bloody)

- Host dependence in the specified use. An isolated *Bloody!* wouldn’t straightforwardly instantiate this adjective-modifying configuration. Any independent use or ellipsis would require a separate analysis.

- Regional and register association. The intensive use is conventionally associated particularly with British English and informal, potentially offensive expression. That’s a distributional association, not a restriction to British speakers or to hostile contexts. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/bloody)

- Historically uncertain development. The route into intensification is disputed. Reed offers evidence for possible Anglo-Norman influence; it would be premature to assign a settled origin in a particular religious oath. [White Rose Research Online](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/143789/3/Some%20evidence%20for%20bloody%20as%20an%20Anglo%20Norman%20intensifier%20-%20Emily%20Reed.pdf)

- Changing lexical competition. Comparisons of the spoken BNC corpora from 1994 and 2014 find changes in the distribution of swearing, including *FUCK* overtaking *BLOODY* at lemma level. That doesn’t establish the trajectory of this particular adjective-modifying construction, which would need separate coding. [Aston Research Explorer](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/swearing-in-informal-spoken-english-1990s-2010s/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## 4. *Damn*: degree modifier
*They drove damn fast.*

- Adverb-directed modification. Here the immediate host is adverbial *fast*, not an adjective. The resulting *damn fast* is an adverb phrase functioning within the VP.

- Local scalar interpretation. Its most direct degree-related contribution concerns speed, rather than frequency of driving, duration of the journey, or the number of drivers.

- Strengthening rather than merely ornamenting the assertion. *They drove fast* and *They drove damn fast* needn’t make identical claims about degree. Treating the modifier as wholly truth-conditionally inert would prejudge the analysis. Expressive intensifiers have been analyzed as contributing both intensification and expressive content. [De Gruyter Brill](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/tl-2025-2007/html)

- Separability of degree and attitude under negation. Consider *They didn’t drive damn fast—they barely moved*. On an available reading, the high-speed claim is rejected while the forceful or affective manner of presentation remains. The scope behavior needs to be assessed component by component.

- Compatibility with different scalar directions. *Damn slow* is also possible. The modifier strengthens the property supplied by its host; it doesn’t itself encode rapidity.

- No required condemnation. The example doesn’t perform the action expressed by the verb in *They damned the proposal*, nor does it condemn anyone to hell. The modifier and those verbal uses have different distributions and contributions. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/damn)

- No necessary negative attitude towards the event. In a racing context, *They drove damn fast!* can be admiring. The taboo form and the evaluative polarity of the whole utterance are separate dimensions.

- Contrast with detached *Damn!*. Compare *Damn, they drove fast*. That places an independently usable expressive before the clause rather than locally modifying *fast*. The forms overlap, but their integration and possible scopes differ.

- Availability inside a larger degree expression. *So damn fast* permits additional degree structure around the combination. That’s different from claiming that *damn* itself has ordinary comparative morphology.

- Monosyllabic phonological form. A common pronunciation is /dæm/. The written final *n* isn’t realized as an additional consonant in that form, making the spelling–pronunciation relationship another relevant property. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/damn)

- Morphological invariance. Changing *drove* to *drive*, *drives*, or *will drive* doesn’t require a corresponding change in the modifier.

- Historical connection to condemnation. The word is historically connected through Anglo-French to Latin *damnare*. The modifier’s history is related to, but not interchangeable with, the history of the verb; a date for the word’s earliest occurrence wouldn’t date *damn fast*. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/damn)

- A useful neutral-substitution contrast. Replacing it with *very* preserves much of the degree-related function while changing register and expressive force. The degree contribution needn’t be exactly equivalent for that contrast to be informative.

## 5. *Fucking*: attributive expletive
*A fucking investigation.*

- Prenominal integration. It occurs inside the NP, following the determiner and preceding the noun.

- Attributive restriction of this use. *The investigation is fucking* doesn’t provide the corresponding expressive predication. This restriction belongs to the specified use, not to every occurrence of the written form *fucking*. [LEL](https://lel.ed.ac.uk/~gpullum/slurs_and_obscenities.pdf)

- Formal identity with a verbal participle. Its *-ing* form resembles the gerund-participle of the sexual verb, but this occurrence doesn’t straightforwardly inherit that verb’s argument structure, progressive interpretation, or sexual meaning. [LEL](https://lel.ed.ac.uk/~gpullum/slurs_and_obscenities.pdf)

- No ordinary classification of investigations. Unlike *criminal* or *parliamentary*, it doesn’t normally identify a subtype of investigation. An investigation needn’t possess some independently identifiable property of being *fucking*.

- No ordinary property scale. The use doesn’t supply the kind of dimension that supports *a more thorough investigation*. Expressive force can vary without there being a corresponding scale of investigations’ *fuckingness*.

- Morphological invariance. *A fucking investigation* and *several fucking investigations* retain the same modifier form.

- Syntactic host–evaluative target mismatch. The noun is the structural host, but the attitude might concern the investigation, its authorization, the investigators, or the surrounding predicament. The distinction between local and extended evaluative targets is central to work on expressive adjectives. [journals.linguisticsociety.org](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/SALT/article/view/32.003)

- Context-sensitive target resolution. Experiments using expressive adjectives, including *fucking* among a mixed set, investigate how syntactic placement and perceived blameworthiness affect interpretation. That supports treating target selection as an empirical property, not simply reading it off the nearest noun. It doesn’t establish a specific interpretation for this isolated NP. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-and-cognition/article/culprithypothesis-how-blame-attribution-influences-the-interpretation-of-expressive-adjectives/5920D25088C87F053583C30966C891C7?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

- Compatibility with welcome developments. *Finally, a fucking investigation!* can express satisfaction that an investigation is happening. Hostility towards the investigation isn’t entailed by the modifier.

- Potential persistence through propositional operators. In *They haven’t authorized a fucking investigation*, the negative clause needn’t withdraw the expressive stance. Expressive modification and descriptive modification differ in how their contributions interact with such operators. [De Gruyter Brill](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/tl-2025-2007/html)

- Perspective attribution. The speaker is the default source of the stance, but reported discourse and narrative perspective can complicate that attribution. An annotation scheme should distinguish the grammatical speaker from the inferred evaluator. [Springer Nature Link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10988-010-9070-5)

- Distributed repeatability. Compare *The fucking committee ordered a fucking investigation*. Repeated expressives can contribute cumulative affect rather than merely repeat descriptive information. That doesn’t establish either the acceptability or the effect of adjacent repetition in *a fucking fucking investigation*. [Semantics Archive](https://semanticsarchive.net/Archive/TJhZDgwZ/potts-expressives06.pdf)

- Lexical polyfunctionality without identity of use. The same form also occurs as a degree modifier and in other expressive positions. Those relationships matter for lexical representation, but they don’t make this occurrence an adverb or an inserted element. [LEL](https://lel.ed.ac.uk/~gpullum/slurs_and_obscenities.pdf)

- A challenge for sentiment attribution. A system could correctly detect profanity while incorrectly inferring negative sentiment towards *investigation*. Profanity detection, sentiment polarity, and target identification are distinct tasks in this example.

## 6. *-Bloody-*: word-internal insertion
*Abso-bloody-lutely.*

- Word-internal placement. The expression interrupts the ordinary sequence of *absolutely*, rather than preceding or following the whole host.

- Discontinuous host realization. Removing the inserted material restores *absolutely*. The host’s material remains in its original order but is no longer contiguous.

- Insertion inside a larger morphological constituent. The insertion isn’t simply between *absolute* and *-ly*: it interrupts the material of *absolute*. It therefore can’t be described merely as an additional suffix at an ordinary affix boundary.

- Prosodically conditioned placement. The insertion site is constrained by rhythmic and stress structure, rather than being freely selectable. McCarthy’s classic analysis relates the pattern to metrical prosody; a simple rule referring only to the primary-stressed syllable would be an oversimplification. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language/article/abs/prosodic-structure-and-expletive-infixation/0B8A66DF84C096F4E69D4011B1D65DC0?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

- Retention of the inserted expression’s stress-bearing capacity. *Bloody* supplies its own stressed–unstressed sequence, rather than behaving like an unstressed segmental interruption. Its rhythm interacts with the host’s prosodic organization. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/expletive-insertion-a-morphological-approach/E43B268D954B120B950DAE9A96AD95AC)

- Preservation of the host’s broad grammatical distribution. *Abso-bloody-lutely* can occupy an adverbial position available to *absolutely*. The insertion doesn’t ordinarily turn it into a noun or a verb.

- Expressive rather than inflectional contribution. It doesn’t mark tense, number, person, or agreement, and it isn’t required by a syntactic dependency.

- Restricted substitutability. Ordinary degree modifiers aren’t freely interchangeable in this slot: *abso-very-lutely* isn’t the routine counterpart of *very good*. Creative wordplay could extend the pattern, but that’s different from unrestricted substitution.

- Dependence on the host in this occurrence. The inserted *bloody* isn’t an independent utterance here, even though the corresponding word exists outside the insertion construction.

- Disputed affixal status. Calling it an *infix* describes its position without settling whether it’s an affix. Zingler argues that the inserted expressions are words and analyzes the products as discontinuous compounds. The word-internal position and the affix-versus-word analysis should therefore be listed separately. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/expletive-insertion-a-morphological-approach/E43B268D954B120B950DAE9A96AD95AC)

- Constrained productivity. Recognizability and the possibility of novel formations don’t entail high token frequency or unrestricted productivity. Zingler reports a much more restricted corpus picture than some textbook descriptions suggest. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/expletive-insertion-a-morphological-approach/E43B268D954B120B950DAE9A96AD95AC)

- Orthographic segmentation independent of morphological analysis. The supplied hyphens make the interruption visible. Hyphenation, spacing, and solid spelling would affect tokenization without by themselves deciding the construction’s morphological status.

- Possible whole-expression utterancehood. *Abso-bloody-lutely!* can function as a complete emphatic response. That’s a property of the whole construction, not evidence that the inserted component independently carries the response.

- A construction-specific processing question. Recognition might involve retrieval of a familiar whole, decomposition into host plus insertion, or both. Acceptability of the familiar example doesn’t establish productive processing of a novel host.

- A distinct historical problem. The emergence of intensive *bloody* and the emergence of this insertion pattern are separate changes. Evidence dating the former doesn’t automatically date the latter.

## 7. *The hell*: emotive modifier after an interrogative
*What the hell is she trying to do?*

- Multiword internal form. It contains material resembling a definite NP, but the construction doesn’t require identifying a unique place or entity called *hell*.

- Tight association with the interrogative expression. In this use, *the hell* follows *what* rather than functioning as a freely positioned clause adjunct. Corpus and constructional analyses treat the resulting sequence as a tightly connected unit. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/aggressively-nondlinked-construction-and-ellipsis-a-direct-interpretation-approach/C92D413574503A1D36B8F849A1CA9C23)

- Compatibility with several interrogative hosts. Related combinations include *who the hell*, *where the hell*, *why the hell*, and *how the hell*. Their distribution can’t be reduced to modification of one ordinary lexical category.

- Constructional selectivity. The interrogative use of *who* in *Who the hell called?* differs from ordinary relative *who* in *the person who called*. Simply inserting *the hell* after every occurrence of a *wh*-form doesn’t preserve the original construction. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/aggressively-nondlinked-construction-and-ellipsis-a-direct-interpretation-approach/C92D413574503A1D36B8F849A1CA9C23)

- Restricted internal flexibility. It isn’t completely frozen: *what the bloody hell* is among the elaborated forms identified in corpus work. Nevertheless, the possibility of such modification doesn’t make arbitrary definite NPs interchangeable with it. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/aggressively-nondlinked-construction-and-ellipsis-a-direct-interpretation-approach/C92D413574503A1D36B8F849A1CA9C23)

- Preservation of the basic information variable. In the supplied example, the question still concerns what she’s trying to do. *The hell* doesn’t introduce another participant or an additional thing she’s trying to do.

- Non-neutral question presentation. It presents the issue as somehow problematic, baffling, objectionable, or difficult to resolve, rather than simply requesting information without a marked stance. The precise semantic characterization remains disputed. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jos/article/41/1/53/7610788)

- No necessary rhetorical interpretation. A speaker can genuinely require an answer. Expressive marking and information-seeking aren’t mutually exclusive. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jos/article/41/1/53/7610788)

- No necessary anger. Bafflement or incredulity can be central. An account that records only *negative emotion* would lose distinctions among the relevant attitudes. Ippolito’s analysis specifically develops the idea of conflict among the speaker’s beliefs and the possible answers. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jos/article/41/1/53/7610788)

- Non-equivalence to a simple degree booster. There needn’t be a scalar adjective or adverb to intensify. Its contribution concerns the question and its answer possibilities, not merely a higher degree of some lexical property.

- Availability in embedded questions. Compare *I wonder what the hell she’s trying to do*. Its expressive contribution doesn’t force it to occur only in an independent interrogative.

- Qualified polarity restrictions. Although influential analyses treat *wh-the-hell* expressions as polarity-sensitive, corpus research finds examples in affirmative, veridical environments with predicates such as *know* and *find out*. A blanket negative-polarity classification would miss those occurrences. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/aggressively-nondlinked-construction-and-ellipsis-a-direct-interpretation-approach/C92D413574503A1D36B8F849A1CA9C23)

- Qualified discourse-linking restrictions. Restrictions involving *which* shouldn’t be equated with a universal prohibition on contextually supplied alternatives. *What the hell do you want—pizza or pasta?* explicitly supplies a small answer set. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jos/article/41/1/53/7610788)

- Availability in some elliptical environments. *Why the hell?* and related fragments make a categorical ban on ellipsis untenable. Different elliptical configurations and discourse antecedents need separate treatment. [Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-linguistics/article/aggressively-nondlinked-construction-and-ellipsis-a-direct-interpretation-approach/C92D413574503A1D36B8F849A1CA9C23)

- No theological commitment. This occurrence doesn’t assert that hell exists or express a belief about damnation. Its religious lexical associations and its current question-modifying contribution are different properties.

- Cross-linguistic non-uniformity. Expressions serving comparable functions don’t necessarily share English restrictions: Ippolito discusses counterparts that combine with *which*-type interrogatives. Functional similarity therefore doesn’t guarantee identical host selection. [OUP Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jos/article/41/1/53/7610788)

## Further properties worth recording, but not assigning values from these examples

To extend the inventory into acquisition, psycholinguistics, neurolinguistics, and variation, I’d add the following as presently unvalued dimensions rather than attribute generic properties of *swearing* to all seven:

- Acquisition and generalization: age of comprehension versus production; acquisition of the particular construction versus its component word; ability to extend *you bastard* to new epithets or word-internal insertion to unfamiliar hosts.

- Processing and representation: recognition latency, predictability effects, memory, whole-form retrieval, decomposition, and the timing of evaluative-target resolution. For *the bastard*, anaphoric resolution is especially relevant; for *abso-bloody-lutely*, recovery of the interrupted host is.

- Physiological and clinical response: autonomic response, attentional capture, differential preservation under language impairment, and dependence on the language of acquisition. None follows merely from placing an expression in a taboo vocabulary list.

- Acoustic and multimodal realization: pitch, duration, intensity, voice quality, boundary placement, gaze, gesture, and laughter. These could distinguish hostile, affiliative, theatrical, or low-affect productions without changing the words.

- Social distribution and evaluation: rates across communities, relationships, activities, and media; perceived offensiveness; perceived authenticity or affectation; and differences between speakers’ intentions and recipients’ evaluations.

- Translation and cross-language equivalence: whether another language preserves the same grammatical construction, the same evaluative target, comparable offensiveness, or merely the same broad communicative action. Those are four potentially different kinds of equivalence.

The resulting profiles should distinguish having a structural property, licensing a use, typically receiving an interpretation, and producing a measurable effect. Those aren’t interchangeable senses of an expression *having a property*.
