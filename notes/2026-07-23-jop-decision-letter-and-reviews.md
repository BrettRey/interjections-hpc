Ref.: Ms. No. PRAGMA-D-26-00584
What English Interjections Let Us Predict: Stable Causal-Pragmatic Clustering and Path Dependence
Journal of Pragmatics

Dear Reynolds,

Thank you for submitting your manuscript to the Journal of Pragmatics. Your submission has now undergone peer review, alongside a thorough internal review by the editorial team. While one reviewer highlighted the potential interest of your topic and the importance of your work, the second reviewer raised significant concerns regarding the overall clarity and prose density of the paper recommending rejection.

In view of this conflict of opinion, your paper went through further editorial checks, during which we unfortunately identified critical issues regarding citation integrity. Specifically, three quotations attributed to the literature could not be located or verified within the cited works:

    "parenthetical strings that are not integrated in clause structure" (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 1350)
    Only one of the three examples attributed to Huddleston & Pullum on page 4 is located on page 1350, and another one features later on in the same chapter, but we were unable to find the third one.
    The interjection use has been "bleached of [its] original meaning", and it no longer refers to the entity the noun originally picked out (Gehweiler, 2008, p. 72).

As I trust you can appreciate, accurate source attribution is a fundamental requirement for publication in the Journal of Pragmatics and we unfortunately have a blanket policy to reject papers when such issues are identified (and also not accept to revised versions of such papers). Given this, we unfortunately cannot move forward with your manuscript.

We nonetheless hope that the attached reviewer comments will help you develop your ideas even further, and wish you the best of luck in finding a different venue for your work once you've fixed the citation misattributions.

We appreciate your submitting your manuscript to our journal and for giving us the opportunity to consider your work.

Kind regards,
Stavros Assimakopoulos
Co-Editor in Chief
Journal of Pragmatics

Comments:

Reviewer #1: This is an interesting and important paper. I have only a few small comments.
On p. 1 it says, "English interjections are small forms with large pragmatic consequences". However, not all of them are so small, e.g. goddammit (mentioned a couple pages later), which consists of three syllables, and hence is longer than many nouns and verbs of English.
On p. 3, I don't know whether "is deferred to" is the right word here. In the same paragraph, "goddammit preserves a verb-object-pronoun structure" -- I don't think this is a matter of morphology (the topic of this paragraph), but rather of a phrase becoming a single interjection -- maybe that is morphology, but the preservation of VO structure seems like a syntactic matter.
In the next paragraph, "Forms that don't inflect can't agree with a verb" -- normally we talk of a verb agreeing with a (subject) NP, not a verb being agreed with.
Also on p. 4, "supplements, "parenthetical strings that are not integrated n clause structure" (Huddleston & Pullum, 2002, p. 1350)" -- I am not sure what H&P mean, but relative clauses and appositional phrases are still part of the structure of a sentence, as we would see if we drew phrase structure diagrams of the sentences involved -- unlike vocatives, they still have some connection with a clause/sentence. I also think that some "supplements" (though not interjections) contribute to truth-conditional meaning, e.g. in (1c), if the professor involved is not Dr. Brown, the assertion is false.
P. 5: "Interjections greet (hey, hello), mark agreement (yes, amen)" -- as the author is aware, the boundaries of the class of interjection are difficult to define; some definitions of interjections would exclude greetings and/or words such as yes. Further, consider p. 24: "Routine formulae (bye, hello, sorry) share prosodic isolation and pragmatic function with interjections
but" -- here it seems that hello is not seens as an interjection, while on p. 5 it is.
P. 6: "No single property is necessary and sufficient [to define interjections]"-- See Libert (2012) "On Conversational Valence and the Definition of Interjections". Proceedings of the 42nd
Australian Linguistic Society Conference - 2011. Canberra: ANU Research Repository for a definition of interjections using a single property.
P. 15: "A change-of-state token such as oh" -- is token the right term here? (Likewise later in the same paragraph.) For me, token means a single occurrence of an item, this seems more like a type, since (I assume) you are not talking about a single instance of oh.
P. 17: Table 3 seems to be stuck in without any introduction or discussion -- I would add a sentence or two to introduce it (as I would do with tables and figures in general -- the previous figure is introduced in the text). Actually, now I have noticed that Table 2 is not introduced either (although Table 1 is).
P. 18: "The cluster is also continuously replenished via onomatopoeia" -- this seems strange to me -- I would have thought that onomatopoeias were a (fairly) closed class, since e.g. we don't keep discovering new animals which make new sounds. What is the source of new onomatopoeias? New ways of expressing sounds which had been expressed by other onomatopoeias before (maybe bam! in Batman was like this?). Also, not everyone would consider onomatopoeias to be interjections, as they are rather different in nature. On a related note, p. 23: "The ha data is noisy because the tagger captures laughter representations (HA! HA!) alongside genuine interjection uses" -- if onomatopoeias are interjections, then why are laughter representations not interjections?

A general stylistic note -- this paper has a lot of contractions, e.g. it's. I think in academic writing it would be better to use the uncontracted forms (it is, in this case).

Finally, about the question asked at the beginning of the paper: "what does identifying a form as an interjection let speakers, hearers, and analysts predict?" -- is it necessary to identify a form as an interjection to make (all) such predictions? I think in many (most?) cases some predictions can be made without identification as an interjection, i.e. the form itself leads to the prediction, not necessarily the identification of the form. Consider one of my favorite interjections, Bummer! (I am from the 1960s) -- people of a certain age will predict that this form (and its users) are from the 1960s or identify with that era, even if they do not realize that it is an interjection. The same is true of a word which is not (always) an interjection, groovy. It is sometimes an adjective, as in "We had a groovy time". This will also lead hearers to make some predictions, even though it is not (and is not identified as) an interjection. Admittedly, this may only be true of what we might call sociolinguistic predictions (e.g. about nationality of the speaker), not the pragmatic ones, but even with the latter, are hearers required to recognize an interjection as such in order to make these predictions?

Reviewer #2: [NB. this is an abridged version containing content-related comments for the author.]

Several compounds present in the article are so unfamiliar that I wasn't able to guess what they could mean. For instance, the author uses the compound "syntactic supplementarity", which is never used to describe interjections' syntactic behavior. Stylistic oddness oozes from every line of this text. But it is not the worst problem: the worst problem is the fact that this stylistic incongruity seems to hide, in my view, a referential void:
- what does the author refer to when he says : "This paper contributes to theoretical pragmatics" Which theory?
- On line 33, he mentions "pathways into or out of the profile". But what profile? Is he talking about the Langackerian profile?
- When he says: "Identifying a form as an interjection supports inferences about prosodic packaging, syntactic supplementarity, use-conditional stance, sequential position, response function, and interactional uptake.", I don't understand this sentence: why choosing the verb "to support"? why would it "support" inferences about all the elements mentioned? This assertion is not clear.
- Why does he use the term "Homeostatics" ? is it relevant here and why ?
- I can't make sense of the following paragraph, line 34-39. For me, this is absolutely meaningless:
"By path dependence, I mean that a form's route into the interjectional profile continues to shape its later boundary behaviour. Convergence on the profile doesn't erase source history: invocative, verbal, and onomatopoeic recruitment leave different residues, and regional/indexical conditioning adds another source of boundary structure. Those residues support diagnostic historical inference".
