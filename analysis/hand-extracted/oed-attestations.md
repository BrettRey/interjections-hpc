# OED attestations for *oof* and *bruh*

Transcribed from entries Brett pasted on 2026-07-26. Headwords verified by
resolving the permanent-link DOIs.

## oof, int.

`oed.com/dictionary/oof_int` · meaning & use <https://doi.org/10.1093/OED/3893496378> · etymology <https://doi.org/10.1093/OED/5084840633>

> Representing the sound made when expelling breath loudly through the mouth,
> and hence expressing various emotions conveyed by this action, such as alarm,
> annoyance, wonder, relief, resignation, etc. **Occasionally as n.**

Etymology: "An imitative or expressive formation. Imitative. Compare earlier
*phew* int."

First attestations (note the early spelling *ouf*):

| date | quotation | source |
|---|---|---|
| 1777 | "1000 thanks (I'm filling again! ouf!)." | T. Twining, Letter 24 January (1991) vol. I. 131 |
| 1788 | "Ouf, I'm all of a muck, let's take a little breath." | M. P. Andrews, *Belphegor* i. 6 |

**Bearing on the paper.** Two things, both used in §3. The 1777 attestation
refutes "a recent arrival in standalone use". And "an imitative or expressive
formation" places *oof* on the onomatopoeic recruitment route of §4.1 rather
than on either bleaching path, which is a route the paper independently posits.
The "occasionally as n." also supports the declaration's restriction to
standalone use rather than the whole word family.

## bruh, n. (colloquial)

**Sense 1, 1894–** <https://doi.org/10.1093/OED/3318156215>

> Chiefly in African American usage (originally U.S. regional (southern)). As a
> title preceding a man's name: 'Brother'.

"Earliest and often in the names of male animals in African American folk
tales; cf. *Brer* n."

| date | quotation | source |
|---|---|---|
| 1894 | "It is 'Bruh Rabbit,' if one gives the common sound to the letters." | *Century Magazine* October 875/2 |
| 1920 | "Pull down de windah shade an' shet de blin', bruh Bass." | A. M. Dunbar-Nelson, *Dunbar Speaker & Entertainer* ii. 47 |
| 1974 | "As bruh Larry Neal says: A cultural revolution [etc.]." | *Black World* April 12 |
| 2008 | "Bruh Fox thinks the rabbit is trapped." | R. Bjorklund, *Rabbits* i. 7 |

**Sense 2, 1967–** <https://doi.org/10.1093/OED/3506369048>

> Originally and chiefly North American. Used as a familiar form of address to a
> man. Cf. *brother* n. A.II.4c.

| date | quotation | source |
|---|---|---|
| 1967 | "We waited some more and a colored cat came down. 'Hey, bruh,' I called." | P. Thomas, *Down these Mean Streets* x. 104 |
| 1984 | "'Hey Bruh,' he begins his story…" | *Guardian* (Nexis) 15 November |
| 1998 | "'Be cool, bruh,' he says. 'Good to see you again.'" | *Esquire* February 37/3 |
| 2005 | "Listen bruh, I can't even begin to imagine what you're going through…" | M. M. Frisby, *Wifebeater* xxiii. 156 |

**Bearing on the paper, and the important point.** OED files *bruh* as a
**noun**, and both senses are nominal: a title before a name (1894) and a
familiar form of address (1967). **Neither is an interjection.** Every quotation
in sense 2 is vocative and addressed to someone: *Hey, bruh*; *Be cool, bruh*;
*Listen bruh*.

So the standalone interjectional use the paper appeals to, where *bruh* expresses
dismay or disbelief and is addressed to nobody, is **not recorded in this entry
at all**. That is a gain rather than a problem: the paper's claim is precisely
that the interjectional use is newly conventionalized, and OED's silence is
consistent with it while the noun senses show the word family it emerged from.
It also means the paper must not cite OED as authority for an interjectional
*bruh*; the citation supports the age of the family and the vocative source, not
the interjection.

The corpus figures fit the same shape. COHA has *bruh* absent until the 1980s
and only nine tokens overall. COCA rises from 0.01 per million in 1990–94 to
1.09 in 2015–19, roughly a hundredfold, and the tokens sit overwhelmingly in
TV/Movies (1.44 per million) with **zero in the spoken section**, which is worth
stating as a caveat: the record is scripted representation of speech rather than
recorded speech.
