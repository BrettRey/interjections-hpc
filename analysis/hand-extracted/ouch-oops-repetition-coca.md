## Reading (Claude Opus 5.5), after both pastes

KWIC lists one line per adjacent pair, so a run of three or more shows up more than once. Runs were grouped by script and then checked by hand: two pairs in one passage separated by other material count as two runs (New Yorker 2019; *Madeline's Madeline*; *I Married a Strange Person!*; *Magic Kid II*).

| | Runs with no punctuation between | Runs with one mark between | Texts | Tokens of the word | Runs per token |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *ouch* | 8 | 66 | 58 | 3,140 | about 1 in 42 |
| *oops* | 0 | 15 | 13 | 4,223 | about 1 in 280 |

**Both are repeated.** Adjacent repetition is attested for OUCH and OOPS alike, across films, TV, speech, fiction and magazines. *Oops* repeats about a sixth as often per token as *ouch*, and is never written run together (*oops oops*). The difference is a rate, not a yes and a no. Whether the unpunctuated zero means anything about *oops*'s prosody can't be told from text.

**Not every run is one speaker repeating.**
- Some are probably a second speaker echoing:
  - *oops* lines 1, 12, 14 (*Oops. Oops? Did you say "Oops"?*);
  - *ouch* lines 6, 21, 40 (*Ouch for me too, Dalts*; *Ouch. Ouch? That's it?*; *Ouch, indeed*).
- *Oops* line 13 is a song lyric.
- Eight *ouch* runs come from one KenyonRev text with a refrain (*Ouch, ouch. This malaise hurts*).

Leaving those out, *ouch* has about 63 runs and *oops* about 11.

**Bearing on Gutzmann 2013 p. 47.**
- His odd sequences put a full clause between the tokens (*Ouch! I've hit my thumb. Ouch! It was the hammer. Ouch!*). These queries find adjacent tokens only, so they don't test his pattern. A collocate search for *ouch* within a few words of *ouch*, excluding adjacent pairs, would.
- A few lines come close, with other interjections or a vocative between: *Ouch. Ouch. Madeline? Ouch! Ouch!*; *Oops! Oops! Whoa! Oops!*.
- His class sentence ("interjections and other isolated UCIs are not repeatable without sounding odd") is broader than what he tested, and these lines contradict it for adjacent repetition.

**What it supports in the matrix.**
- P10A Y for adjacent repetition of OUCH and of OOPS, Tier B presences, with the counts above.
- P10A N for distributed recurrence across clauses stays on Gutzmann's examples, marked as infelicity on constructed examples.
- P10S stays U: a line shows that repetition happened, not what it did. Some runs read as one *ouch* per blow (*The kicks and punches keep ouch ouch coming*; *Ouch! Ouch! Ouch!* under a falling ceiling), which is multiplication (P10M, outside v1).

**Leads on other properties, not yet in the matrix:**
- *Ouch for him. Ouch for me.* (WEB 2012) and *Ouch for me too* (TV 2015): *ouch* with a *for* phrase, which bears on P04 (dependents).
- *Each ouch lasted about three syllables* (KenyonRev 1994): *ouch* with a determiner, which bears on P06, though as a noun use.

**What follows.**
- Gutzmann's # pattern is attested for both forms: about five *ouch* passages and two or three *oops*.
- It is much rarer than recurrence across separate events, which is common for both.
- Read as a possibility claim, his judgement is contradicted. Read as a tendency, it holds: a speaker reacting to one mishap seldom says the interjection again after saying something else.
- Recurrence across separate events reads as one token per event, which is multiplication (P10M, outside v1), not strengthening.

**More leads on P04 (dependents):** *Ouch again*; *very ouch*; *Definitely ouch*; *Double ouch*; *Oops and double oops*; *ouch for him*. Each has *ouch* or *oops* taking a modifier or a *for* phrase.
